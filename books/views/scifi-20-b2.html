0 Chapter 1: The Empty Patch

Mara Venn adjusted the foam earcup and let the steady hiss of the receiver fill her head. Outside the cataloged windows of the North Array control room, dishes tilted across the scrubland like quiet white flowers seeking the sun. The sky was a flat, glimmering blue that meant nothing at the frequencies they worked; radio ears heard whole universes where eyes saw blankness. Mara liked the contrast—what looked empty could be full of math. Her hands moved with the practiced gentleness of someone who had learned to trust instruments the way other people trusted family. She had come here after long years of graduate school and colder nights in coastal arrays, carving an existence around signal-to-noise ratios, calibration sequences, and the patience that radio astronomy demanded. The work was steady, almost meditative—until it wasn't. Her life had its own quiet losses, a marriage that slid apart gradually like a dish misaligned over years, and a son, Jules, who drew circuits in crayon and liked to ask how far things were. Those questions sat in her chest while she listened to the sky. "Morning, Mara," Leo called from the console behind her. He had the easy, practical energy that made him fix broken amplifiers before coffee. "Pipeline looks healthy. Warmed the LNA. How's your patch?"

She smiled at him. "Quiet. Standard calibrator source nearby. Doing the three-point scan." The three-point was their way of checking beamshape: a quick, reliable calibration so they could separate true celestial features from the array's own quirks. Calibration was a ritual, but it was also an ethic. A misstep here could let your imagination become a headline. Inez sat with her fingers tapping a tablet, eyes flicking between spectrogram displays. "I'm cross-matching with previous nights. Nothing weird in the logs. Helen said keep an eye on the empty sectors while I finish the machine-learning rework."

Helen Ward, director and steady hand, appeared in the doorway like someone who belonged to the place even more than the instruments. She carried herself with the careful bravery that had steered the observatory through budget fights and technical disasters. "Keep the coffee nearby; it's going to be a long day," she said. "We've got the Sol family visiting the outreach dome today at ten. Try not to scare them."

"The Sol family are our human element," Leo joked, but all of them knew Helen's joke had an edge. Public trust was as fragile as a dish's alignment. Mara swept the beam across an empty patch in the catalog, a sector the survey maps had long labeled as "low emission." The sky there had no bright radio galaxies, no known pulsars, no molecular clouds rich enough to glow at the frequency they observed. Empty—and yet empty in the way a math problem can be "solved": a clean blanker of data. At 09:17 a narrow spike nudged the spectrometer. The line was thin, far narrower than the thermal noise of the surrounding band. Mara saved the initial files and set a high-priority alert. The spike repeated, three seconds, three seconds, as if some distant voice were knocking in a rhythm they could almost learn to count. The empty patch in the catalog was not empty now. It had a rule and a rhythm, and beyond that, something else—an origin they could measure but not yet fully understand. "All right," Mara said, feeling the day tilt. "We find out what it's not, then what it might be. We do it right."

Outside, the dishes kept listening, patient as bones. Inside, Mara felt the science tighten into focus like a lens. They were at the beginning of a long inquiry, and the first decision had been made: test, calibrate, wait—and prepare to stand by the truth, whatever it might be.

1 Chapter 2: Noise or Message

They moved through the control room like a single organism. Leo checked the front-end electronics—preamps, attenuators, the cryogenic temperatures on the receivers—while Inez ran a quick scan for known satellites and transients. Rook watched the door and the power lines with a restless intensity; his palm rested on the panel that could shut the array down in an instant. Helen called up historical logs, cross-referencing garbage collection schedules, solar weather data, and flights that could have passed overhead. Mara sat between them, fingers on the console, listening to data roll in as if it were a heartbeat. "Calibration first," she said. "No heroics. If there's a bias in the instrumentation, we need to see it." She spoke like she was threading a needle. "Run the noise diode, inject a known signal into the feed, sweep the band."

Leo grunted and spun the dial. The calibration pulse hopped through the frequencies—flat where it should be, a tiny dip at a manufacturing seam they knew about from last year's firmware. The noise floor remained steady. Inez overlaid past measurements and pointed out a millidecibel wobble near the band edge. "Thermal drift from the compressor," she said. "We've seen that. Not enough to shape a coherent spike."

"Run interference checks," Helen added. "Local RFI, power harmonics. Tell me we can exclude things like trucks hauling generators past the east fence."

Rook tapped the screen, sending a drone of spectrum analyzers on a sweep around the facility. The result came back almost politely clean. There were signals—cell towers beyond the perimeter, the faint Morse blips of a weather buoy—none matched the timing or narrowband character of the repeating spikes. On the map, satellites in low orbit scrolled by like constellations on a planetarium. Inez overlaid their paths against the source coordinates. The spike's position didn't move with any satellite track. "Not terrestrial," Leo said, voice low. "Not anything in our database."

Mara let that thought sit only a second. "Not yet," she corrected. "Don't turn a preliminary negative into an alien provenance. We still have to test pointing and sidelobe effects."

They swung the array, a chorus of servos and feedback, away by ten degrees and then twenty. The dishes shifted with a weighty confidence, their shadows of metal moving across the scrub outside. When the array changed direction, the spikes vanished. The room felt colder in that absence. "It disappeared," Inez said, fingers flying on pattern-recognition code. "As soon as we off-pointed."

"Point back," Mara ordered. The dishes obeyed; their faces found the coordinates again. The spikes returned, as punctual as a metronome, three seconds apart with that same harmonic structure, phase-locked across every element. Relief and a new kind of tension moved through the team. "It obeys our beam," Helen said. "That suggests a localized sky position. Could be a sidelobe from something much brighter out of frame, but then it would smear. This is narrow. The discovery had handed them a problem with moral weight: to preserve truth and prevent rumor. It was a responsibility that fell on Mara as leader and on each of them as scientists. As the last backups copied, Inez looked up with a face that had the softness of someone returning from the edge of a puzzle. "If it's a name," she said, almost whispering, "we might be the audience."

Mara's reply was neither an answer nor a denial. She felt the possibility of wonder, of danger, and the finite, heavy knowledge that whatever the signal meant, it would not speak in comfortable full sentences. It would offer rules that they could test—and secrets it would not give. "Then we listen," she said. "We listen, and we do the work."

2 Chapter 3: Three Impossible Details

They locked the door. Rook took the keypad and sat like an anchor at the threshold, fingers steepled, eyes flicking across the room and occasionally to the dish through the glass. Outside, the East Array acknowledged the ping and replied with a string of metadata: UTC times, antenna boresight coordinates, polarization vectors, system temperatures. Leo matched numbers on his pad. "Phase noise within expected bounds," he said. "Pointing within three arcseconds of our solution. No suspicious RFI in their log."

"Good," Helen said. Her voice was firm, but she touched the receiver box as if to reassure herself the hardware was still real. "Independent confirmation is the first wall against our own mistakes."

The team had thought about false positives before—the gift of a scientist is pessimism—but independent observatories were the second wall. Half an hour later, the South Ridge observatory sent a cross-check. Their spectrum showed the same discrete structure, the same repeating envelope, altered only by local noise differences and a slight delay consistent with light-travel time across the Earth and the differing pointing geometry. The signal had not been manufactured in one room. It was not a glitch of their instruments. It was in the sky. Mara closed her eyes and held the feeling with both palms. "Six months away," she said. Someone had rescaled distance into time when they first fed the dispersion data into the propagation model. The source—its apparent geometric positioning, its modulation—pointed to a system on a path that would put a particular alignment six months from now. It might be a conjunction, an occultation, or something like gravitational lensing coming into focus. Whoever was sending this—whatever was producing the pattern—seemed to mark that date. "Possible name," Inez said, and the word landed like a pebble into a pond. She pinned a new window and scrolled her annotated transcription. The three-part signal had a short cluster of binary-like pulses, a long structured pattern of intervals, and then a tonal sweep. When the first cluster repeated, Inez had mapped it into letters using multiple schemes. One mapping yielded NIA SOL. "Could be a coincidence," Leo said. He rubbed the bridge of his nose. "Extraction bias. We map random bits to things our minds like. Nia Sol could be a pattern of phase-shifts that just happens to coincide with that transliteration."

"It doesn't appear in any current catalog," Helen replied. "We ran searches against census databases, social media archives, genealogies. Nothing that matches exactly. Could be an old name, or a constructed label."

Rook's jaw tightened. "If it's a name, it's not one of ours."

Inez stood, moving to the whiteboard as if the room could not hold the noise in her head. She wrote primes and constants, scribbling gaps between primes, Fibonacci indices, the first hundred digits of pi. "The second part—the long pattern—contains repeated substructures at multiple scales," she said. "At first glance, it's chaotic. The room fell into rhythm: the soft clack of keys, a muffled laugh from Leo, Rook's slow breathing. It was only then that the pad on Mara's desk chimed with a single new entry from an automated monitoring station: a timestamped alert that a nearby pulsar—PSR J2144+0654—had shown a microglitch synchronized to the exact interval predicted by Inez's clock marker. Silence swallowed the room until Mara exhaled. "That's the synchronization," she said. "We just measured their clock against a pulsar. It's not evidence of intent, but it is evidence of coordination. We have a rule, a rendezvous, and a date."

She looked at the faces around her and felt the gravity of what they had discovered—three impossible details that were now commands. "We follow the instructions," she said. "We keep the channels closed. We prepare for what happens in six months."

3 Chapter 4: The Natural Explanation

They worked through the night, the observatory becoming a small island of light amid the sleeping desert. Leo leaned over one of the cryo-cooled front-ends, his forehead close to the casing as if the metal might whisper secrets. "Pulsars do odd things," he said, not looking up. "Microglitches happen. Plasma lenses in the ionized medium can focus and defocus radio beams. Occasionally you get frequency-dependent arrival times that look structured if you only listen at one band."

Inez’s fingers flew across her tablet. "But this pattern isn't dispersive in the usual way," she said. "Dispersion shifts low frequencies more than high. The burst we've extracted sweeps in time like a chirp, but its arrival time differences between bands are linear, not the ν^-2 law for cold plasma."

Mara watched the spectrograms fill the large screen: clean bands, false-color streaks, and a slender repeating motif that refused to broaden with frequency. She could see the math in her head—time-delay equations, plasma column densities, the expected scattering tails—and the way those models bent around their data. "If it's a plasma event," she said slowly, "it would need a very controlled refractive index gradient. Natural? Highly unlikely. Not impossible."

Leo straightened and fixed her with a practical stare. "Or it's a pulsar," he insisted. "There are pulsars with complex magnetospheres. Modes switch. You can get periodic subpulses that line up when you fold on the rotation period. If the object is young or has a close companion, magnetospheric interactions can produce near-coherent emission. We shouldn't jump to exotic explanations when known astrophysics can produce odd periodicities."

"Agreed," Mara said. "But the thing about the pulsar hypothesis—if it is a pulsar—we should see a characteristic drift or phase behavior to the pulses. And pulsar emission usually has a polarization signature tied to the rotation. The pattern we're seeing has no normal drift, and its polarization is... constrained in a way I don't expect."

They tested that immediately. Mara ordered observations at two more frequency bands, one below, one above their standard receiving band. The dishes slewed with an audible groan. Rook stood at the window, arms folded, watching the panorama of radomes like a defensive line. He had the posture of someone who measured safety in angles and closures. "Don't forget to calibrate," Helen reminded them, pen poised over her notebook. She had arrived from an optical position and wasn't afraid to be hands-on. "Use the noise diode. Run the gain curve for each feed. If the receiver's response is non-linear at those frequencies, we could create spurious structure."

Leo nodded with a curt smile. "Already on it. Injecting the calibration tone now."

They recorded calibration sweeps, matched them against known celestial calibrators, and then cross-correlated the data across bands. The watch on Mara’s wrist ticked toward dawn. The spectrograms began to show a property that set teeth on edge: the pattern remained phase-stable across 1.2 to 2.8 GHz. Inez stared at the tiny spike in the autocorrelation plot and swallowed. "There's structure inside the structure," she said. "A nested periodicity. It's not human. It's not a pulsar in any catalog. It's not pure noise."

Mara felt the old scientific caution war with a new, harder wonder. "Then we map it," she said. "We test the map. We do not claim a sender, but we do follow the rules the signal gives us. And we protect those who are human here."

They set the instruments to trace the nested periodicity, letting the dishes listen as if holding a breath. Outside, the sky cooled. Inside, the team prepared for six months of measurement, of hypotheses sharpened against reality. The discovery—behaving like a mirror and like a door—hung before them. They would open it slowly, and only after they had learned how to close it again.

4 Chapter 5: The Name in the Future

The lab hummed with air and cooled metal. Screens glowed with rolling spectrograms; orange and blue streaks folded into one another as frequencies slid past the filters. Outside, the array's dishes turned almost imperceptibly, hunting a ghost that refused to be pinned. Mara sat with her hands folded, feeling the map of the data more than seeing it. Her fingers tapped the edge of her notepad, a slow metronome against the drift of possibilities. "Run a cross-correlation across the baseline," Inez said without looking up. "Sequence the phase for each receiver. If that's a gravitational lens, the arrival times should scale with the lensing geometry. If it's local interference, we'll see coherence collapse across long baselines."

Leo rubbed his jaw. "We've subtracted known RFI—aircraft, satellites, the comms tower on Ridge Five. The SNR is low, but the autocorr spike persists when we integrate. Whatever it is, it's coherent enough to survive a hundred-second fold."

Mara listened to the technical rhythm like a second language. Signal-to-noise ratio: the balance between the whisper and the world's static. Calibration: the slow, meticulous act of making instruments speak the truth of the sky. She imagined the array as a set of ears learning to trust one another. "Map the light-travel delays," she said. "Put the timestamps on GPS time and fold over the six dishes. If there's lensing, the same emission will arrive with microsecond offsets depending on path length. That will be evidence, not inference."

Helen moved to the control bank. Her voice was soft enough that it could be mistaken for intrigue. "And evidence is what we need. Not narratives. Not wishes. We do the math first."

They did the math. Numbers spilled out on the wall screen, translated into curves and then into a rough geometric model. There was a weak, repeatable structure across frequencies that bent slightly with frequency—dispersion consistent with propagation through a plasma, but overlaid with a delay pattern that looked suspiciously like multiple images stitched by time. Rook, who had been silent, leaned into the screen. He was protective in posture, vigilant in his silence. "Gravitational lensing normally rearranges paths without changing frequency content," he said. "Here we have dispersive delay that grows at lower frequencies. That implies the medium itself is doing shaping—plasma gradients in a curved space. Or something placed along the line of sight."

Inez chewed the inside of her cheek and scribbled a new series of transforms. "If it's a plasma lens, it would act like a frequency-dependent lens, focusing certain bands and smearing others. That could create repeating substructures—'structure inside structure' as I said. But the periodicity? That's not usually from a plasma."

Mara remembered Elias's instruction to keep the circle tight. The Sol family occupied a modest house two streets over, with a garden of tomato vines and a crib company catalog still half-open on the kitchen table. Her investigation took a different dress and a different ID: a medical database access code for policy review. "We prepare for the date," he said. "We stake the perimeter. We measure every parameter so that on that day, we know if the sky sends a confirmation or if it's only a coincidence stretched to feel like prophecy."

Mara felt the weight of causality and compassion balanced on her knuckles. "And we tell ourselves the truth," she said. "Evidence first. No leaps. But we'll be ready if the sky chooses to speak on a calendar day it has no right to know."

They all turned back to the screens. The nested spike pulsed, patient and inscrutable, like a creature sleeping in the geometry of space. For now, the choice was theirs: wait, measure, and protect. In the map of future time, a name had appeared. Whether it belonged to a person or to a pattern, they would learn by watching the clock and listening to the light.

5 Chapter 6: A Warning with No Verb

The nested spike held its breath in the spectrogram as if waiting for permission to speak. Mara moved her fingers over the keyboard without touching it, thinking about how radio telescopes listen to the past because light takes time. The dish didn't eavesdrop on now; it sampled a thousand different nows, each one stretched by distance and travel time. That meant the calendar invite could be mapping to an alignment that happened centuries ago or to a transit that would happen next month. Evidence, she reminded herself, not story. Inez tapped a line of code. "We can fold the signal with different reference frames, account for barycentric corrections, light-travel delay, even proper motion of possible sources." She pointed at the windowed display. "But first: we finish decoding these symbols. If the message uses prime spacing and repetition, it's synthetic. If it's too messy, it's natural noise."

They fed the last batch of symbols through the pattern engine. Leo stood beside Rook, arms crossed, watching the waveform like a dog watching a rabbit hole. "Keep it tight," he said. "No wild hypotheses. If it's instructions, follow slow. If it's a warning, take the hint."

Helen, having unrolled a printout with the decoded fragments, swallowed. The fragments were short, repeating sequences with punctuation marks that might be pauses. The math suggested a base-12 or base-60 counting system; the grammar markers, though, were the oddest. There were breaks—intervals long enough to be deliberate silence. "Think of Morse with sleep," she said. "The intervals are as meaningful as the pulses."

Rook's voice was quiet and directive. "Translate the pauses into temporal markers. See if they align with occultation windows, the empty region maps Inez produced, and gravitational lensing models. If the message warns against opening an interval, we need to know what that interval is."

They worked in loops—model, observe, debate. The menagerie of screens showed different reductions: raw voltage, power spectral density, dynamic waterfall, and a new plot that Inez had thrown together—symbol occurrence versus light-travel corrected time. The symbols clustered like constellations of punctuation. When Mara overlaid the cluster on the empty region map, a chill ran along the base of her skull: the symbol clusters pointed toward specific chords of the void, not particular coordinates. "Do not open the dark interval," Inez read aloud, her voice modulated by fatigue and awe. She had forced the software to spit out the most likely linguistic mapping for the symbols. It wasn't perfect English—no language might be—but the syntactic markers suggested negation, a verb, and then an object phrase that fit the translation. "The 'do not' marker repeats with different object markers, always paired with a long pause afterward. Either it's emphasis or it's a grammatical rule we don't know."

Mara's hands trembled slightly. "If it's a warning, it's a safety message. But to whom?" Her eyes flicked to the Sol family's room where sunlight now struck the white crib, unassembled. "Us? The region itself? Mara looked at the moving centroid on the monitor—an illusion of motion, a physics puzzle, or a messenger on an arc she couldn't see. "We will not open the dark interval," she said aloud, not quite a command and not quite a vow. "If it is a warning, we will heed it. If it is a test, we will be careful. If it is both, then we will survive long enough to understand the grammar of a sky that speaks by delays."

They set the watch, doubled the baselines, and informed their partners. The observation windows multiplied across time zones like cautious footprints. Outside, the dishes tilted and listened. Inside, a new translation pipeline hummed. Somewhere in the dark interval, something that could not be seen continued to move, patient and deliberate, and the team committed themselves to the slow, precise labor of proof over panic.

6 Chapter 7: The Broadcast Decision

Director Ward arrived like a weather front—sharp, efficient, the kind of presence that made people tidy their anxieties into neat folders. She waited for the briefing to finish, then took the chair at the head of the table as if it had been waiting for her signature. Her eyes traveled the room and settled on the live plots. "You have my attention," she said. "Show me evidence."

Mara went straight into the language she loved: numbers with meaning. "The signal has consistent intervals in the radio band between 1.4 and 1.6 gigahertz. We see periodic drops in the SNR—signal-to-noise ratio—every 8.42 hours, lasting roughly 47 seconds. The pattern holds across three dishes with independent clocks. We've calibrated for local interference, ionospheric delay, and even a thermal drift in Dish Two. Cross-correlation shows the same sequence. It's not a pulsar; the frequency structure is inconsistent with known emissions."

Helen pushed her glasses up and added a cautionary layer. "Calibration matters here. We've run noise diodes, verified time-stamps against GPS and a local hydrogen maser, and dithering tests show no systematic artifact. But gravitational lensing could spread or delay components of a signal. We can't assume a single path. Light-travel time must be factored for each possible route."

Rook's voice was low and firm. "All potential transmitters within that line of sight checked out: no craft, no debris, no known human beacon. We sweep for active probes using radar and lidar. Anything that moves fast enough to create that delay pattern would be visible in optical follow-ups."

"Which is why we must be honest with the public," Mara said, looking straight at Ward. "Hiding data leaves rumor space. People fill unknowns with fear. If we publish confirmed observations—even partial, even tentative—we help the Sol family and the public make reasoned choices."

Director Ward's jaw tightened in a way that suggested maps of consequence were unrolling behind her eyes. "Confirmed data," she repeated. "I will not allow an unvetted claim to become wildfire. We publish only what passes our highest threshold. Otherwise, we sow panic and undermine the institution."

Rook's fingers tightened on the table. "And silence can be a form of panic too. If this gets out through other channels and we were seen as concealing—"

"Leak control is standard," Ward cut in. "We hold until peer review. People will understand that caution equals responsibility."

Leo, who had mostly kept his hands busy with a tablet, shrugged. "Peer review takes weeks. Viral videos take hours. If someone outside wants to race us to the narrative, they're going to put out whatever they want. We need to plan for the leak scenario, not pretend it's impossible."

They heard the near-simultaneous chime of an incoming message. Helen's face paled. "We have a leak," she said. "Social feed indicates an anonymous post—date and a name. The Sols' calendar has already been screen-capped." She tapped a fingertip; a stream of comments scrolled like rising water. It fit the rules they had observed and didn't violate light-travel limits. It was a deliberate message, or a cosmic coincidence shaped like meaning. Director Ward leaned forward. "We will notify the world," she said. "And we will ask one thing of everyone: do not act on speculation. Let the evidence lead."

Mara let the words settle and then keyed the release. The observatory's broadcast went out, calibrated and annotated, a careful lantern thrown into the dark. The storm that followed would be human, messy, and unavoidable. But at least, for now, the rules of the signal held true: time-delays, no FTL shortcuts, and a pattern that demanded not just explanation, but patient understanding.

8 Chapter 9: The Light-Travel Problem

Mara left the quiet of the briefing room with her hands vibrating from adrenaline and coffee. Outside, the observatory hummed in its familiar mechanical rhythm: coolant pumps, antenna servos, the low, human chatter that kept complex instruments from feeling like occult objects. She walked past a bank of dish monitors where Leo adjusted gain settings. He looked up, wiped grease from his palms, and gestured her over like he had a small, private joke. "You're still thinking in light-years," he said without ceremony. "Inez pulled a fresh run on the archival data while you were talking. Twelve-Six's burst has echoes—faint, but measurable—embedded in older scans. The pattern repeats, but each time it's delayed by a consistent interval. Whoever or whatever sent it knew how long their signal would take to reach us."

Mara sat and let that settle. "Light-travel time," she murmured. It was a basic idea, taught in first-year astrophysics: you cannot outrun the speed of light. Any message arriving now left its origin long ago, measured in years or centuries. The simple truth felt like a weight: there was no real-time conversation possible across those distances. Helen joined them, stepping around a rack of thermal blankets. She kept her voice low. "If the origin is many light-years away, then the 'warning'—if that word applies—left before our current civilization began. It could have been sent by an earlier society, or produced by a transient astrophysical process whose signal happens to carry structure."

Inez stared at a plotted waveform, her fingers tapping a rhythm on the screen as if she were deciphering music. "The modulation isn't random. Look here." She enlarged one burst. The peaks fall into ratios that repeat: 2:3:5, then a pause, then 7:11:13. "It's prime-related, but not strictly primes. It's a mathematical motif layered on a chirped carrier. That suggests calibration to an instrument—something measuring time or mass—and perhaps encoding positional coordinates in gravitational lens geometry."

Rook listened, arms folded, guarding every expression. He liked certainty; he liked lines drawn clearly between friend and threat. Yet even he could see the edges of this problem being blurred by time. "So someone mapped a mass and used gravity as punctuation," he said. "No faster-than-light tricks. No instantaneous handshake."

"Exactly," Mara said. She pulled up her notebook and did what she did best: turned speculation into numbers. "If the compact mass sits at a distance D and the lensing event has a duration τ as observed, then the original signal's emission time t_emit = t_observed - D/c, where c is light speed. Plug in the best-fit distance from parallax and spectral dispersion, and the emission date predates our reception by decades to centuries."

Leo made a face. "You mean the 'warning' could be older than the message it supposedly warns about?"

"It must be," Mara replied. The light-travel problem was not a mechanism to frustrate them; it was an honest ledger of cause and effect. Her comm buzzed with a secure note from the international partners: confirmation of additional time on southern dishes, priority allocated. The plan was in motion. At midnight, with the team exhausted and the instruments humming in a dozen languages, the first secondary peak arrived—small, precise, and consistent with the recorded-broadcast model. Her future name had already been written into the provisional papers: Nia Sol, the neuralist whose work could make sense of signals no one initially encoded in human language. Mara had felt an oddly maternal pride at seeing it printed, then a chill. Recognition did not equal destiny. When the return blinked across the monitor, it was not a voice or an image but a vector of data. A packet of radio-frequency power values, neatly timestamped and wrapped in a calibration header. It suggested respect for instrument specifics, perhaps an understanding of our interference. Or it was a mimicry the machine had learned from listening. "They used our referents," Inez said, pointing. "They can tell the difference between noise and a calibration tone."

Mara watched each face. The numbers were comforting; they were math. But evidence was not reassurance. She thought of the structure's header that had matched their timestamps. A decision, not a promise. If an intelligence operating through gravitationally-corrected time frames had chosen to impose a rule, what did compliance mean for them? And what would a breach mean? Helen swallowed. "We have to be precise. If the return is deliberately calibrated to our system, then any deviation we make—adding power, shifting frequency—changes the variables. It may change their response. Or their behavior."

Rook's jaw tightened. "Then we keep the power low. Keep the band narrow. Lock out any other experimenters in the field. A controlled connection means a choke point. We protect the array and whatever lies behind it."

Mara's hand hovered over the console switch. Her training was chords of caution and probability: Bayes' theorem had saved a half-dozen careers by turning hopeful patterns into testable hypotheses. But science was not only calculation; it was also a social act. They would be defining themselves as a species by how they handled this contact. The choice was not simply technical; it was profoundly ethical. They had not been handed destiny. They had been handed a protocol and a mirror. The choice of the witness had been made: not to stop out of fear, nor to rush into contact, but to proceed as stewards of knowledge and guardians of their species. The array hummed its measured song into the void, and for the first time since the header had accepted their timestamps, Mara allowed herself to believe the experiment might teach them something about the structure—and something about themselves. Far beyond their instruments, through a gravitational lens and across years of light, a response began to form—a series of calibrated pulses that obeyed the same constraints they had set. It was not a greeting. It was an invitation to continue under the rules the witness had given.

Helen, always stationed on the edge between the machine and the human, rubbed her temples. "We need to keep the psychological load down," she said quietly.
