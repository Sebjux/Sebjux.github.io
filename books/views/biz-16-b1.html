0 A New Desk and Old Numbers

The bell over the shop door rang as Maya Singh walked into Northfield Furniture. It was a cool morning in a Manchester suburb. The smell of varnish and fresh wood was familiar. Peter Cole, the owner, stood by a tall show table. He had a quiet face with lines from many years of making things and worrying about money.

"You must be Maya," Peter said, offering a hand. "Welcome to Northfield. We have made tables and cabinets here for twenty-eight years."

"Thank you," Maya answered. She carried a small laptop and a folder. She was thirty, sharp, and used numbers like others used tools. "I'm glad to be here. Rachel said you'd like me to look at the accounts."

Rachel, Peter's daughter and the daily manager, appeared from the workshop. She moved fast. "We do things by hand and by eye here," she said. "But I do think the numbers need a clearer view. Sales are good, but our bank is small. People pay late. Timber costs more. It feels heavy."

Maya smiled but felt a small knot in her stomach. "Show me the books," she said. "We will look at revenue, expenses, stock and the cash flow. Then we can see the problem."

Lucy, the sales employee, set a cup of tea on the desk. "Clients love our work," she said. "Our monthly sales are around £72,000 most months. People come for solid oak. We have a good reputation. But sometimes profit is thin."

Maya opened the folder. Invoices were neat but many were old. "Who pays when?" she asked. "Our supplier, Alan from the sawmill, gives us 30 days, but our customers often pay in 60 to 75 days. That causes a gap. Also, unfinished stock ties up a lot of cash. I see it here: stock and unfinished orders total £95,000."

Peter's face tightened. "We have customers who are loyal. But bills arrive faster than money comes in. Our fixed monthly costs are about £38,000. That covers wages, rent, insurance and the workshop lights. We sell £72,000 a month, but sometimes the gross margin on certain orders drops to just 22 percent. That leaves little to pay our fixed costs."

"So revenue is there, but profit is small and cash is scarce," Maya said. "Where is the bank balance now?"

"Small," Rachel admitted. "About £6,200 right now. We have supplier invoices and a loan, and customer payments pending. It is stressful."

Tom, the foreman, came over with a sample drawer. "If we speed up an order, we can finish stock and get paid, but that takes more hours. That costs money too," he said. He rubbed his hands, worried about the team.

Maya listened. She liked the shop, the people and the smell of sawdust. She also saw the ledger like a puzzle. Revenue, expenses, assets, liabilities, cash flow—all these words had to match a real life. She felt ready to try.

They walked through the workshop. The shelves were full of parts and half-made tables. "That stock is an asset," Maya said. "But it is also cash tied up. If clients do not pay, our bank feels it. We need to free some of that money."

Peter looked at her with tired hope. "We have kept the business honest for a long time. I hate the idea of changing too much. But if you can help, we will do what is needed." Maya promised to start with the bank statements and invoices that afternoon. She felt excitement and pressure. This job would test her skills and her care for a family business.

1 Files, Figures and Tough Choices

Maya sat at the small office desk behind the shop. Sunlight fell on a stack of invoices. She opened the online bank and a spreadsheet. Numbers filled the screen. She liked order and clarity, but these numbers were messy. Trade receivables — money customers owed — were high. She counted £68,000 in customer payments outstanding, many overdue.

Lucy brought a list of orders. "This month we have £72,000 in sales," she said. "But some orders have low margins. A big oak dining table sale was priced to move. The gross margin was just 22 percent because of rising timber costs and a rush delivery."

Maya marked the line. "Revenue is good, but gross profit is not always enough. After the cost of materials and labour (cost of goods sold), we need to cover our fixed costs of £38,000 a month. If gross margin is low, net profit disappears."

"And cash leaves before money returns," Rachel replied. "We pay Alan and his sawmill in 30 days. But the customer for that dining table paid us in 75 days. We are short for a month. It is a timing problem as much as cost."

Maya made a simple list. Assets: stock and unfinished work £95,000, the bank £6,200, tools and workshop equipment. Liabilities: supplier invoices £48,000 and a small loan. She wrote ‘working capital’ at the top: money tied up in stock and unpaid customer invoices.

"If we sell £72,000 a month but have to spend money first, we need working capital to bridge the gap," Maya said. "Right now we are short. We need to reduce the stock level tied up, speed customer payments, or stretch supplier terms."

Rachel looked away. "I am afraid if we change terms with customers, they may go elsewhere."

Maya understood. "We cannot force anything. But we can be practical. A small deposit before work begins is fair. For a large order of £4,500, a 20 percent deposit is £900. That helps cash flow and shows the client is committed."

Peter listened quietly. He had built the firm by trusting people. He feared asking for deposits might harm relationships. But he also feared unpaid bills.

Tom walked in. "We could finish orders that have a fast sale," he suggested. "If we deliver sooner, we may collect sooner. And we could stop making new stock until cash frees up."

"That will reduce the asset of stock from £95,000 over time," Maya said. "Even a £25,000 reduction in stock would help cash flow immediately. But it will be hard work."

Maya prepared a short plan. Ask customers for 20 percent deposits, negotiate longer supplier terms where possible, and stop non-essential production. She also wanted to check the gross margins line by line and price some items differently. Small changes could improve cash flow without closing the shop.

Rachel frowned but nodded. "We will need to be honest with clients and staff," she said. "No one likes a sudden change, but I see the numbers."

At the end of the day, Maya felt the data in her mind. The business had healthy monthly revenue, but the timing of payments, low margins on some orders and high stock meant liabilities and cash flow were the real problem. She felt both anxious and patient. Real help would take time and steady talks.

2 Negotiations and Small Wins

Maya started the week by calling Alan at the sawmill. He had been a supplier for many years. His voice was warm but blunt. "Peter, I know you well. But I have costs too. I give 30 days because that is fair for us both."

Maya listened and explained. "Alan, we value your wood. We ask if you can stretch the terms to 45 days for some shipments if we keep orders steady. In return, we can promise regular monthly orders and pay on time for those batches."

There was a pause, then Alan said, "Forty-five days? I cannot always, but for regular work I can. I like that you want to commit. I will try."

Maya felt a small victory. Extending supplier terms from 30 to 45 days would ease immediate cash pressure. It would reduce the speed at which liabilities needed paying, giving the workshop time to collect customer payments.

Rachel took the lead with customers. She called Mr. Patel, a loyal café owner who ordered tables for his three shops. Rachel explained, "We want to ask for a 20 percent deposit on larger orders. It helps us plan and keeps your price stable."

Mr. Patel hesitated and then said, "I have used you for years. I can send a deposit on this next batch. I want my tables, and I like that we are clear."

Lucy drew up new invoices. Now each large order showed a 20 percent deposit line. For average monthly sales of £72,000, if most customers paid a 20 percent deposit, Northfield would immediately have about £14,400 in cash. That number felt important.

"That £14,400 would change our bank balance," Maya said aloud. "It can cover parts of fixed costs or supplier invoices. It does not solve everything, but it helps cash flow."

Tom organised the team to finish two near-complete orders first. "If we deliver and invoice, we might shorten payment days," he said. "Clients want their goods. They will pay when they see the work done."

Not all talks were easy. A regular client bristled at paying a deposit and chose another maker for a small order. Rachel felt annoyed. "We lost a sale," she said. "But we cannot chase every penny and stay open."

Peter watched and was quiet. He had learned to accept some loss as part of staying healthy. "A small lost sale is okay if it keeps the business alive," he said. "Reputation matters, but so does being here next year."

By the end of the week, a small change had happened. The bank balance rose from £6,200 to about £20,600 after receiving deposits and delivering finished goods. Stock value had started to fall slightly as they completed orders. Maya recorded this on her spreadsheet as improved cash flow and lower short-term liabilities. The team felt relief.

Maya knew the work was not done. There were still supplier invoices of £48,000 and trade receivables of £68,000. But she also saw that careful negotiation and clear communication could make a real difference. The family began to trust a different kind of talking—one that mixed kindness and numbers.

3 Pressure and Practical Compromise

A month passed with small improvements. Maya updated the ledger. Sales had stayed near £72,000. Deposits from customers had brought in £14,400 in a month. The bank balance looked healthier at £20,600. Stock had reduced from £95,000 to £70,000 as Northfield finished and delivered orders.

But not everything smoothed. An important supplier invoice for £30,000 arrived with a tight deadline. The invoice was for recent timber deliveries. Alan called to ask when Northfield would pay the full amount.

Peter sat at his desk, hands pressed together. "We have to pay the invoice, or Alan will stop deliveries," he said. "We cannot lose him. But paying in full will empty the bank."

Maya checked the numbers and then suggested a practical compromise. "We can offer a partial payment now and agree a short schedule for the rest. Also, we can delay the purchase of a new sanding machine we planned. That will keep some cash. If we keep the 20 percent deposits and the new supplier terms, we will catch up."

Rachel looked weary. "We had hoped to buy the machine this quarter. It would save hours and reduce waste. But I know the cash is king. If we pay Alan partly, maybe he will accept the plan."

Tom joined the discussion. "I can rearrange shifts to finish certain orders quicker. If we finish three larger jobs, their balances should come in soon. It will not be easy, but the team will work."

They called Alan. He was firm but fair. "I cannot take too many late payments," he said. "But you have been a good customer. I will accept £15,000 now and the rest in two payments over the next six weeks if you keep orders to me."

"Thank you," Peter said, relief clear on his voice. "We will do that."

This agreement cut the immediate liability from £30,000 to £15,000 due now. It gave the business breathing room. Maya adjusted the cash flow forecast and saw the bank balance dip but then climb as customer deposits and payments arrived.

There were emotional costs. Rachel had to email three clients and explain the new deposit policy. One regular client complained and moved to a competitor. The workshop postponed the machine purchase. Some staff bonuses were delayed. Everyone felt the pinch.

Yet there were also human gains. Tom's team pulled longer days to finish orders. Lucy worked hard to keep customers happy while asking for deposits. Customers who cared for Northfield were willing to help by paying on time. The family made practical compromises to keep the business stable.

Maya looked at the month-end figures. Fixed costs stayed at £38,000. The gross margin on re-priced orders had improved from 22 percent to about 28 percent for new work. This increased profit margin slightly. After paying the £15,000 to Alan, the bank balance was predicted to be about £15,000 by mid-month if customers paid as expected.

It was not perfect. Liabilities still lingered. Stock was lower but still significant. Yet the company now had a plan to manage cash flow, a better grip on profit margins, and the family working together with Maya's small, rational changes. They felt steadier, if still cautious.

4 Slow Gains and a Clearer Path

Three months after Maya's arrival, the rhythm at Northfield Furniture was different. The small deposits policy was routine. Supplier terms had improved for steady orders. The bank balance showed a healthier line. Maya's spreadsheet made it easy to see the changes.

Sales remained around £72,000 a month. Fixed costs stayed £38,000. But now Northfield had cut stock from £95,000 to about £70,000. Trade receivables were lower because deposits and better invoices reduced the wait. Liabilities had fallen toward £30,000 after payments and new agreements.

Maya met Rachel and Peter in the lunchroom. They ate sandwiches and spoke frankly. "I can see a small profit now," Rachel said. "The gross margin on re-priced orders is around 28 percent. It is not huge, but it makes a difference when we pay fixed costs."

"We still had to delay that sanding machine," Tom added, "but the team feels less rushed and less anxious at the end of the month."

Peter folded his hands. "I was worried at first. But seeing the bank grow makes me breathe easier. I like that we kept our reputation and kept our suppliers. You helped us make practical choices, Maya."

Maya explained the words as they came. "Revenue is our sales of £72,000 each month. Profit is what is left after all costs. Expenses like the fixed £38,000 reduce profit. Assets like stock are things we own but can be cash if we finish and sell them. Liabilities are what we owe, like the supplier invoices. Cash flow is how money moves in and out each month. We made small changes that improved cash flow and profit margins."

Rachel smiled. "And we learned to ask customers for a small deposit. Many were happy to help. Some left. Every business has some losses when it changes. But we kept the core."

Lucy entered with a delivery note. "Mr. Patel paid his balance today," she said. "He liked the new table and is ordering two more for December. He paid in full this time. He said he trusts us more for being clear."

Tom clapped once. "That is what we make—good work that lasts. But now we can keep the doors open without borrowing too much."

Maya felt a warm, quiet pride. She had come to prove herself, and she had helped the family make choices that kept their craft alive. The numbers were not magic; they were honest tools. She also knew the work would continue. They still planned to buy the sanding machine next year and to keep improving gross margins by reviewing prices and waste.

Peter put a hand on Maya's shoulder. "You have brought us calm and some new sense," he said. "We keep the family spirit, but now with clearer numbers. That was the right mix."

They walked through the workshop together. Wood dust in the light looked less like chaos and more like work in progress. The business was not out of the woods, but it was moving in a better direction. Cash flow was steadier, assets were lower, liabilities were controlled, and profit had room to grow. The Numbers Behind Success had become a simple lesson: care for customers, respect suppliers, and use facts to make honest choices. For Northfield Furniture, that was enough to keep making tables and a life together.

5 Hidden Costs and Old Prices

After the small wins in chapter five, Maya kept looking for causes of the tight weeks. She sat at the long table in the workshop with a cup of tea. Peter stood by the window. Rachel shuffled old order books.

Maya opened a folder of custom orders. Many were written five years ago. The prices had not changed. A custom oak desk was priced at £800. The cost to the company then was £700. The margin was small. Now material costs had risen. The same desk cost £725 to make.

Rachel frowned. "We have always kept prices steady. Our customers like that," she said. "Tradition is important."

Maya nodded. "I understand, Rachel. But the difference is now costs are higher. If we keep old prices, the margin disappears. For that oak desk, selling at £800 with a cost of £725 gives a gross profit of only £75. That is small when you add delivery and possible rework."

Peter rubbed his chin. "We do rework sometimes. If a customer changes a specification, we fix it. That costs time and maybe a van trip."

Maya checked the workshop ledger. There were many notes about discounts, delivery and rework. A note said a discount of 10 percent had been given to a local client years ago. Another note showed two free deliveries. Those choices eroded margin.

Maya also found large slow-moving stock. Timber worth £25,000 sat in the yard. Half-finished furniture added up to another £9,000. These are part of the company's assets. But too much stock ties up cash.

She explained to the team, "Stock is an asset. But if it does not sell, it is not helping our cash flow. It becomes a problem."

At the end of the morning, Maya looked at the van bills. A recent repair cost £1,200. Overtime last month added £800. Storage costs for the extra timber were £400. These are hidden expenses that did not always sit in the main numbers.

Rachel's voice was quiet. "We never counted every pound of overtime. I thought our people were doing their best, and we paid them. I did not know the cost pile-up was this big."

Maya showed a list. The large retailer, a regular customer, had an unpaid balance. They owed £15,000 and paid late. Late customer payments hurt cash flow even if overall revenue looks fine.

Peter leaned forward. "So revenue might be okay, but the money is not here when we need it?"

"Yes," said Maya. "Revenue is the total sales. But profit is what is left after expenses. Cash flow is the actual money in and out. If customer payments are late, cash flow can be negative even when there is profit on paper."

An employee, Sam, looked worried. "If cash is tight, do we cut jobs?" he asked. The room felt heavy. Everyone knew jobs were linked to the balance of numbers.

Peter answered, "We will try other ways first. Maya is finding the facts. We will not make quick cuts without thinking."

Maya suggested one change. "For new custom orders, ask for a deposit. Twenty percent at order, thirty percent on start, and the rest on delivery. That protects us and shares risk with the customer."

Rachel shook her head slightly. "A deposit feels wrong to me. The customer always trusted us. Asking for money upfront feels like business has become cold."

Maya was gentle. "It is not about trust. It is about keeping the business alive. A deposit helps with cash and reduces our exposure to late payments."

They ended the day with small steps. Rachel still held tradition close. Peter wanted practical solutions. The team was worried but willing to listen. Maya knew the next step was to make the cash picture clear with real numbers.

That evening, the workshop lights went down. Outside, the street was quiet. Maya sat alone with the ledger and the list of hidden costs. She wrote at the top: "Find simple changes that respect the family's values."

She knew this work would test trust. But she also knew clear numbers could protect jobs and the company's good name. The next morning would bring a deeper look at the cash, the overdue payments and a likely difficult conversation with the retailer.

6 The Cash Picture and a Mistake

Maya started chapter seven with a clear plan. She would make a simple cash calculation for one month. She used real numbers from the accounts. The month showed revenue of £60,000. Expenses were £52,500. That meant the accounting profit was £7,500.

She wrote the simple formula on a whiteboard: Revenue (£60,000) - Expenses (£52,500) = Profit (£7,500). Peter nodded when he saw it. He felt better that profit existed on paper.

But Maya also wrote another list. Customer payments outstanding: £18,000, including the retailer's £15,000. Stock value sitting in the yard: £25,000. Liabilities, such as a bank loan, stood at £50,000. Cash in the bank was only £2,000.

She explained, "Profit does not always equal cash. We show £7,500 profit, but cash in the bank is low because customers have not paid. Our cash flow for the month is negative when we pay the expenses now and wait for the customer payments."

Peter said, "So the business can be profitable but still run out of cash?"

Maya answered, "Yes. Profit is what we earn after costs. Cash flow is the movement of money. We must manage both."

She made a small table for the team. Cash available now: £2,000. Expected incoming customer payments this month: £3,000. Expected outgoing payments: wages and suppliers £53,000. This gave a shortfall for the month.

Then Maya made a wrong assumption. She assumed the big retailer had delayed only this month and would pay next week. She told Rachel and Peter, "If we get the £15,000 next week, cash will be okay."

Rachel looked at her. "That would be good. But are we sure?"

Maya did not check properly. She trusted the retailer's routine. Two days later, she phoned their accounts department. She heard their problem: the retailer had a slow season and could not pay the full £15,000 soon. They offered a partial payment of £5,000 in two weeks and the rest in sixty days.

Maya felt a cold spike in her stomach. She had assumed the retailer's delay was short. Now the payments would be slower. She had been wrong. She told Peter and Rachel at once.

Peter's voice was quiet but tight. "Why did we assume? We should have verified."

Maya apologised. "I wanted to give hope. I should have confirmed the payment schedule. I misread the situation."

Rachel did not shout. Her face was a mix of disappointment and worry. "We are a family business. We make mistakes. But we must tell the team the truth now."

At lunch, Maya spoke to Sam and two other workers. She explained the correct situation. She showed the numbers: short-term cash was worse than the earlier estimate. She also showed a plan: ask for a partial payment from the retailer now, speed up deposits on new custom orders, and move some slow stock to sale at a small discount.

Sam put down his sandwich. "I like the honesty. We would rather know and plan than hear a story that is not true," he said.

Maya felt relief at the honesty. The team understood the mistake and wanted to help find solutions. That evening she wrote a new list of actions. She had to contact the retailer again and call a supplier about returning extra timber.

Maya's mistake was a lesson. She learned to verify facts before promising outcomes. The team accepted her apology and felt closer. The numbers were still tight, but the company could move forward with real plans and clear expectations.

7 Talks with the Retailer and Supplier

Maya arranged two important meetings. One was with the large retailer's accounts manager. The other was with Tomas, a timber supplier. She needed to show how decisions by customers and suppliers affected the family firm.

In the retailer's small office, the manager, Clare, was honest. "Our sales dropped this quarter. We have to stretch our payments. I can release £5,000 now. I can pay another £5,000 in thirty days and the final £5,000 in sixty days," she said.

Maya listened and wrote the schedule. She also negotiated. "Can you give us a 30 percent deposit on new custom orders and 30-day payment terms for smaller stock?"

Clare considered it. "We buy a lot from you. A deposit on custom pieces is reasonable. For standard stock, we prefer longer terms. But if you need faster payment, we can try 30 days for new shipments."

Back in the workshop, Peter voiced a worry. "If we push for deposits, we might offend some customers. They are used to trust."

Maya answered, "We can explain it as a practical change. We will ask for a small deposit only for custom work. For our long-standing customers who have good payment records, we can keep the same terms for a while."

Next, Maya met Tomas at the timber yard. He had been a supplier for ten years. Maya explained the stock problem: £25,000 of slow-moving timber and extra half-finished pieces tying cash.

Tomas touched a plank. "I can take back some stock. But I need a clear plan. If you return £10,000 worth of timber, I can offer credit or a trade for faster-moving wood. That will help your cash flow."

Maya smiled. "That is useful. What about price? If we buy less often but in larger batches, can we save?"

Tomas nodded. "There are small savings on bulk orders. But your holding costs are the issue. If you carry less stock, you save on storage and risk."

Maya felt the interdependence strongly. The retailer's payment terms, the supplier's flexibility, and the company's stock levels all affected each other. She wrote it down: retailer payment schedule £5k now, £5k in 30 days, £5k in 60 days. Timber return £10k. This would free some cash.

They discussed deposits and payment terms with real examples. For a custom cabinet priced £1,200 with a cost of £9,500, Maya proposed a 20 percent deposit of £240, then 30 percent at start, and the rest on delivery. This would secure a cash flow of £240 before work began.

Tomas added, "If you ask for deposits, your supplier can trust regular orders. I can give better payment terms if I see consistent deposits."

On the way back, Maya thought of the employees. She could see Sam and others working in the workshop. Their jobs depended on these conversations. She realised small changes with the retailer and Tomas could protect wages.

That evening, she wrote a short letter to the team explaining the new deposit idea and the return deal with Tomas. She said the retailer had agreed to partial payments and that all parts of the chain must make small compromises.

Peter read the letter and said quietly, "It is a sensible plan. We keep our reputation and get the cash we need."

Rachel added, "It is a change, but it keeps the family business alive. Compromise is part of good decisions. Tradition and survival can go together."

The meetings ended the day with cautious hope. The retailer paid £5,000 soon. Tomas accepted a timber return for credit. Small practical choices began to ease the tight cash flow. Maya felt that numbers, when clear, allowed real human decisions.

8 Terms, Deposits and a Small Change

The new week began with small changes. The team updated the order forms. A line for a 20 percent deposit went on custom orders. A note about payment in 30 days for retailer shipments appeared on invoices. These were small wording changes, but they felt significant.

Maya worked with Rachel on how to explain the change to long-standing customers. "We must keep our tone honest and warm," Rachel said. "Say we need a deposit because raw costs rose, not because we distrust them."

Maya agreed. "We will say it keeps the business healthy. It also lets us continue offering quality and aftercare."

An older customer, Mrs. Lee, called about a custom table she ordered months ago at the old price. She needed some changes. Maya explained gently that the rework would mean a small extra charge and a £240 deposit before the new work began.

Mrs. Lee was quiet. "I have bought from you for ten years," she said. "I do not like new charges."

Peter took the call. "We can keep a loyalty discount for you, Mrs. Lee, and ask only for a small deposit. It helps both sides. I promise we will keep the price fair."

Mrs. Lee agreed. She also understood that the deposit protected the workshop from late payments and helps keep the work moving.

At the same time, Maya renegotiated the van insurance to reduce unexpected repair costs. She found a plan that cut the monthly premium by £50 and offered a small service package for van upkeep. This lowered the risk of another £1,200 repair bill.

The team also decided to sell some slow stock at a discount. They marked down a set of chairs and a partly finished bed. They hoped to raise £5,500 quickly. This would free up space and some cash to pay wages.

Sam said, "I hate discounts. It feels like we sell our work short."

Maya replied, "It is a practical move. The chairs are not moving. A discount now is better than no sale and more storage costs."

Peter listened and then suggested an employee bonus plan tied to sales. "If we sell the slow stock quickly, we will share a small bonus. This keeps people motivated."

Rachel was surprised. "Bonuses? We never did that. But it might be a good idea if it keeps morale up and helps clear stock."

They also agreed on a clear policy for custom orders: the 20 percent deposit, 30 percent on start, balance on delivery. For large retailers, they would ask for 30-day terms for new shipments, and monitor payments closely. If late payments doubled, they would request earlier settlements or hold shipments.

Maya updated the cash projection. The retailer's £5,000 arrival, the £10,000 timber return, and the expected £5,500 from stock clearance moved the cash position from £2,000 to a healthier figure over the next month. The expected new deposits would add another £2,500.

She showed the numbers plainly: current cash £2,000 + retailer payment £5,000 + timber credit £10,000 + stock clearance £5,500 + new deposits £2,500 = projected cash £25,000. This projection assumed no big surprises and careful expense control.

Peter smiled for the first time all week. "That is a practical step. We are not fixed yet, but we see a path."

Employees felt more secure. Rachel's trust in new methods grew. Maya learned to verify assumptions first and then to act. The balance of numbers and people felt better.

The week ended with small celebrations. They had made changes that kept the company's reputation and protected jobs. The Numbers Behind Success was not only about figures. It was about choices, care, and the small compromises that kept a family business moving forward.

As the workshop lights went down, Maya looked at the whiteboard full of numbers and names. She felt tired but hopeful. The next months would be about steady work and constant attention to cash and relationships. For now, they had taken real steps, together.

9 Price Review and New Terms

The meeting room smelled faintly of polish and coffee. Maya spread the latest numbers on the table: revenue for the month was still steady at £36,000, cost of goods sold was £21,600, and that left a gross profit of £14,400. After rent, heat, and wages, the business had operating expenses of £10,400 and a net profit of £4,000 for the month. The bank balance was £8,500. Rachel tapped a pen and watched the others.

Tom, the owner, rubbed his forehead. "We have good revenue, but our cash is tight. Stock sits on the floor and does not turn fast enough. That keeps our assets high but our liquid cash low." He pointed at the stock figure on Maya's sheet: stock valued at £45,000 as part of total assets of £165,000. "We need practical changes. Not big speeches. What will customers accept?"

Maya spoke clearly. "We need a price review by product. Not one price across the board. Some items sell well even at a small increase. Others are slow and we can clear them with a discount. If we raise the price of made-to-order cabinets by seven percent, that will increase revenue on those items by about £1,200 a month, while leaving our slower stock ready for clearance."

Rachel frowned. "Customers will notice. I already had Mr Whitaker on the line last week. He is loyal, but he is sensitive to price. He will object to increases on his regular chairs."

Maya nodded. "We must be honest. For custom work, we introduce deposits. We ask for 30 percent at order, 40 percent at delivery or mid-point, and final payment on completion. That staged invoice approach improves cash flow. If we require a £3,500 total in deposits on current custom orders, that would immediately add to our cash position."

Tom calculated aloud. "If we collect £3,500 in deposits and clear £5,000 worth of slow stock with small discounts, our bank balance will improve. But we must keep a balance between pricing and loyalty. Rachel, can you talk to Mr Whitaker? Offer him the older price if he pays a deposit today. He is loyal and likely to do that."

Rachel bit her lip. "I can do that. I am afraid we will lose some orders when we raise prices. But perhaps loyalty and the convenience of a deposit plan will keep customers."

Maya continued, "Also, staged invoices can help our receivables and reduce the pressure on liabilities. Right now our known liabilities total £70,000, including supplier payables of £25,000 and a bank loan of £30,000. We have to show suppliers a plan. We will not hide anything."

Tom looked at the team. "Let's try a small, specific change first. Increase prices on made-to-order cabinets by seven percent, keep chairs the same for loyal customers who pay deposits, and run a slow-stock clearance over four weeks with clear discounts. Introduce a formal deposit policy of 30 percent for custom work. We also stage invoices: 30-40-30 for larger pieces."

Rachel took a deep breath. "I'll call Mr Whitaker and three other regulars. I'll explain the deposit idea and the loyalty offer."

Maya added one more tool: a weekly cash forecast. "We need to track inflows and outflows every week. If we forecast our cash, we will see any shortfalls six or seven days ahead. That way we can ask for staged payments or delay non-essential purchases, and we can schedule deliveries without over-ordering materials."

Tom agreed. "Make the price list product by product. Show the change to our regulars with a clear reason: rising material costs and a need to protect staff wages. We will be practical, not aggressive."

They agreed to test the system for one month. The work felt real and the team felt the small relief of having a plan. Yet the edges of anxiety remained. Maya knew forecasts and plans helped, but they needed fast action if an unexpected bill arrived.

10 Suppliers, Scheduling and Tight Terms

Two days after the price review, Rachel drove to meet Alan, their main supplier. Alan had supplied timber and fittings for years. He was practical but blunt. Rachel sat with a paper cup of tea and explained the new deposit policy and the need to delay some purchases until stock moved.

Alan listened and then said, "I understand, Rachel. I like the family too. But I can't extend terms. My own cash is tight. I have bills and a loan to the bank. I have an invoice of £18,000 from you due in ten days and I need to pay my workers. I can't carry that for long."

Rachel felt her chest tighten. "Alan, we will try to pay, but we also need you to deliver materials on time. If we delay you, we lose orders. Can we schedule the big deliveries to match our cash receipts?"

Alan sighed. "I can ship smaller batches when you pay for each batch. I can't give a long credit extension. If you can pay, I'll deliver. If not, I'll pause deliveries."

Rachel returned to the workshop and told Tom and Maya. The supplier situation made the team uneasy. Alan's inability to extend terms meant they must manage purchase scheduling carefully. Maya pulled up the forecast and changed the purchase plan so raw materials arrived only when the cash would likely cover the bill. She also recommended prioritising purchases for orders with deposits already paid.

Maya sat the team down and drew a simple weekly cash forecast on a whiteboard. The bank balance was £8,500. This week there were normal outflows: wages at £8,000 due next week, regular expenses of £1,200, and supplier invoices of £18,000 coming due in ten days. On the inflow side, retail client payments included a £12,000 invoice from a local retailer that was delayed and not certain to arrive this week. Customer deposits were projected at £3,500 if Rachel's calls worked.

Maya said, "If the retailer payment is delayed, and if tax or a direct debit hits the account, we will have a problem. We must plan for that risk. We will prioritise wages first and then suppliers for deliveries that keep revenue coming. For the rest, we will ask for partial payments or delay purchase orders."

Tom asked quietly, "What about asking the bank for a short overdraft?" Maya looked at him calmly. "We can ask, but the bank will want security. We cannot promise new borrowing as a solution. That is not a miracle. We must adjust our operations so we do not rely on borrowing."

That afternoon, Rachel heard from the retailer. The message was short: their payment that day would be late by at least a week. The words hit the team like cold rain. The delayed £12,000 would have bridged most of their scheduled purchases.

Maya checked the numbers again and felt a knot. She reminded the team about the upcoming tax direct debit that might come in any day. Their plan to ask for deposits and run a clearance had to be quicker. There was no time for slow changes.

11 The Direct Debit and Difficult Choices

Early the next morning, the bank called. A tax direct debit of £9,200 had been lodged by HMRC and would be taken that day. The company bank balance was £8,500. If the tax went through, the account would go to -£700 before any other payments. The team gathered around the office desk as if the number on the screen were a storm cloud.

Tom's shoulders hunched. "If it goes through, and we still have wages in a week for £8,000, we will be in trouble. We must make a quick decision. Do we protect wages or suppliers?"

Maya spoke with calm, firm words. "We must protect wages first. Our staff depend on their pay. I will make three immediate moves. One: start a fast-stock clearance today to raise cash quickly — pick a few items that won't damage our brand and sell them at a small discount. Two: ask customers with upcoming orders for immediate deposits, using the new 30 percent rule. Three: try to collect any showroom sales today. If we can raise about £10,500 quickly, we can cover the tax, pay wages when due, and have something left for critical suppliers."

Rachel began calling customers. She explained the deposit plan honestly to Mr Whitaker first. He objected to a price increase, but when Rachel offered him the old price in return for a 30 percent deposit that day, he agreed. That sale brought in £1,200. A local couple bought a demo sofa for immediate delivery and paid £2,000. The team cleared three slow stock items and raised £5,000 in small sales. In total, they raised £8,200 that morning and afternoon.

The tax direct debit hit in the evening. The bank balance dropped from £8,500 to -£700. Then the day's new cash inflows of £8,200 were processed, and the account came back up to £7,500. That number gave them breathing space. They could pay wages next week and still keep a small buffer.

Tom exhaled and then made another hard decision. "Alan's invoice for £18,000 is still due in ten days. Alan told us he cannot extend terms because his own cash is tight. We cannot pay him in full now. We will offer a partial payment of £1,800 as good faith and explain our plan. We will prioritise wages and essential deliveries. We must accept Alan may pause some shipments until we clear the balance."

Maya helped write the message to Alan. It said: we will pay £1,800 today and will pay the remaining balance as soon as the £12,000 retailer payment arrives. It acknowledged Alan's position and asked if he could supply a small batch in the meantime. Alan replied that he could accept the partial payment as a part settlement but could not continue deliveries until the account was nearer to current. It was not ideal, but it was honest and practical.

Rachel felt guilty and worried. "We might lose some orders or delay jobs. People will be annoyed," she said. Maya touched her arm. "I know. But you kept people working this week and protected wages. That matters. We also set a new rule: from now on, no custom job will start without a 30 percent deposit and staged invoices thereafter. That reduces future risk."

At the end of the chapter of this day, the team had made difficult choices. They had protected staff pay and found quick sales to keep cash moving. They accepted supplier strain and a delay in some orders. It was a practical compromise, not a magic fix. Everyone felt tired but a little steadier in the knowledge that they had faced the immediate problem together.

12 Weekly Forecasts and Practical Compromises

The next week began with a quieter, more purposeful rhythm. Maya prepared a weekly cash forecast and printed it for everyone. It listed the bank balance of £7,500 after the weekend's sales, incoming payments of £12,000 from the retailer expected in five days (though late previously), customer payments and deposits of £3,500, and the scheduled wages of £8,000 next week. Supplier payables totalled £25,000 of which Alan's share was £18,000. The liabilities remained £70,000 and assets £165,000, but the company had more control of short-term cash flow.

Maya explained her core changes: a weekly cash forecast, a purchase schedule matched to confirmed payments and deposits, and a simple cost approval process where any purchase over £1,000 needed sign-off by Tom and Maya. She also proposed a slow-stock clearance plan that would not damage the brand—select items at low margins, promoted as "floor samples."

Tom liked the tone of the rules. "These are small steps but practical ones," he said. "They make us honest about cash and responsible with suppliers."

Rachel still worried about losing orders if prices went up. A loyal customer objected to the new price of a custom cabinet. He said his local council supplier offered a lower price. Rachel negotiated a compromise: a small loyalty discount if the customer paid a 30 percent deposit plus a promise of priority delivery. The customer agreed. The deposit was £1,500 and the order remained in their books.

Maya recorded the resulting change in projected revenue and profit. If the price adjustments and deposit policy worked, the company could add £1,200 monthly revenue from cabinet price changes, and staging payments would smooth out cash flow so operating expenses would be met on time. It would not instantly increase net profit dramatically, but it would reduce the risk of bad cash shortfalls and forced delays.

Alan called that afternoon. He was calmer. He wanted assurance that the scheduled payment from the retailer would arrive, otherwise his own cash problems would bite again. Maya offered a practical way forward: a schedule of staged payments to Alan only when receipts came in, with a clear process to prioritize deliveries for orders with deposits. Alan did not like long waits but agreed to this new process as a temporary measure. He appreciated the honesty.

The team also wrote a short supplier guide. It explained their new purchase schedule, the deposit rule for custom work, and the staged invoice timeline. It was shared with Alan and their other smaller suppliers. Some suppliers pushed back on deposits, but most accepted the change when told it reduced the chance of late payments or cancellations.

By Friday, the retailer's delayed payment of £12,000 arrived. The bank balance climbed. The immediate pressure eased, but everyone understood the lesson. Tom thanked the team in the workshop as they cleared a small delivery area for new stock. "We made hard choices," he said softly. "We protected our people and took practical steps to be clearer with suppliers and customers."

Maya smiled, not triumphant but relieved. "We will keep using the weekly forecast. We'll review price by product once a month and keep the deposit rules. This is not perfect, but it is steady. Cash flow will be the measure, not hope."

As the week closed, Rachel felt less fearful. She had kept key customers and had new tools to explain changes. Alan had agreed to the temporary plan. The company still had £7,500 cash, assets of £165,000 and liabilities of £70,000, but the path forward was clearer. They had made compromises that kept the business honest, the people paid, and the relationships mostly intact. It was the practical work of a family business finding its way through the numbers behind success.

13 Applying the Plan at the Workshop

Peter stood in the middle of the workshop and looked at the benches. The plan from the weekly forecasts felt right, but the team needed to act. "We must finish what we have," he said. "And we will keep our craft. But money matters too."

Maya had a printout of numbers. She laid it on a crate. "Last month we had revenue of £24,500, expenses of £22,900, and profit of £1,600," she said. Her voice was calm. "Cash in the bank is £9,300. Unfinished stock is valued at £9,200. Liabilities are £15,500, including the bank loan of £12,000 and supplier credit of £3,500."

Rachel moved between the shop and the computer. She had spoken to two customers and the retailer who had returned a small batch of tables. "They sent back five tables. The value the retailer says is £1,200," she said. "They issued a credit note, not a cash refund yet. That affects our expected customer payments."

Peter rubbed his forehead. The plan had said to reduce unfinished stock and to get better payment terms from customers. Now they had a return and a new order. The new order was from a community centre. It wanted 20 chairs at £150 each. That is £3,000 revenue, but the cost to them to make the chairs is £2,700. The margin is thin—just £300 profit for the whole order.

Maya wrote the numbers down. "We asked for a 30% deposit," she said. "They paid £900 today. That helps cash flow." She underlined the word cash flow so the number stood out. Cash flow meant actual cash coming in and out each week.

Sam, the workshop foreman, checked the benches. "We can make these chairs," he said. "But if we keep the low-margin contracts, they take time and space. That keeps our unfinished stock high." He felt the pressure of orders piling up.

They talked about the long contract with Townline Interiors. It brought steady revenue of £6,000 a month, Peter explained, but only £240 profit because the job left little margin. "People like us because of that deal," Peter said. "It keeps our name in stores. But it costs us on margin and on time."

Maya pointed to the spreadsheet. "We must show the real numbers. That contract ties up benches and labour. If we stop it, we lose £6,000 revenue, but we free capacity for higher-margin work and reduce unfinished stock over time."

Rachel listened. She worried about customers and reputation. "If we drop Townline, what will people say?" she asked. "Our name matters. But I also see customers who want clearer terms. Some paid a deposit quickly when I asked. Others left when we tightened conditions."

Peter made a small decision then. "We will not react today. Finish these chairs and get their deposit logged as customer payment. Maya, run the monthly cash-flow forecast again. Put the Townline numbers in and show me the impact for three months."

Maya nodded and left with the printout. She felt tense but ready. The deposit of £900 had gone into the bank, so cash would show £10,200 now. She wrote that number carefully in her ledger.

That evening Sam and the team worked steadily. They understood the new emphasis: fewer half-done pieces, clearer customer payments, and finishing what they started. The workshop smelled of wood and polish, and the work felt like progress.

In the shop Rachel told a customer about the return and the chairs. "We ask for deposits now," she said. "It helps us keep stock and leads times fair. Some customers like it. Some do not. But you get a clearer promise."

One regular customer, Mrs. Hale, paused. She bought a small coffee table and listened. "I support you," she said softly. "I paid a deposit for my bench. I will wait. But reputation matters too." Her words made Peter smile when Rachel told him later.

By the end of the day Maya had updated the short forecast. It showed the deposit as a customer payment and marked the retailer's credit note as pending. She noted accounts receivable of £6,300 that were not yet paid. She also saw supplier credit of £3,500 due in ten days.

Peter sat at his old desk. He realised he could delegate the numbers now. He had done the bookkeeping for years. He still valued the craft and the relationships. But Maya's clear sheets meant he could focus on the workshop and the people. He told Rachel and Sam, "You handle the shop and the benches. Maya handles the money. I'll talk to Townline myself."

They left the workshop later. The deposit improved cash and gave the team energy. But the return and the Townline decision hung in the air. Old reputation and new finance had to meet somewhere in the middle.

That night Peter opened a drawer and took out an old ledger. He ran his finger over the handwriting. He had to keep the craft and the business. He felt tired, but the numbers showed hope. The plan had begun. Cash flow was clearer. Unfinished stock was visible. And they had one small win—a deposit of £900 that meant a customer payment on the books.

14 Testing the Changes

A week passed with a steady rhythm. The team finished many items. Energy in the workshop rose. Sam organised shifts so finishing could catch up. That cut unfinished stock from £9,200 to £7,400 during the week of work.

Maya updated the figures. She used simple lines: revenue so far still £24,500 for the month, but once the chairs were delivered the revenue would increase by £3,000. She wrote a note: expected revenue after delivery £27,500. Expenses were under control at £22,500 because overtime had dropped a little and storage costs fell.

Peter watched the deliveries. When they loaded the van for the community centre, Rachel checked the paperwork. The customer paid the remaining £2,100 at delivery. That payment was a customer payment they could bank that week. It improved cash flow and made the order truly profitable.

Maya changed the cash number. The cash in bank rose from £10,200 to £12,300 after that payment. Then the retailer issued a small cash refund for the returned tables. It was £600. Cash rose to £12,900. Every bit helped with the weekly cash flow swings.

Sam counted the finished chairs. "We lost time before to low-margin work," he said. "But finishing these reduced the backlog. Unfinished stock is lower. We are moving product." He felt relief at the benches being clear.

Even with the wins, not everything was simple. A small dispute with a supplier led to an invoice delay. Maya moved the supplier credit in the ledger from immediate to pending. "We still owe suppliers £3,200 in about two weeks," she said. She kept that as a short-term liability on the sheet.

Rachel spoke to long-standing customers and also to a small high-street retailer. The retailer liked the new clearer terms. They paid deposits on future orders quickly. Two smaller customers, however, did not like the deposit rule and decided to go elsewhere. The revenue that those customers brought was small—about £400 a month—but every loss mattered.

Peter and Maya sat for an hour and looked at margins on each job. The chairs earned £3,000 revenue and cost £2,700 to make. The profit for that job was £300. It was not huge, but it was a positive margin. That small profit, repeated, was healthier than low-margin work that used the same resources and left almost no profit.

By the middle of the month their reported revenue had risen on paper to £27,500. Expenses had fallen to £22,500. That made profit £5,000 for the month so far, if nothing else changed. Peter felt the numbers in his chest. The profit could pay a little back on the loan and rebuild a safety buffer.

They also saw other benefits. Lower unfinished stock reduced the need to buy more materials immediately. That freed cash and reduced assets tied up in work-in-progress. Maya explained, "Less stock means less money sitting idle. That helps our cash flow and reduces storage expenses."

At the end of the week there was a small celebration. Rachel bought a cake from the shop down the road. They marked the progress—not success, but better process. Peter raised a paper cup of tea. "We are not out of the woods," he said. "But this is a start. Keep the records tight. Keep the deposits clear. Finish what we promise."

Maya stayed late and ran the forecast again. She added the small retailer's new deposit. She marked two customer payments still outstanding in accounts receivable of £6,300. The forecast showed that if collections continued and expenses stayed at £22,500, the month could end with £3,000 to £4,000 profit. That was a hopeful number.

Peter felt a new trust growing. He let Maya present the figures to him. He kept craft decisions for the workshop and let Rachel handle customer relations. The clarity of roles made the team calmer. It also meant that when tough choices came, like the Townline contract, he could make them with a clearer picture of the numbers.

They closed another day with wood dust on their shoes and clearer ledgers on the desk. The small deposit had become a practical win. Finishing stock had freed time. Cash flow looked better. But they all knew one bad return or a late payment could still hurt the month.

15 A Hard Decision on Old Work

Peter reviewed the contract with Townline Interiors again. It had been valuable for the company's reputation. But the numbers were blunt. The Townline work brought £6,000 in revenue a month and only £240 profit. It used space and labour that could make other work with higher margins.

Maya made a projection for three months. She presented two paths. Path A kept Townline and assumed steady revenue. Path B gave three months' notice to Townline, stopped the contract after that, and tried to replace some of the revenue with local custom orders that averaged a 12% margin. She showed expected cash flow and the effect on liabilities.

Rachel felt torn. "We have a reputation for being reliable for big clients," she said. "If we stop, some will praise us for standing up for the business, but others will say we turned them away."

Peter listened and then surprised them. "We will give three months' notice," he said. "We cannot cut ties suddenly. Reputation matters. But we cannot continue to run jobs that bring almost no profit and tie up benches. That is not sustainable."

Maya put the numbers on the table. She said that if Townline stopped now, they would lose £6,000 revenue in future months. But they had already found a next month's order from the Bexley Café for £3,500 with an expected profit of £420. Two small customers leaving cost £400 in revenue. The immediate projected revenue was therefore £24,600 if Townline left and these changes happened.

Peter thought about capacity. Sam had agreed to take on some of the custom work. The team could use benches freed up by not doing low-margin runs. They could take better-paying local orders, finish them quickly, and improve cash flow.

There was a personal side too. Old clients called Peter a friend. One of the Townline buyers, Mr. Clarke, had worked with Peter for years. Rachel phoned him, explained the three-month notice, and promised careful handover. He was disappointed but understanding. "We will miss you," he said. "But I respect your decision. Keep in touch."

Maya tracked immediate cash changes. The Bexley Café paid a 25% deposit of £875. That went into the bank and raised cash. Two small customers leaving meant potential monthly revenue lost was £400. They adjusted the short-term forecast.

Unfinished stock fell again to £6,200 as the team finished backlog work. Expenses dropped a touch to £21,800 because overtime dropped and storage rental charges were lower. The pattern was getting healthier.

But not everyone was happy. A local independent shop had been a steady small client. When Rachel explained the new deposit policy they moved to a competitor. She felt bad. "I hate losing them," she said. "They were good people. But we must be fair and clear for the whole business."

Peter delegated the financial review to Maya. He still made craft and client decisions, but he accepted Maya's numbers as the guide. This move freed him to talk to clients and to keep the shop's reputation intact as they shifted the business balance toward healthier margins.

Maya felt validated when Peter told her. "I trust you with the books," he said quietly. "I still make the calls about who we work with, but you tell me the costs and the cash. That matters."

They agreed to keep Townline for three months while they slowly replace volume. It was a compromise that preserved relationships and started a real change in direction. The team felt the weight lift a little as benches became less crowded and the weekly forecast looked less risky.

At the end of the chapter of this week, Maya wrote the projected numbers in the ledger: next month's projected revenue £24,600, projected expenses £21,800, projected profit £2,800. She capped the page and put the sheet in Peter's folder. It was a clear number, not a promise. That clarity itself was progress.

16 A Realistic Monthly Review

The month closed with a quiet meeting in the back office. Peter, Rachel and Maya sat around the old table with cups of tea and a printout. The ledger had all the numbers they had tracked. It was time for a monthly review.

Maya put the final numbers on the table. "Revenue for the month ended at £25,700," she said. "We had some disputes and a returned batch that reduced what we expected. Expenses totalled £22,100, including a small tool repair of £300. That leaves a profit of £3,600 for the month."

Peter took in the figures slowly. They were better than the early months but still not a big sum. He felt relief that profit had risen from £1,600 a month or so before to £3,600 now. It was a sign that margins were improving and the plan worked in part.

Maya explained the cash position. "Cash at the bank is £12,975. We have assets of £54,375, which includes machinery at £30,000, finished stock valued at £5,200, and unfinished stock at £6,200. Liabilities are £15,200, which includes the loan and supplier credit. That gives us equity of £39,175."

The meeting ended with a small sense of closure and realism. They had not solved everything. Revenue was still changing month to month. The three-month Townline notice would be a test. That evening, as they locked up, Rachel hugged Maya. "You have changed how we work," she said. "We needed that. The family needed that." Peter watched, and his expression softened. He had begun to let go of a little control and the family felt more honest with each other.

They walked home separately under the same streetlights. The town was quiet, and the workshop lights were off. The numbers were not magic. They were tools. Peter knew reputation still mattered, but Maya's work had shown him that reputation needed sound management to survive. The month closed, and with it came a clearer path forward.
