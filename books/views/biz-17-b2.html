0 A Tight Runway and a Taller Promise

The ClearRoute office was two rooms above a bakery, the smell of fresh bread drifting through the open window as Maya Ellis wrote numbers on the whiteboard. “£62,000,” she said aloud. “Monthly burn eight and a half. That’s seven months.”

Leo Martin sat on the radiator, laptop balanced on his knees. He kept his hands folded, the curl of a man who coded and fixed things rather than sold them. “Seven months if nothing changes. We need customers or a better burn plan,” he replied. His voice had that careful tone of someone who expected the code to be the truth. “And don’t forget product limitations. The MVP doesn’t do automated reorders or full delivery routing algorithms. It gives alerts and suggested routes.”

Priya Nair, who did marketing, flipped through a stack of printed screenshots. “People want a story,” she said. “They want confidence. We can show how shops can reduce missed deliveries and waste. We’ll use George’s numbers as proof—real evidence.”

They had all heard George’s story. He owned a small grocer in Bedminster, three employees, a fridge full of fresh food and a gentle worry that more bread would end up wasted because deliveries were missed or stock management was sloppy. He had been the kind of customer that turned their idea into a product; a good problem, a clear need.

Maya looked at them and smiled in the way of someone who could already see the headlines. At twenty-eight she loved the moment before everything aligned. “We can promise clear cuts in missed deliveries, reduced waste, and a smooth pilot in two weeks. We’ll say: ClearRoute automates smart routing and stock alerts so your shop misses fewer orders and throws away less.”

Leo’s hand rose. “We need to be precise. The system gives route suggestions and alerts when stock is low based on sales patterns. It can’t automatically place orders with suppliers, and it doesn’t connect with all delivery partners yet. If we say ‘automates’ we’ll be misleading.”

Maya’s eyes flicked to the whiteboard. “Investors like certainty. Customers like certainty. We have to sell them the future.”

Priya put the screenshots down. “We can be proud and bold,” she said. “But honest. The message can be simple: fewer missed deliveries, less waste, and affordable pricing. We’ve landed the pricing at £45 a month per shop for the basic plan. That’s competitive, and easy to explain.”

The ownership split was on the bottom corner of Maya’s notes—an uncomfortable but necessary fact. Maya fifty percent, Leo thirty, Priya twenty. It sat alongside firm figures: twelve pilot shops signed up, twelve times forty-five pounds a month, bringing in £540 monthly recurring revenue. Small, but real.

That evening Maya walked to George’s shop on her own. She liked the directness of meetings where you could see the bread on the shelf and count the boxes in the storeroom. George was honest and gruff in the way of someone who had run a shop for thirty years. He needed a solution that would stop him calling suppliers three times a week and losing sales to missed items.

Around midnight, the team left the office. Maya felt the push of momentum. They had seven months, George’s pilot, and an invitation pending from investor Adrian Cole in six weeks. If they could convert pilots to paying shops, they could show traction. If they could show growth and tighten the burn, maybe the runway would stretch. She did not know yet how a single sentence in a sales call would ripple through January and into the investor meeting ahead.

1 The Call That Pulled the Thread

The next morning Maya woke to three missed calls and a voicemail from George. Her stomach tightened as she listened. “Maya, we had a missed delivery yesterday,” George said, voice brittle. “I was sure you said it’d handle the reorders and the driver routes. My staff were confused and the tomatoes went off. What now?”

She sat on the edge of her bed and felt the familiar two-pulse thud of panic and resolve. She called him back. “George, I’m really sorry. I over-explained earlier. ClearRoute gives strong alerts and suggested routes, but we don’t yet automate ordering with suppliers. We should have made that clearer.”

There was silence. Then George spoke, quieter: “Honesty is better than promises. But money and food don’t wait for clarity. We can’t have people missing deliveries and me paying for produce I don’t sell.”

When Maya hung up, she told Leo and Priya immediately. Leo’s face grew long. “This is why the language matters,” he said. “We should be able to say ‘we’re building towards automation’ rather than ‘we automate’. George’s trust is part of our evidence. Without him, the case to investors is weaker.”

Priya, who had drafted the pitch slides, ran a hand over her chin. “We can fix this publicly and ethically,” she said. “Say sorry, say exactly what we do, and outline a short roadmap for the feature people expect.”

They called George and offered a short-term workaround: a tighter schedule for alerts, manual confirmation steps, and a weekly check-in to make sure deliveries matched orders. They also offered a fee credit for the month he had lost fresh produce. He accepted the credit and the check-ins, but his earlier warmth had cooled.

Helen Shaw, their mentor, came by a few days later. Helen had sold a business and now advised founders with a no-nonsense approach. She listened to the recording of Maya’s earlier call and to the team’s discussion of finances. At the end she tapped her pen on a notepad. “This startup life is always a negotiation between aspiration and reality,” she said. “You need both. Maya, the instinct to promise comes from wanting to seal the deal. But the truth is a better foundation for long-term customers and investors. Rebuild trust with George by showing him exactly what you will do and when.”

Helen asked about the numbers. The team repeated them: £62,000 in the bank, a burn of £8,500 a month, runway seven months. “And your pricing?” she asked. “£45 per month per shop basic; £120 for multi-branch premium,” Priya answered. “Twelve pilot shops signed up, but only eight are active,” Leo added. Active meant they used the dashboard frequently and responded to alerts. The revenue remained small—£360 monthly recurring revenue from active shops, an alarming reality when burn was £8,500.

Helen folded her hands. “That’s not unusual at this stage,” she said. “But you must treat each customer like an investor. George is not merely a user; he is proof of product-market fit. Damage to that relationship costs you credibility—especially when you have an investor meeting in six weeks. Fix it, and document every step.”

That night, the founders sat with Helen’s advice and the ledger open. Maya felt the weight of a single sentence made in eagerness. The team could recover, but only by aligning their messages with the product they actually had and the honest roadmap they could deliver.

2 Roadmaps, Risks, and a Rigged Demo

The week that followed became a blur of technical fixes and message edits. Leo rewrote parts of the dashboard so alerts were clearer and the manual reorder button could be emphasized in the UX. He added logs so the team could show proof of the system suggesting actions. Priya reshaped the pitch deck, replacing the phrase ‘full automation’ with ‘automated suggestions with manual confirmation’, and added George’s week-over-week data as a case study.

Maya pushed for bold visuals. “If we can show a 30 percent reduction in missed deliveries in the pilot, Adrian will be impressed,” she said. She wanted graphs, headlines, and a short customer testimonial video. Priya smiled; she liked the headline. Leo frowned. “We must not rig the demo,” he said. “We should show real numbers, not a best-case scenario.”

They argued like colleagues who had gone too long without sleep. Maya believed boldness would win the investor and then free them to build. Leo believed honesty and technical certainty would carry them through both customer relationships and due diligence. Priya tried to be the mediator, restraining Maya’s shine with facts and helping Leo find a way to present the technical limitations without sounding incapable.

In the middle of the week George’s shop reported improvement: one fewer missed delivery, better ordering concentrations, and staff who found the suggested routes useful. The system had not automated the orders, but the manual checks cut mistakes. When Maya saw the numbers—ten percent reduction in missed deliveries in that first week—she felt vindicated. Ten percent was better than nothing, but she still wanted more for the slides.

They mapped the roadmap on sticky notes. Phase one: stabilize alerts and improve UX by end of month. Phase two: integrate with two regional suppliers to support semi-automated orders in three months. Phase three: begin full delivery routing algorithms once they proved the data quality. The roadmap was honest but ambitious.

Adrian Cole’s invitation sat in the calendar like a blinking light: investor meeting in six weeks. Adrian was a seasoned angel and early-stage investor in Bristol. He had invested in logistics before and had a reputation for blunt, practical questions. The team knew he would test both their numbers and their integrity.

They rehearsed the pitch twice a day. Maya polished the opening: a crisp problem statement, a human story—George—and a slide that compared food waste margins to the cost of their subscription. Priya worked on customer evidence slides and a clear pricing table—£45 per shop for basic, £120 for multi-branch—along with CAC estimates. Leo prepared to answer technical questions and to show product limitations honestly: no automatic reorders yet, no full driver integration, but strong signals from sales patterns.

On Thursday Leo found a bug in the route suggestion algorithm. It occurred in rare cases when the sales data from a shop was sparse, leading the system to suggest suboptimal delivery sequences. He reported it; the fix would take three days. Maya felt the sting of urgency. “We can scope the demo to shops without sparse data,” she said. “We’ll present the best performing case.”

Leo pushed back. “If Adrian asks about data sparsity, we must answer. If he asks why we selected the shops in the demo, we must be ready.”

Maya sat late at the office that night drafting the investor story. She still wanted to promise the moon. But as she typed and erased and typed again, the story that fit reality felt stronger. It was not as shiny, but it was credible. That credibility would be their currency when Adrian leaned in with his questions.

3 Ethics, Equity, and the Ask

Week five narrowed the runway until it gleamed. The numbers did not change: £62,000, burn £8,500. The team could count months left like breaths before a swim. The deck had a new slide on traction: eight active shops, twelve pilots, weekly active users metric at forty percent for staff, and a ten percent reduction in missed deliveries in the strongest pilot—George’s.

The big question still waited: how much to ask for? Maya wanted a figure that would give them runway and speed: £300,000. Priya nodded; the marketing plan and hiring a sales lead and customer success person made sense. Leo wanted a smaller, safer ask that would focus the team on product maturity: £200,000, recruit a senior engineer, and fix the technical debt first.

They argued through the logic. £300,000 for twenty percent would imply a pre-money valuation of £1.2 million—ambitious, but possible if Adrian believed in market fit and growth potential. £200,000 for twenty-five percent felt humble but could buy them the engineering time they needed. Equity dilution meant something visceral to Maya—her fifty percent would shrink, but the capital could buy more headroom.

They framed the ask to Adrian as a proposed instrument: seed equity with milestones. Priya prepared a slide showing use of funds: 40 percent product development and hiring, 30 percent sales and customer success to lower CAC, 20 percent marketing and partnership building, and 10 percent reserves. Leo’s technical milestones were explicit—deliver a robust confidence scoring mechanism for route suggestions within three months and pilot integrated supplier connections within five months.

Helen offered a final set of words: “Ask for what you need. But structure the deal to protect both sides. Many angels want milestones because that reduces risk. Be clear how you measure success.”

They also rehearsed an ethical stance. If Adrian pressed them about George’s earlier feelings, they would say: we overpromised in one sales call, we apologised and provided a credit, we reworked onboarding language, and we have measurable improvements in delivery rates through manual confirmation. Honest transparency might dent the shine, but it would show they had the character investors look for.

A week before the meeting Maya felt the old temptation to over-promise return of capital. She sat alone with the revenue numbers and her mental projections. She drafted two asks: A asks for £300,000 for twenty percent with faster hiring timelines; B asks for £200,000 for twenty-five percent tied to delivered product milestones. She wrote out reasons for each and then changed the deck to present both options as scenarios.

Leo, practical as ever, said, “Present one plan and a fallback. Investors hate indecision.” Priya agreed. They chose plan B publicly in the deck—a conservative ask with clear milestones—and prepared a paragraph explaining plan A as a stretch option, contingent on accelerated commitments from strategic partners. Honesty again: no illusion of secrecy.

They practiced the investor meeting until the script felt like a conversation. Adrian’s credentials were in the back of their minds: years in logistics tech, an investor who had both backed companies and sat on boards. They imagined him asking about churn, CAC, pricing elasticity, and technical debt. They imagined him pushing back on valuation.

They left the mock meeting drained but steadier. The argument about equity and ethics had clarified the ask. They would be honest, they would protect the product’s reputation, and they would ask precisely for what they required. The only unknown was Adrian himself, and that would be revealed in person on the day.

4 The Meeting with Adrian Cole

Adrian Cole arrived on a damp Bristol morning, umbrella folded in his hand and a presence that suggested both patience and impatience. He sat in the ClearRoute office with a coffee and read their deck. He was in his fifties, had a map of logistics investments behind him, and asked the first question without preamble: “Start with the problem. Tell me why the independent shops are still suffering despite many logistics solutions.”

Maya began: “Independent food shops juggle suppliers, manual order books, and staff with limited time. Missed deliveries happen because routes are inefficient and stock runs out because ordering is manual or based on guesswork. For a small grocer, missing three percent of orders can cut into margins that are already thin.”

Adrian nodded. He studied the slide with George’s data. “Show me proof,” he said. Priya brought up the week-over-week numbers for George: initial missed deliveries at eight percent of orders, reduced to six percent after the first week with manual confirmations; revenue impact measured in saved stock of roughly £120 a week. Adrian’s eyes narrowed on the active shop metric—eight active shops, twelve pilots.

“Customer evidence is solid,” he said. “But scale is the key. Tell me your unit economics and CAC.”

Priya spoke: “Our average revenue per shop is £45 per month. Our current CAC—customer acquisition cost—is about £120 per shop, because pilot setup takes a lot of hand-holding. With the right customer success and sales hires, we project CAC down to £60 in nine months.”

Adrian tapped his pen. “With £45 monthly, LTV matters. What retention are you seeing?”

Leo answered, precise: “Current retention is early—around sixty percent at three months among pilot shops that become active. Our LTV, assuming a three-year average customer lifetime, is about £1,620 at current pricing. There’s room to increase revenue through premium offerings and supplier commissions, but we can only scale when the product handles data sparsity and integrates with suppliers for semi-automation.”

Adrian asked about the technical limitations directly: “You say you don’t automate orders. What would it take to get there, and what risks remain?”

Leo took that one. He explained the bug they had found: sparse data caused the route suggestion algorithm to perform worse, and the system needed a smoothing layer and confidence scoring. He laid out the technical roadmap—hire a senior engineer, dedicate three sprints to the smoothing algorithm and confidence model, then build two supplier integrations for pilot semi-automation. The timeline: three months for the smoothing layer, five months for supplier integrations. He also spelled out costs—£80,000 for the engineering hires and platform upgrades to reach full confidence in suggestions.

Adrian listened, then leaned forward. “Walk me through the numbers of your ask.”

Maya spoke, steady now: “We are asking for £250,000 for twenty-two percent equity. Use of funds: 40 percent product and engineering, 30 percent sales and customer success to reduce CAC, 20 percent marketing and partnerships, 10 percent reserves. This gives us about twenty months of runway if we reduce burn by hiring strategically and increasing revenue through conversion of pilots.”

Adrian calculated quickly on his tablet. “You currently have £62,000 and burn £8,500 a month. Why not ask for the full £300,000? That would give you more runway for experimentation.”

Outside, the bakery clock chimed. The team had a deal that bought time and demanded honesty. They had addressed the consequence of Maya’s early promise by being transparent in public with George and in private with an investor. In doing so, they had not solved every problem—data sparsity still needed fixing, and the integration work would be hard—but they had arrived at a path that respected both ambition and truth.

5 Interviews, Insights, and a Tight Script

Maya put the laptop on the long meeting table and looked at the five faces around her. They had fewer than six months of runway left, as they'd agreed in earlier meetings — that fact felt like a steady clock in the room. "We need to be clear about the pitch on Tuesday," she began. "The pilot price stays at £79 per month. Priya, you're on messaging. Leo, the demo has to be bulletproof. George, gather customer quotes."

Priya smiled and wrote 'clarity, credibility, customers' on the whiteboard. She liked big language — big total addressable market, or TAM — because VCs often reacted to scale. She put a figure on the board: "TAM of one billion pounds across the sector," she said, deliberately loose. Leo frowned. "We can say the market is big, but don't conflate TAM with reachable market. That's projection vocabulary — it needs context, or investors will call it fluff."

George tapped the table. "I'll run the interviews. We need evidence of the problem and also of our solution working. Don't forget the limitation: our data import currently only handles CSVs with a single header row. If a customer has nested fields or multiple header rows, it fails. We built a workaround, but it's fragile and slow." He explained the limitation like he might to a user: import works for standard exports, but bespoke CRMs or nested JSON need normalization first. Helen, the finance lead, nodded. "That's support cost we haven't fully modelled. We can't pretend it doesn't exist."

They split tasks. Priya would rework the deck to make the £79 pilot prominent and to frame early-adopter economics. Maya wanted three strong customer quotes on the landing slide. George booked four interviews with prospects who had asked about alerts — the team's feature they thought would be a killer. Alerts would notify managers when a threshold was reached; George had overheard clients saying 'an early warning is priceless.'

The first interview surprised them. A regional HR director on the call praised the concept of alerts but said, "It looks good, but my team can't justify the time required to set them up. Our IT refuses unless it's plug-and-play. We need a one-click import or a connector to our payroll system." George's face tightened when the director mentioned the exact payroll provider they hadn't accounted for. "We thought the alert was the product. The customer really meant setup and integration are the product. That's different."

Later, another customer asked about support. "If we run a pilot at £79 per month, who supports us? Who cleans the data?" Helen jumped in. "Support hours scale with messy imports. If we sell 200 pilots next year at £79, that's £15,800 monthly recurring revenue (MRR). But if even 20% need hands-on setup, support costs could kill margins. We have to include that in our projections." She explained MRR plainly: monthly recurring revenue is what subscribers bring each month — multiply by 12 to get an annualised figure, but don't call that ARR unless it's stable.

Maya felt pressure. Investors liked customer evidence, but they also liked tidy metrics. "We need to show that customers will pay £79 and that support is manageable," she said. Leo suggested a conservative model: price at £79, assume 20% of customers need six hours of custom setup at £50/hour equivalent cost. Priya raised her hand. "That makes us sound small. We could say we'll move to a higher tier for enterprise integrations and keep pilots cheap."

They ended the day with a list of contradictions: promising alerts as a killer feature while admitting setup friction; saying pilots cost £79 but not having a packaged setup plan; and believing customers would adopt quickly, even when customers emphasised integration pain. The meeting closed with an agreement to prioritise honest evidence in the deck. Honesty would preserve credibility — and credibility, they all understood in the language of founders and investors, was a currency worth protecting.

6 Numbers on a Whiteboard

They met Wednesday in the small office with the window that let in a slice of grey London sky. Helen spread a spreadsheet and turned the screen so everyone could see. "Runway is six months if burn stays as is," she said, drawing a simple cash runway line. "If we hit 200 pilots at £79 in twelve months, that's £15,800 MRR, roughly £189,600 revenue a year. But that's not profit. Support, hosting, and sales will take at least 60% in year one if we don't automate imports."

Priya tapped the screen. "We should put a slide on TAM. Investors love scale. I'll say we can reach three percent of mid-market UK employers, which becomes a credible subset. That still sounds massive. We'll get attention." Maya smiled. "Make sure the slide ties to a go-to-market plan — that's the GTM. Explain channels: direct sales, a pilot programme, and a referral model."

Leo was quieter. "We need to be practical on capacity. If we promise faster onboarding, we're promising engineering time. There's the technical debt — quick fixes piled onto the system. Each patch saves a pitch but costs future months. We haven't modelled the time to build connectors. If we give a delivery date, we must be realistic. Support load scales non-linearly with integrations. It's not just headcount." He used technical-debt vocabulary and explained it as 'shortcuts that accumulate and later require extra work.'

George stood by the whiteboard and drew a funnel. "Conversion assumptions matter. If we target 2,000 leads and convert at 2% to paid pilots, that's 40 pilots. To get 200 pilots we need a different funnel or channels. Priya — how will you source those leads? Paid ads won't work on such a small price point; it's a relationship sell." Priya had a stack of notes. "Content, partnerships, and a few anchor customers who will vouch for us. Case studies convert."

They argued over claims. Priya wanted language like 'we will dominate the mid-market HR analytics space.' Leo shut it down. "Dominance is a claim about share and capability. We can't dominate when our import feature is fragile and a competitor has a similar roadmap. We need to project confidence without overstating capability." Maya tried to mediate. "Investors need to see a market vision, not just a shipping list. But they also ask for evidence. Let's show our pilot pricing and customer interest and be honest about integrations."

Helen interrupted with sensitivity to the cap table. "We have to keep dilution in mind. If we close a seed at the valuation we hope for, we can't give away too much. Our ask has to match the runway extension we need: twelve months of runway at our current burn means an ask of roughly £450k to £600k, depending on growth spend. That's investor vocabulary — 'ask' and 'valuation' — which needs to be justified by traction." She explained valuation simply: it's how much the company is worth for a given share price — a number investors use to decide what portion of the company they get for their money.

Their financial model now had two scenarios. Conservative: 40 pilots in year one, £3,160 MRR; aggressive: 200 pilots, £15,800 MRR. The difference was staffing. The conservative model needed minimal engineering hires; the aggressive model required a small integrations team and a customer success hire. That difference in spend changed the ask and the story they would tell to investors.

By the end of the day, there was agreement on one point: keep the £79 pilot price visible, but be explicit about tiering and integration charges. They would not hide the import limitation or the support cost. It was a lesson in investor vocabulary turned into practice: runway, ask, valuation, MRR, TAM, GTM — all were now terms on the whiteboard, each tied to numbers and a plan. The numbers didn't feel abstract anymore; they felt like decisions that would determine whether the company survived the next six months.

7 A Faulty Import and a Faster Competitor

Friday's rehearsal was supposed to be about narrative polishing. Instead, it became a crisis. They had a live demo planned with a mid-sized customer who had agreed to let them import real data. The goal was to show the alert working end-to-end. Priya was on the call selling the story. Leo and George were at their laptops. Maya took the lead on the demo. "This is the moment we show flow and value," she said to the team and then to the customer on the call.

They uploaded the customer's export. The dashboard spun. The import failed. The screen showed an error: "Header mismatch — multiple header rows detected." George's heart sank. He walked the customer through the workaround: open the CSV, delete the extra header, re-upload. The customer was polite — at first. "We run multiple reports automatically. I won't ask my team to clean files for a pilot. That kills the use case."

After the call, the room flickered with tension. "This is the exact friction customers mentioned in interviews," George said. "We thought it was a small problem. It's not." Maya bit her lip. "We can't show that in the pitch. Investors will hear about this and think it's basic." Leo's voice was measured but firm. "We should have built at least one connector by now, or an import wizard that normalises nested fields. We didn't because we prioritised visible features over plumbing."

While they were troubleshooting, a push notification from an industry newsfeed pinged their phones: a competitor had launched an 'auto-normalise import' feature plus alerts in one package. Their competitor's press release used words some investors liked: 'seamless integrations' and 'enterprise connectors.' The timeline stung — the competitor had shipped a connector to the payroll provider the team had just failed with.

Maya felt a flash of panic. In a heated moment that later everyone called a mistake, she told George and Leo, "Tell me how long to build that connector, and we'll promise it to Adrian on Tuesday." Adrian was the investor they had yet to impress after the earlier meeting — a seasoned angel whose feedback had been blunt. Promising something they didn't already have changed the dynamic in the room. Priya looked at Maya with concern. "We can't promise a specific feature delivery date for a critical integration unless we're sure. Investors test claims with diligence and references."

The team argued. Leo said it would take at least four weeks of focused engineering plus QA and some resources for documentation. He warned about technical debt: "If we rush, we'll build brittle code that needs refactoring. That doubles time later and increases support calls." George suggested a staged approach: ship a hosted connector that required initial configuration by engineers, then automate. That would reduce early support but take the customer through a managed onboarding experience.

Priya worried about the pitch narrative. "If Adrian hears a promise and checks with our customer references, we'll get found out if it's not done. Or worse, we'll get a conditional offer that hinges on delivery. That's dangerous." Helen agreed. "If we commit publicly to a date and miss it, our credibility — which matters more than any one feature — will take a hit. We don't have the cash to pay overtime or hire immediately to hit a deadline unless we secure the funding first."

In the quiet after the argument, Maya felt the familiar, nasty mix of ambition and fear. She had interpreted criticism from Adrian as rejection before; now she was desperate not to be rejected again. That fear made her reach for big promises. The team left the room with a new list: make the import transparent in the deck; prepare a contingency slide showing staged delivery; and call their mentor, because outside perspective was becoming the thing they needed most.

8 Mentor's Mirror and the Cost of a Promise

They rang Tom, their mentor — an ex-founder who had been through three rounds and one exit. He listened without interruption as they replayed the demo failure and Maya's promise. After a long pause he said, "Confidence is not the same as overreach. Investors want evidence you can do the work. Promising a feature you don't have is not confidence; it's a liability. They will call your bluff." Maya felt exposed. "But if we don't promise, we look small," she said. "We already lost momentum when the competitor launched."

Tom's voice was gentle but direct. "Momentum is useful, but not at the cost of credibility. There are two ways to show ambition: one is to promise deliverables without evidence; the other is to lay out a clear, verifiable plan. Say: 'We will prioritise a connector, here are wireframes, here's the team allocation, and here's the realistic timeline with milestones.' That is confidence. You don't need to say you'll be integrated on Tuesday if you can't prove the path."

He explained the investor due diligence process in plain terms: "Investors will ask for references, product demos, code access sometimes, and your unit economics. They'll call customers and ask about support responsiveness. They can and will verify claims. If you misrepresent, you risk losing more than this round — you can burn a bridge with investors who talk to each other."

Helen wrote updated financials after that call. She modelled what it would mean to hire two engineers for four weeks to build a connector: salaries and employer taxes, additional AWS hosting, QA time, and some contract work for the customer success team to help on onboarding. The burn increased substantially and shrank the runway by more than a month. Suddenly the 'ask' number for a safe runway extension grew. "If we hire before the round, we must raise an extra £150k to be comfortable," she said. "If we wait and promise a date, we risk delivering late and burning credibility."

The debate turned practical. Leo proposed a Minimum Viable Connector that could be built in phases: phase one would be a semi-manual import with a simple mapping UI; phase two would be an authenticated connector; phase three would automate scheduled syncs. Each phase had support costs. George suggested a pilot service package at £249 for customers who wanted hands-on setup — an upsell to cover initial manual integrations. Priya worried this would change the story around the £79 pilot.

They rehearsed answers to likely investor questions. "How do you handle messy data?" Helen took this: "We acknowledge imports are nonstandard. We sell the pilot at £79 for standardised imports. For custom imports, we have a service package. Our plan is to invest in a connector over three months once we close the seed. The capital raise will fund that work and acquisitions." She said it in terms investors would understand: a bridge between product limitations and financing plans.

Rehearsal exposed more weaknesses. When an imaginary investor asked, "What's your churn rate?" they had no hard number; they'd never run a long-enough pilot. When asked about competition, they knew names but not differentiation. Tom made them practice answers until each reply tied to evidence: customer quotes, demo artifacts, costed engineering plans, and payment commitments. It was tedious but stabilising.

By Friday night, the team made a pact. No more promises without a plan. Maya admitted her instinct to overpromise came from fear. She apologised to Leo and George. They agreed to trade a headline ambition for a credible roadmap framed with conservative numbers. The mentor's mirror had shown them the difference between sound leadership and performance under pressure. The next step was to incorporate that honesty into slides and practice until they could answer uncomfortable questions in plain language.

9 Final Rehearsals and the Investor Table

Pitch day felt like a summit. The meeting room at a small VC had glass walls and a long wooden table. Priya wore a navy blazer and a folder of printouts. Maya opened with the company's story — what they did, why it mattered, and how customers reacted. The £79 monthly pilot was on slide two, clearly labelled. Helen, seated with the laptop showing the financial model, watched the investors' faces. "We ask for £550k for 12 months of runway and to build priority integrations," she said when it came to the ask. She explained the use of proceeds: engineering hires, customer success, and marketing for channel partnerships.

The investors were three people: a partner who was direct, an associate who took notes, and a product-focussed angel who asked technical questions. The partner started with the usual: "Walk me through a pilot — what does £79 get a customer?" Maya answered with a calm, practiced cadence: "It gives access to core alerts, standard imports, and a two-week onboarding. For bespoke integrations there is a paid setup package. We highlight this up front so customers understand what the pilot covers and we protect unit economics." She mentioned the staged connector plan briefly and offered a concrete timeline if they funded the work.

The product-focused angel leaned forward. "How will you handle the data import variance?" George took that one. He explained the CSV limitation, showed anonymised screenshots from the failed demo and the error message, and then presented the phased connector roadmap. He was candid about the customer evidence: quotes that praised alerts but asked for a simpler import. The investors appreciated the honesty. The associate scribbled numbers on a notepad.

The partner asked about competitive risk. Priya answered with the reframed narrative: the competitor had launched a similar feature, but their integration required a paid enterprise contract and no onboarding service. Adrian's external reviewer arrived two weeks later. The reviewer audited the mapping tests, the code, and the training data pipeline. She found the approach sensible and the results reproducible. Adrian released the second tranche of the convertible funds after the reviewer signed off on the milestone and after he verified the signed pilot agreement with Helen.

The pitch that changed everything wasn't a single moment of magic. It was the decision to tell the truth, to listen to customers, and to trade quick theatrical wins for slow, durable trust. And that, in a way, made the startup feel steadier, more human and more likely to last.

20 Chapter 12: Morning Light

Those terms felt fair. Maya insisted on them being conservative. She had learned her lesson about mixing ambition with fact. "We will label everything," she told Leo and Priya. Estimate: in past pilots we reduced out-of-stocks by around 25 percent on covered SKUs within the pilot period. Ambition: with full roll-out we hope to reduce out-of-stocks by 40 percent and increase sales by five to eight percent. Carla's tone softened. "Okay. Two more things. First: we need a clause about data privacy. Second: we need to know what the biggest risk is, from your side." Maya had expected the privacy question; she had a signed policy ready. The risk question felt like an invitation to be frank.

"The biggest realistic risk is integration with messy, real-world store environments," Maya said. But it is the main risk on our side—accuracy in non-ideal conditions."
