0 Sunday at My Parents' House

Michael Turner stood by the window of his small flat and looked down at the street. A blue bus stopped outside the bakery. Two people got on, carrying paper bags. It was Sunday morning, but the town was already awake.

Michael was twenty-six and lived alone in a small city flat. During the week, he worked at a marketing company. He made pictures and short texts for shops. He liked the creative part of his job. He did not like the long meetings or the many messages on his phone.

On the table lay a leaflet for a design course. It was for people who wanted to learn more about drawing and computer design. Michael had looked at it many times. He had not called the school yet. The course cost money, and he was not sure he could fit it around his work.

His phone rang. It was his mother, Helen. “Are you still coming for dinner?” she asked.

“Yes,” said Michael. “I’m leaving soon.”

“Good. Your father says the trains are slow today. You could take the bus.”

Michael looked at the time. “I’ll check.”

After the call, he put the leaflet in his bag. He packed a book, a clean shirt, and the small box of biscuits he had bought for his parents. Then he locked the flat and walked to the bus stop.

The bus ride took forty minutes. It went past the library, the old stone church, and a row of shops with bright signs. Michael watched the streets pass. He thought about work. On Friday, his manager had asked him to help with a new project. It sounded interesting, but the team was already busy.

Then he thought about his family. Sunday dinner at his parents’ house was a habit. It was a good habit, he told himself. Still, he knew the questions that often came with it. How was work? Was he seeing anyone? Had he thought about buying a home?

He did not mind that his family cared. He just did not always know what to say. Sometimes he gave a short answer. Sometimes he made a joke. Sometimes he asked about the food.

The bus turned into a quiet street. His parents’ house stood near a small park. It had a red front door and a narrow garden. Michael had lived there until he left for college. A little stone bird still sat beside the path. He had painted it green when he was ten. The green paint was now almost gone.

He got off the bus and walked to the gate. From the house came the warm smell of bread and roasted vegetables. Michael smiled. Whatever questions waited inside, lunch would be good.

1 The Red Front Door

Helen opened the door before Michael could knock. She wore a yellow apron and had flour on one sleeve.

“You’re here!” she said, and hugged him. “Come in. It’s cold outside.”

“It’s not that cold,” Michael said.

“Your hands are cold.” She took his coat anyway and hung it beside the door.

Robert came from the kitchen. He was tall, with grey hair and a neat blue shirt. He had worked as an engineer for many years. Even at home, he liked things to be in their right place.

“The bus was on time?” Robert asked.

“Almost,” said Michael. “There was a long line at the bakery.”

“A long line for bread?” Robert said. “People have too much time on Sunday.”

Helen gave him a look. “You bought bread there last week.”

Michael followed them into the kitchen. On the table were plates, glasses, and a bowl of apples. A small wooden horse stood on the shelf. It had a broken ear. Michael had made it in school and had painted it brown.

“You still have that?” he asked.

“Of course,” said Helen. “It keeps the shelf from looking empty.”

Robert laughed. “It also keeps the other things safe. No one goes near it.”

Michael put the biscuits on the table. Helen thanked him and set them beside the fruit.

“Laura and Peter are on their way,” she said. “Emma wants to show you a picture.”

“Is she still drawing cats?” Michael asked.

“Mostly,” said Helen. “She says they are easy to draw because they sit still.”

They went to the living room. The room looked much as Michael remembered it. There was a green sofa, a low table, and a large clock that made a soft tick each second. On the wall were family photos. In one, Michael had a bowl on his head. In another, Laura held a very small rabbit.

“I remember that rabbit,” Michael said. “It ate my school paper.”

“Only the corner,” said Robert. “You said it was your best paper, but you had not started it yet.”

“That is not the point,” Michael said, smiling.

Helen sat beside him. “How is work?” she asked.

“Busy,” Michael said. “We have a new shop project.”

“That sounds good,” said Robert. “A new project can be a chance to do well.”

Michael heard the start of a longer talk, but Helen called from the kitchen. She had left a dish in the oven. Robert went to help her, though he said he was only checking the clock.

A few minutes later, the doorbell rang. Emma’s voice came from the hall. “Grandma! We’re here!”

Michael stood up. The house filled with coats, bags, and happy voices. For a moment, he forgot the questions he had expected. Emma ran into the room with a sheet of paper in her hand.

2 Emma's Cat

Emma was five years old. She had dark hair held back with a red clip. She ran to Michael and gave him the picture.

“Look,” she said. “This is a cat in a hat.”

The cat had a round head, a long tail, and a large blue hat. One leg was much longer than the other.

“That is a very good hat,” said Michael.

“It is for rain,” Emma explained. “Cats don’t like wet hats.”

“I think cats don’t like any hats,” said Peter as he came into the room.

“This cat does,” Emma said.

Laura laughed and took off her coat. She was Michael’s older sister. She worked at a school office and often spoke quickly, as if she had just remembered something important. Peter worked in a small bookshop. He was quiet at first, but he had a dry sense of humour.

“Did Emma tell you the name of the cat?” Laura asked.

“No,” said Michael.

“It’s called Sunday,” Emma said. “Because it only works one day a week.”

“That sounds like a good job,” said Michael. “I would like to work one day a week.”

“You can work one day and spend the other six drawing cats,” said Peter.

Emma looked at him. “I don’t draw cats every day.”

“Only on school days,” Laura said.

They all laughed. Emma went to the table to show her picture to Helen. Peter helped Robert carry a dish into the dining room. Laura sat beside Michael on the sofa.

“How are things?” she asked.

“Fine,” he said. “Busy.”

“That is what you said last month.”

Michael looked at her. Laura’s voice was kind, but she was watching him closely.

“The work is busy,” he said. “I’m all right.”

“Do you still want to take that design course?”

He was surprised she remembered. “I have the paper at home. I haven’t called them.”

“Why not?”

“I don’t know yet. It costs money. And I work late sometimes.”

Laura nodded. “That makes sense.” She did not tell him what to do. Michael felt glad that she had asked.

From the dining room, Robert called, “The food is ready. Please come before Helen changes the menu again.”

“I heard that,” Helen called back.

They found their places at the table. Michael sat between Emma and Robert. Helen brought out a bowl of roasted potatoes and a dish of carrots. The chicken smelled rich and warm.

“I made more potatoes,” Helen said. “Some people here eat them very quickly.”

Robert raised a hand. “I accept this charge.”

Peter passed the bread. Emma asked if she could have two pieces. Helen said yes, but only if she ate some carrots too.

“I like carrots,” Emma said.

“Good,” said Helen.

“I like them more when they are not on my plate,” Emma added.

Robert covered his smile with his glass. The first minutes of dinner were easy. They talked about Emma’s picture, Peter’s bookshop, and a dog that had chased a ball into the park pond. Michael ate and listened. He knew the harder questions might come, but he did not feel in a hurry to answer them.

3 Questions at the Table

Robert asked about the weather outside. Peter said it had rained while they walked from the car. Helen said the garden needed rain, but not so much rain that the path became mud.

“The plants are growing well,” Robert said. “The small apple tree has new leaves.”

“Can I pick apples soon?” Emma asked.

“Not yet,” said Robert. “First the tree needs flowers. Then it needs time.”

Emma looked at the apple bowl. “Can I pick one from there?”

“Yes,” said Helen. “Those are ready.”

Emma took an apple and held it in both hands. The talk moved to the park, then to the new swing there. Michael joined in. He said he had walked past it on Friday and had seen three children trying to sit on it at once.

“That is how children test a swing,” Laura said. “They want to know how much trouble it can hold.”

Robert passed Michael the potatoes. “How is the new project at work?”

“It’s for a shop that sells kitchen things,” Michael said. “We’re making pictures for its new website.”

“That sounds useful,” said Robert. “Will it help you get a better job?”

Michael put a potato on his plate. “Maybe. I don’t know.”

“You are good at design,” Robert said. “You should look for a job with more pay and more room to grow. You cannot stay in the same place forever.”

Michael felt his shoulders tighten. “I haven’t been there forever. It’s been two years.”

“I know,” said Robert. “I only mean that you should think ahead.”

Helen looked from one to the other. “Let him eat,” she said. “The food is not a test.”

Robert gave a small nod. “That’s fair.” Michael was glad his mother had spoken, though he was not sure he wanted anyone to speak for him.

Helen cut another piece of bread. “Are you seeing anyone these days?” she asked.

Michael stared at the bread. “No, Mum.”

“There was a woman you met at work,” Helen said. “You told me about her at Christmas.”

“We were only talking,” Michael said. “And it was months ago.”

“All right,” said Helen. “I was only asking.”

Laura passed Emma a napkin. “I have a question too. Have you thought about buying a home? Prices are high, but it can be good to start planning.”

Michael let out a short breath. “I don’t know. I’m not ready to plan a house or a relationship today.”

For a moment, the room was quiet. Robert put down his fork.

“We ask because we care,” he said. “But I can see how it feels like a lot at once.”

“It does,” Michael said. “I know you’re trying to help. I just need time to work out what I want. The design course is one thing I’m thinking about.”

Helen nodded. “Thank you for telling us.” Laura said, “You don’t have to decide today.”

Emma held up her apple. “I know what I want. I want another apple.”

The family laughed. Helen cut the apple into pieces, and Michael went back to his dinner. The questions had not all gone away, but he had said a little more than usual. For now, that felt like enough.

4 The Dinner Table

The plates were already on the table. Robert sat at one end, and Helen sat at the other. Michael sat between Laura and Peter. The roast potatoes were hot, and a bowl of green beans stood near the bread.

“Who wants more?” Helen asked.

“I do,” said Peter. “I have had a difficult day.”

Laura looked at him. “You watched football on the sofa.”

“Exactly,” Peter said. “It was hard to sit still for so long.”

Everyone laughed, and Helen put more potatoes on his plate. For a few minutes, the family talked about the town, the weather, and the small dog that had barked at Robert in the street.

Then Robert looked at Michael. “How is work?”

Michael moved a green bean around his plate. “Fine. Busy.”

“And your salary?” Robert asked. “Is it enough for the flat?”

Michael felt his shoulders grow tight. “It pays the rent. It pays the bills.”

“That is important,” Robert said. “A regular salary gives you peace.”

“It does,” Michael said, though he did not feel peaceful. He had worked at the same office for four years. He knew the forms, the meetings, and the broken coffee machine. He did not know if he wanted to do it for four more years.

Helen passed him the bread. “Are you happy there?” she asked.

Robert sighed. “Not everyone can be happy at work every day.”

“I did not say every day,” Helen replied. “I want Michael to enjoy his life, and to have someone to share it with.”

Michael looked down. “Mum, I am all right on my own.”

“I know,” she said gently. “I am not trying to choose a partner for you.”

Laura reached for her glass. “People always ask me when I will have a baby. Then they ask why I have not bought a bigger house. It never ends.”

Her smile was light, but Michael heard something tired in her voice.

“You have a nice house,” Robert said.

“We do,” Laura answered. “But sometimes I think we chose it because everyone said it was the sensible thing to do.”

Peter lifted his fork. “I chose my kitchen chair because it was on sale. Now it is the boss of the house. It makes a noise when I sit down.”

“Your chair is not a life plan,” Laura said.

“That is what it wants us to think,” Peter replied.

The laughter returned, but Michael still felt the question sitting beside him. Robert believed a steady job was a good life. Helen wanted him to be happy and loved. Laura seemed to have everything, but she had her own worries. Peter made jokes whenever the room grew quiet.

Michael took a bite of potato. It was soft and warm. He wanted to tell them about the evening design course he had found online. Instead, he said, “Work is fine.”

Robert nodded. “Good. A promotion may come if you stay focused.”

Michael looked at his plate again. He wondered if being sensible was the same as being satisfied.

5 After Dinner

After dinner, Helen brought tea into the living room. Robert carried the plates to the kitchen, although he said he was only helping because the table was in his way.

Peter sat in the biggest chair. “This is my chair now,” he said.

“You can have it,” Laura said. “It is too small for you.”

Peter looked at the chair arms. “It is not small. I am growing.”

“At your age?” asked Robert.

“It is never too late,” Peter said.

Michael sat on the sofa beside Helen. The room looked much as it had when he was a child. There was a family photo on the shelf, with Michael at twelve in a football shirt and Robert behind him, smiling. Michael remembered the day. He had scored one goal and then kicked the ball into a garden.

“You were very proud of that shirt,” Helen said, following his eyes.

“I wore it every Saturday,” Michael said.

“Even when it was too small,” Laura added. “You said it made you run faster.”

“It did,” Michael said. “Until the sleeves hurt my arms.”

They laughed. Then Robert sat down across from him. “I was thinking about what we said at dinner,” he began. “There may be a chance for you to move up at work. More responsibility, better pay.”

“That sounds good,” Helen said. “If it is what he wants.”

Robert looked at her. “Of course. But a good chance should not be thrown away.”

Michael pressed his hands together. “I know. I just don’t know if I want that job.”

The room grew quiet.

Laura leaned forward. “What do you want instead?”

Michael took a breath. He had planned to say nothing. But the question was gentle, and nobody had laughed at him.

“I’ve been looking at an evening design course,” he said. “It is about drawing and making simple designs for websites and shops.”

“Design?” Robert asked.

“I used to like art at school,” Michael said. “And I still make things on my computer sometimes. I haven’t told anyone because I thought it might sound silly.”

“Why would it sound silly?” Helen asked.

Robert rubbed his chin. “You studied business. You have a steady job. Starting again could take time and money.”

“I know,” Michael said. “That’s why I haven’t done anything yet.”

Peter raised his tea cup. “I once tried to make a poster for my football team. It looked like a chicken had sat on the words.”

“That is not helpful,” Laura said.

“It is a very honest review,” Peter replied.

Michael smiled, but his worry stayed. Robert was not angry. Still, he could hear what his father feared: wasted time, less money, a life that did not go to plan. He did not yet know how to answer that fear. For now, he was glad he had said the course aloud.

6 A Call from Emma

Michael's phone buzzed in his pocket. He checked the screen. Emma had sent a message: Are you still at your parents' house? I found your old drawing book.

He smiled. “Emma found my old art book.”

“The one with the terrible pictures?” Peter asked.

“You have seen them?” Michael said.

“I was there when you drew a horse with six legs.”

“It was a fast horse,” Michael said.

Helen laughed. “You used to draw every day.”

Michael typed back: I am here. Send me a picture.

Emma sent a photo a moment later. The book lay open on a table. On the page was a picture of a small café with a red door and bright blue windows. The lines were uneven, but the place looked cheerful.

“I remember this,” Michael said. “I wanted to open a café.”

“You wanted to open a zoo, too,” Laura said. “You drew a giraffe in the garden.”

“The garden was very large in my picture,” he said.

“It was your best work,” Peter said. “The giraffe looked almost like a giraffe.”

Michael sent Emma a message asking if he could call. She replied at once. He stepped into the hall, where it was quieter, and phoned her.

“Hello,” Emma said. “I hope I did not interrupt dinner.”

“You didn’t. We finished eating. We’re talking about work and the future.”

“That sounds like a family evening.”

Michael leaned against the wall. “I told them about the design course.”

“Good,” Emma said. “How did it feel?”

“A bit like jumping into a cold lake.”

“And did you jump?”

“Only a little.”

Emma laughed. “What did you want to say before you told them?”

Michael looked at the photo of the café on his screen. “I wanted to say that I’m tired of choosing the safe thing just because it is safe. But I also don’t want to make a stupid choice.”

“That makes sense,” Emma said. “You don’t have to decide your whole life tonight. What do you want to try first?”

He was quiet for a moment. No one had asked him that before. People asked what he earned, or what job he might get next. Emma wanted to know what he wanted.

“I could go to the open evening for the course,” he said. “Then I could see what it is really like.”

“That sounds like a good first step.”

“It still feels scary.”

“You can be scared and still go,” Emma said. “You used to draw those pictures even when you thought they were bad.”

Michael looked again at the little café. “They were bad.”

“Some were,” Emma said. “But you kept drawing.”

He thanked her. When he returned to the living room, Robert was asking Peter about his noisy chair. The room felt ordinary and warm. Michael still had no clear answer about his future, but he had one small thing to do next.

7 One Small Step

Michael put his phone on the table. “Emma says the course has an open evening next week,” he said. “I might go and find out more.”

Robert looked at him carefully. “That is different from leaving your job tomorrow.”

“Yes,” Michael said. “I’m not planning to leave tomorrow.”

“Good,” Robert said. “I don’t want you to take a big risk without knowing the cost.”

Helen set down her tea. “And I don’t want you to stay unhappy because you are afraid to try.”

Robert looked at her, then back at Michael. “I want you to be careful. That is not the same as saying you cannot do it.”

Michael let out a slow breath. He had heard his father say careful many times. He had not often heard the rest.

“I understand,” Michael said. “I’ll check the price and the hours. I’ll keep my job while I look into it.”

“That sounds sensible,” Robert replied.

“It also sounds like you,” Laura said. “You can look before you leap.”

Peter raised one finger. “I once looked before I leapt from a low wall. I still fell.”

“That is because you were looking at a sandwich,” Laura said.

Peter nodded. “It was a very good sandwich.”

Helen brought out a plate of small cakes. They had been in the kitchen all evening, waiting for the right moment. Peter took one and checked it from every side.

“It is not a sandwich,” Laura said.

“I know. I’m learning,” he replied.

The family laughed again. Michael took a cake and sat back. He noticed that the pressure in his chest had eased a little. Nothing had changed about his job, his flat, or his future. Robert still cared about steady work. Helen still wanted him to be happy. Laura still carried questions of her own.

But Michael had said what he wanted to try, and his family had listened. They did not all agree in the same way, yet they could talk without turning the evening into a fight.

“What about you?” he asked Laura. “Do you still think about changing things at home?”

Laura looked at her hands. “Sometimes. I love my house and my family. But I want people to stop acting as if I must be grateful every minute. I can love my life and still want more time for myself.”

Helen reached across and touched her arm. “That is fair.”

Robert nodded slowly. “I don’t always know what people expect from me, either.”

“You could start by sitting in Peter’s chair,” Michael said.

Robert looked at the chair. “I have no wish to make it louder.”

“It has been waiting for you,” Peter said.

They finished the cakes and tea. Later, Michael helped Helen carry the cups to the kitchen. Robert dried them, and Peter stood nearby giving advice without touching anything.

Before Michael left, Robert said, “Tell me when you know the price of the course. We can talk about it.”

“I will,” Michael said.

The next week still seemed far away. He was not suddenly sure of himself. But on the way home, he opened Emma’s photo again and looked at the small café with its red door. Then he wrote down the date of the open evening so he would not forget.

8 A Question from Robert

The plates were still on the table. Peter had gone to the kitchen to find more tea, and Emma was showing Laura a picture on her phone. Michael sat back in his chair. He felt full and sleepy. The room was warm, and the clock above the door ticked loudly.

Robert put down his cup. “Michael, can I ask you something?”

Michael looked up. “Of course.”

“Why have you not applied for the promotion at work?” Robert asked. “You told us about it some time ago.”

Michael's shoulders became tight. “I haven't decided what to do.”

“It sounds like a good chance,” Robert said. “You are good at your job. I don't understand why you have not even applied.”

“Perhaps he is still thinking,” Helen said gently.

Robert shook his head. “I think he is afraid of responsibility. A bigger job means more responsibility, yes, but he can do it.”

Michael's face grew hot. “You don't know what I can do.”

Laura stopped looking at Emma's phone. “Robert may be right that you can do it,” she said. “But you still need a plan. You can't wait forever and hope the answer comes to you.”

Michael turned to her. “You don't understand.”

Laura's mouth closed. For a moment, no one spoke.

“Michael,” Helen said, “let's not make this harder than it needs to be.”

“I'm not trying to make it hard,” Michael said. He pushed his chair back. “I just don't want this job. I don't want to spend every evening answering messages and worrying about work.”

“Then say that,” Robert replied. “But you have been saying you don't know. That is different.”

Michael stood up. “I need some air.”

He walked through the kitchen and opened the back door. The garden was small, with a narrow path and two old chairs. A clean towel hung over the garden fence. Peter had put it there to dry after washing the kitchen floor.

Michael took a slow breath. He could still hear the family inside. A chair moved. Someone laughed softly at something Emma said. He wanted to go back, but he did not want to meet their eyes yet.

He sat on one of the chairs. It was cool from the shade. His thoughts moved around without finding a clear answer. He knew Laura wanted to help. He knew Robert wanted him to be safe and successful. But their words made him feel like a boy who had done something wrong.

The back door opened. Helen came out with two cups of tea. She gave one to Michael and sat beside him.

“You don't have to answer them right now,” she said.

Michael held the warm cup in both hands. “I know. I just wish they would stop telling me what I should want.”

Helen looked at him for a moment. “That is fair,” she said. “But they may not know what you want because you have not told them clearly.”

Michael looked down at his tea. “I don't know how to tell them without starting another argument.”

“Start with what is true for you,” Helen said. “Not with what you think they want to hear.”

He gave a short laugh. “That sounds simple.”

“It is simple,” Helen said. “It isn't always easy.”

Inside, Robert called, “Helen, do you want more tea?”

“No, thank you,” she called back. Then she turned to Michael. “Would you like me to stay?”

“Maybe for a minute.”

Helen stayed. She did not ask another question. Michael drank his tea, while the sound of the clock came through the open door.

After a while, Michael said, “I don't want the promotion. At least, I don't want the job as it is now. But I don't know what I want instead.”

Helen nodded. “That is more honest than saying you don't know about the promotion.”

He watched a small bird hop near the flower bed. “I still don't want to say it in there.”

“You don't have to be ready all at once,” Helen said. “But you can go back when you are ready to listen and speak.”

Michael looked towards the house. He was not ready yet. Still, the tight feeling in his chest had eased a little.

“I'll go back soon,” he said.

“All right,” said Helen. “I'll tell them you need a few more minutes.”

She stood and went inside. Michael stayed in the garden, holding the empty cup. He knew a few minutes would not fix everything. But he had said one true thing aloud, and that felt like a small start.

9 Tea in the Garden

Helen came back a few minutes later. She had no tea this time. She sat in the other garden chair and pulled her cardigan around her.

“Your father is washing cups,” she said. “He is washing the same cup for a long time.”

Michael almost smiled. “That sounds like Dad.”

“He is worried,” Helen said. “Worry makes him talk too much.”

Michael looked at the wet towel on the fence. “He said I was afraid of responsibility.”

“He should not have said it like that,” Helen replied. “But he may be trying to ask if you are happy.”

“He could just ask.”

“Yes. He could.” Helen paused. “Your father is not very good at asking a question and waiting for the answer.”

Michael let out a small breath. “I know.”

Helen rested her hands in her lap. “When I was young, I wanted to work in a bookshop. I loved books, and I liked talking to people. But then my mother became ill. I stayed near home and found work at the bakery.”

“You never told me that,” Michael said.

“It was a long time ago. I don't regret helping her. But there were years when I wondered what might have happened if I had tried the bookshop.”

Michael looked at his mother. “Were you unhappy?”

“Some days, yes. Most days, no. Life is not one thing or the other.” Helen smiled. “The bakery helped us pay for our first flat. It also gave me funny stories. One customer bought the same small cake every Friday and said it was for his wife. One day his wife came in and told me he ate it on the bus.”

Michael laughed. “Did you tell him?”

“No. I let him keep his secret.”

They sat quietly for a moment. From inside came Peter's voice. He was explaining that the cups were clean, even though no one had asked.

“I have a habit of agreeing,” Michael said. “At work, with Laura, with you and Dad. I say yes so people won't be unhappy. Then I feel trapped.”

Helen did not answer at once. “That sounds tiring.”

“It is. And now I don't know which things are really mine. I have not applied for the promotion because I don't want it. But I also don't have another plan.”

“Not having a plan today does not mean you must accept a plan someone else makes for you,” Helen said.

Michael rubbed his thumb over the edge of his cup. “I don't want to disappoint everyone.”

“You cannot make every choice so that nobody is disappointed,” she said. “You can be kind and still choose for yourself.”

Michael looked through the kitchen window. Laura was helping Emma put the phone away. Robert stood at the sink, holding a dish towel. He was not washing anything now.

“I want them to understand,” Michael said. “But I don't want to blame them for caring.”

“Then tell them both things,” Helen replied. “Tell them you know they care, and tell them what their words do to you.”

Michael nodded, but his stomach still felt tight. “What if Dad gets angry again?”

“He might,” Helen said honestly. “And you might feel angry too. That does not mean you have to stop talking.”

Michael leaned back. The old chair made a small cracking sound.

Helen glanced at it. “That chair has been making that noise for years.”

“Maybe it needs a promotion,” Michael said.

Helen laughed. “It would need to learn how to stay still first.”

For a short time, they talked about easy things. Helen asked about Michael's journey home. He told her the bus had been late, and a man had carried a large plant onto it. The plant had taken the seat beside him. Helen said it sounded like a quiet passenger.

The garden did not feel like a place to hide now. It felt like a place to think. Michael knew that his worries would still be there when he went inside. He also knew that he did not have to solve his whole life in one afternoon.

“I think I can go back,” he said.

Helen stood first. “Good. I will come with you.”

“I can walk in alone.” Michael took a breath. “But will you sit near me?”

“Of course.”

They went through the back door together. Robert had put the cups away. Laura was standing by the table, and Emma was looking for her coat.

Robert glanced at Michael. “Are you all right?”

Michael felt the first answer rise to his mouth: Fine. He stopped it there.

“I'm calmer,” he said. “But I want to talk about what you asked me.”

Robert gave a small nod. “All right.”

Michael sat down again. He was not sure what would come next, but he was no longer trying to find a quick answer that would please everyone.

10 Robert's Story

Michael sat at the table. Helen took the chair beside him. Robert sat opposite, with his hands flat on the table. Laura and Emma stayed in the room. Peter came in with a plate of biscuits.

“I found these in the cupboard,” he said. “They are a little soft, but they are still biscuits.”

“Thank you, Peter,” Helen said.

Peter put the plate down and sat near Emma. He picked up a biscuit and bent it. It broke in two.

“Very soft,” he said.

Michael looked at Robert. “I want to explain what I meant before.”

Robert nodded. His face was serious, but he did not speak.

“I don't want the promotion as it is,” Michael said. “The new person has to answer calls at night. I see how tired she is. I don't want that kind of work.”

“I didn't know about the night calls,” Robert said.

“I haven't told you. I wasn't sure how to say it.”

Laura moved her cup a little. “You could have told me too,” she said. “I thought you were waiting because you were afraid to try.”

Michael looked at her. “I know. But when you said I needed a plan, I heard that I was doing everything wrong.”

Laura's face softened. “I didn't mean that. I want you to feel good about what you do. I also get worried when you say you don't know, and then we don't talk about it again.”

“I understand,” Michael said. “I don't want you to carry that worry for me.”

Robert's fingers moved on the table. “I thought the promotion would make your life easier later. Better pay. A better place to live, perhaps.”

“Maybe,” Michael said. “But I don't want to choose only for the money. And I don't know if I want to stay in my job forever.”

Robert looked down at his hands. For a few seconds, the only sound was Peter carefully breaking another biscuit.

“I said you were afraid of responsibility,” Robert said at last. “That was not fair. I was making a guess, and I said it like I knew.”

Michael's shoulders loosened a little. “Thank you.”

“I worry that you will miss good chances,” Robert continued. “When I was your age, I said no to a job in another town. Your mother and I had just moved into our flat. I thought I had to stay close to help my father. I don't know if saying no was the right choice.”

Helen looked at him. “You helped your father when he needed you.”

“Yes,” Robert said. “But sometimes I still wonder. I don't want you to look back and wish you had tried.”

Michael understood more now. Robert's question had come from fear, not from a wish to control him. But it had still hurt.

“I know you want me to be safe,” Michael said. “But I need to find out what I want, even if the answer is not the job you would choose.”

Robert took a slow breath. “I can understand that,” he said. “I may not always like your choice. That is not the same as not loving you.”

The words were simple, but Michael felt them settle inside him.

“I love you too,” he said. “I don't want to fight every time we talk about work.”

“Nor do I,” Robert replied.

Laura leaned forward. “I don't want to choose for you either,” she said. “I want us to talk before you feel alone with it.”

Michael turned to her. “I need to tell you when I'm not ready to agree. I often say yes because I don't want a difficult talk.”

“I would rather hear a difficult truth than a quick yes,” Laura said.

Peter lifted his hand. “I would also like a difficult truth about these biscuits.”

Emma laughed. “They are old, Peter.”

“Thank you,” he said. “Now I can make a clear choice.” He put the biscuit down.

The small laugh helped the room feel less tight. Michael saw that no one was asking him to choose his whole future right there. Still, he had more to say.

“I don't have a new plan yet,” he said. “I can look at other jobs. I can also ask my manager what the promotion is really like. But I don't want to promise that I will apply.”

Robert nodded slowly. “That seems fair.”

“And I don't want every Sunday to become a meeting about my job,” Michael added.

“That seems fair too,” said Helen.

Robert rubbed his chin. “Can I ask about it next month?”

Michael thought about it. His first wish was to say no. His second was to say yes and hope Robert forgot. He tried a different answer.

“You can ask,” he said. “But first, please ask how I feel. Don't tell me what I fear.”

Robert gave a small, careful smile. “I will try.”

“I will try to answer,” Michael said.

Laura reached across the table and touched his hand. She did not say that everything was solved. Michael was glad. It was not solved. But they had spoken more clearly than before.

Outside, a light rain began to tap at the window. Peter looked at the biscuits again.

“Perhaps the rain will make them softer,” he said.

“Please don't test that,” Helen replied.

Michael laughed with the others. He still had questions about work, and he still felt some fear when he thought about making a choice. But now he knew he could speak before he had every answer.

11 A More Honest Answer

The rain grew stronger. Robert went to the front room and turned on a lamp. Helen collected the cups, while Peter put the plate of soft biscuits on a high shelf.

“For another day,” he said.

“That may be best,” Emma replied.

Laura helped Michael carry the cups to the kitchen. For a moment, they stood together by the sink. The others were in the next room, talking about the rain and the bus home.

Laura turned on the tap. “I'm sorry I said you needed a plan like that,” she said. “I was worried, but I made it sound like a rule.”

Michael passed her a cup. “I know you were trying to help. I should have said that I felt pushed instead of saying you didn't understand.”

“You did feel pushed,” Laura said. “You can tell me that.”

He dried his hands on a towel. “I want to make my own choices. But I also want you beside me. I don't always know how to do both.”

Laura leaned against the counter. “We can practise. We don't have to agree about every choice.”

Michael smiled a little. “That sounds less scary than agreeing about everything.”

“It is also more honest,” she said.

They returned to the front room. Helen was looking through a drawer for an umbrella. She found two and opened one inside by mistake. The dark umbrella spread over her head, and Peter stepped back.

“It works,” Peter said. “Now we know.”

“And now we know we should not open umbrellas indoors,” Robert said.

“That old idea is not true,” said Helen. “But it makes people move away, which can be useful.”

Everyone laughed. Michael felt the room grow easy again, though he knew their talk had not ended forever.

Robert stood near the door. “The rain may stop soon. You can take my umbrella if you like.”

“We can wait a little,” Michael said. He was going to add, “Whatever you think.” He caught the words before they came out.

“Actually, I would like to wait ten minutes,” he said. “Then we'll decide.”

Robert looked pleased, not angry. “Good. Ten minutes it is.”

They sat down again. Emma told them about a funny sign she had seen at the bus stop. It said, “Please do not leave your bags alone.” Peter asked if bags ever wanted to be alone. Emma said she did not know, but the sign made her feel sorry for them.

Michael listened. He did not have to make every quiet moment into a chance to explain himself. After a while, Robert asked what Michael had said about speaking to his manager.

“I can tell you,” Michael said. “But not as a promise to apply.”

“Understood,” Robert replied.

Michael took a breath and began to explain.

“I can ask my manager about the hours and the evening calls,” Michael said. “I can also ask if there are other jobs in the company. I want to know more before I choose anything.”

“That sounds sensible,” Laura said. “Would you like me to help you write down questions?”

Michael thought about it. “Yes, but I want to write the first list myself.”

“All right,” said Laura. “You can show it to me when you are ready.”

It was a small answer, but it felt important. Michael had asked for help without giving away the choice.

Helen brought the umbrellas to the front door. The rain had become light. Robert looked outside and said it might stop in another five minutes. Peter said it might start again in six.

“Peter is our weather expert,” Helen said.

“I have no training,” Peter replied. “But I have been right once.”

“When?” Emma asked.

“I don't remember,” Peter said. “But I am sure it happened.”

Michael and Laura put on their coats. Robert held out an umbrella, but Michael did not take it at once.

“We can use the small one between us,” he said. “If that is all right with you.”

“It is,” Laura said.

Robert lowered his hand. “Drive carefully,” he said.

“We will,” Michael replied. Then he looked at his father. “Thanks for listening, Dad.”

Robert nodded. “Thanks for telling me.”

Helen hugged Michael. She held him a little longer than usual. “You don't have to know everything yet,” she said softly.

“I know,” he answered. This time, he meant it.

At the door, Emma asked if they would come again next Sunday. Michael looked at Laura. They would check their week and decide, he thought. He did not need to say yes just to make everyone happy.

“We will talk about it,” he said. “I would like to come, but I need to check our plans first.”

“That is a real answer,” Emma said.

“It is,” Michael replied.

Outside, the pavement shone with rain. Michael and Laura walked close beneath the small umbrella. It was not large enough for both of them. Laura's sleeve got wet, and Michael's shoulder did too.

“We need a bigger umbrella,” Laura said.

“Or we need to walk closer.”

They moved nearer. Michael knew that one honest afternoon would not change every old habit. He might still agree too quickly. He might still feel pressure when someone asked him to choose.

But he had spoken clearly once, and he had not lost his family or Laura. The next step could be small too. For now, he was glad to walk home beside her, without pretending to know the whole way.

12 A Plan on the Table

The cups were still on the table after tea. Robert sat with his hands around his cup. Helen was folding a clean tea towel. Laura leaned against the kitchen counter, and Peter had stopped making jokes.

Michael looked at his family. He had spoken about his worries before, but he had not yet said what he wanted to do. His stomach felt tight.

“I have a plan,” he said. “It is not a big change. I want to keep my job at the office. I also want to take an evening course in design.”

“A course?” Robert asked.

“Yes. It costs money, but I have looked for one I can afford. The lessons are two evenings a week. I can keep working during the day.”

Helen put down the tea towel. “And what happens after the course?”

“I don’t know yet. I want to study for six months and then look at how things are going. I can decide what to do next after that.”

Robert’s face did not change much. “You will have less free time.”

“I know. I may be tired. I have thought about that.” Michael took a breath. “I have also made a budget. I can pay for the course and still save a little each month.”

Peter lifted his cup. “That is more planning than I did before I bought my old motorbike.”

“You bought it because it was red,” Laura said.

“It was a very good red,” Peter replied. Then he looked at Michael. “Sorry. I was going to make a joke. But I think you have done the right thing by checking the cost first.”

Robert tapped one finger on the table. “What if the course is not useful? What if the lessons are too hard after work?”

Michael felt himself wanting to say that it would all be fine. He stopped. “Those things could happen. I don’t know if the course will lead to a different job. I’m choosing it because I want to learn, not because it promises me a new life.”

Robert looked at him for a moment. “That is an honest answer,” he said. “I am not sure it is a good plan yet.”

“I understand,” Michael said. It was not the support he had hoped for, but he did not feel the need to defend every part of his idea.

Helen sat beside him. “I worry because I want you to be safe. But you have checked the money, and you are keeping your job. That helps me.”

“I don’t want you to worry,” Michael said.

“I know,” she replied. “But I may worry anyway.”

Laura came to the table and sat across from him. “Can I ask one useful question?”

“Please.”

“What would make you stop after six months? Not because you feel scared for one bad week, but because the plan really isn’t working?”

Michael thought. “If I can’t keep up at work, or if I start using money I need for rent and bills. I could also ask the teacher for help before I decide to stop.”

“That sounds clear,” Laura said. “And I should tell you something. My life looks settled, but I still feel pressure to make every choice perfectly. My job is steady, and people think that means I never worry. I do.”

Michael had not expected that. “What do you worry about?”

Laura gave a small shrug. “Whether I should want something different. Whether changing my mind would mean I made a bad choice. I don’t always know.”

Peter nodded. “Less joking, more truth. I worry that I have stayed in the same job because it is easy, not because I like it.”

The room grew quiet, but it was not an angry quiet. They were all listening.

Robert still looked unsure. Helen still looked worried. Michael’s plan had not solved every question. But he had said what he knew, and what he did not know.

“We don’t have to agree tonight,” Michael said.

Robert nodded slowly. “No. But we can talk about it.”

13 A List of Small Things

Robert stood and carried the cups to the sink. “Come into the sitting room,” he said to Michael. “I want to talk about the money part.”

Michael followed him. A small lamp was on beside the sofa. Helen stayed in the kitchen with Laura and Peter, and the sound of running water came from the sink.

Robert took a notebook from a shelf. It was old and brown, with a bent corner. “When I first started working, I wrote down what came in and what went out. It was not exciting, but it stopped surprises.”

“I have a list on my phone,” Michael said.

“Good. Keep it simple. Write down the course fee, travel, and anything you need for books. Then leave some money for things you did not expect.”

Michael sat beside him. “I put a small amount aside, but I’m not sure it is enough.”

“Then check the course rules. Can you pay each month, or do they want the full amount at once?” Robert asked. “And check if you get your money back if you leave early.”

Michael wrote the questions on his phone. He felt calmer because these were things he could find out.

Robert turned the notebook toward him. “I still think you should be careful. A course can sound better when you are unhappy with your work.”

“Maybe,” Michael said. “I’m not unhappy every day. I just want to learn something that matters to me.”

Robert looked at him. “I did not always choose what mattered to me. When you were small, I took extra hours because I wanted to give you and Laura a good life. I don’t regret it. But I sometimes wonder what else I might have done.”

Michael had heard Robert talk about work before, but never quite like this.

“I don’t want you to make the same choices I made,” Robert continued. “But I also don’t want you to think a new course will answer everything.”

“I don’t,” Michael said. “I want to try it and see.”

Robert closed the notebook. “That is better than expecting it to fix your whole life.”

They sat quietly for a few seconds. Michael was glad Robert had spoken honestly, even though he still did not agree with the whole plan.

Helen came in with a container in her hands. “I packed some food for you,” she said. “There is enough for tomorrow’s lunch.”

“Mum, I can make my own lunch.”

“I know,” Helen said. “I made extra.” She put the container in his bag before he could answer.

Laura found Michael’s coat. “Call me after you check the course dates. I can help you compare them with your work hours.”

“Thanks. I will.”

Peter walked him to the hall. “I’m trying not to give advice unless you ask,” he said. “It is harder than I thought.”

“You can still tell me what you think,” Michael said.

“Good. I think you should buy a proper notebook. Your phone may fall in a cup of tea.”

“That is your advice?”

“It is the best advice I have.”

They laughed. It was a small laugh, but it made the goodbye feel easy.

Robert came to the door with Michael’s coat. “I’m not convinced yet,” he said. “But I can see you have tried to be careful.”

“That’s fair,” Michael replied.

Helen kissed his cheek. “Eat the food before it gets old.”

Outside, the evening air was cool. Michael walked to the bus stop with the container in his bag and the questions on his phone. His family had not given him one clear answer. They had given him things to check and stories of their own.

On the bus, he looked out at the lit shops and quiet streets. His worries were still there, but they no longer filled the whole space in his mind. He could take the next step without knowing every step after it.

14 The Ride Home

The bus moved slowly through town. A man near the front was carrying a bunch of flowers. Two children whispered over a game on a phone. Michael held his bag on his knees and watched the familiar streets pass.

He thought about his father’s notebook and his mother’s food. He thought about Laura saying that a steady life did not stop a person from feeling pressure. He thought about Peter choosing not to hide behind a joke.

At his stop, Michael got off and walked the last few streets to his flat. The air smelled of rain, though the road was still dry. He let himself in and put the food container on the kitchen counter.

His flat was quiet. He liked that quiet most evenings, but tonight it felt different. He had things to do, and they were small enough to begin.

He took out his laptop and searched for the course website. He found the page he had read before and checked the dates again. The next course began in four weeks. Lessons were on Tuesday and Thursday evenings.

Michael opened his work calendar. Tuesday could be difficult once a month, when his team stayed late. Thursday was usually fine. He made a note to ask his manager if he could leave on time on Tuesdays.

Then he checked the course fee. It could be paid in two parts. There was also a small fee for a set of drawing tools. He added both costs to his budget.

His first budget looked neat, but he knew real life was not neat. He added a line for extra travel and another for unexpected costs. The amount left for saving was smaller than before, but it was still there.

Michael leaned back in his chair. He had wanted the numbers to say yes without any doubt. Instead, they showed him what he would have to give up. He would eat at home more often and wait before buying a new pair of shoes.

That did not seem terrible. Still, he felt a little sad about it. He did not want every new choice to mean saying no to something else.

He made tea and opened the food container. Helen had packed bread, cheese, and a small piece of cake. A note was tucked under the lid: “Eat well. Call when you can. Love, Mum.”

Michael smiled. His mother had put the cake in a separate piece of paper, as if it were important cargo.

He ate at the kitchen table and checked the course page again. There was a number for the teacher, and the website said people could ask questions before signing up. Michael wrote down two questions: Could he miss a class if work ran late? Were the tools included in the price?

He still felt nervous about calling. He had always found it easier to wait until he knew what to say. But this was a simple call, and he did know what to ask.

He picked up his phone, then put it down again. It was too late to call that evening. He set a reminder for the next morning.

Before bed, he sent Laura a message: “I checked the dates and costs. Two evenings a week. I’ll ask my manager about Tuesdays and call the course teacher tomorrow. Thanks for listening.”

Laura replied a few minutes later: “Good. One question at a time. And you can ask me about the calendar if you want.”

Michael read her message twice. He felt grateful, but he also felt proud that the work was his to do. Laura could help him think, but she could not make the call or choose the course for him.

He opened a new note on his phone and wrote three steps:

“Call the course teacher. Ask my manager about Tuesday evenings. Check the budget again at the end of the month.”

The list was not a promise that everything would go well. It was a way to keep moving without pretending he could see the future.

He placed the phone beside his bed. Then he stood by the window for a moment. Across the street, a neighbour was closing the curtains. A light went on in the flat above his.

Michael thought of what Robert had said: a course would not answer everything. He agreed. It might not lead to a new job. He might find the lessons tiring. He might change his mind after six months.

But doing nothing also had a cost. It left him with the same questions and no new information.

He turned off the kitchen light and went to bed. His plan was still uncertain, but it had dates, costs, and questions now. That made it feel less like a wish and more like something he could test.

15 A Step He Can Take

The next morning, Michael called the course teacher during his lunch break. He walked to a quiet corner near the office stairs and read his questions from his phone.

The teacher spoke slowly and answered both. The tools were not included, but students could borrow some during the first weeks. If Michael missed a class, he could read the notes and ask for help at the next lesson.

“I can’t promise that every lesson will be easy,” the teacher said. “But you can come and see the room before you sign up.”

Michael liked that answer. It did not try to sell him a perfect future.

After the call, he sent a short message to his manager. He asked if they could talk about his Tuesday hours. His manager replied that they could meet on Friday.

At home that evening, Michael opened his budget again. He added the cost of travel and changed the amount he would save each month. It was less than before, but it was not zero. He also wrote down the date when he would review the plan: six months after the course began.

He looked at the page. It was not a complete answer to his life. It was a list of choices he could make now.

Michael called his mother after dinner. “I checked the course dates,” he said. “I spoke to the teacher, too. I can borrow the tools at first. I’m meeting my manager on Friday about my hours.”

“That sounds like a good start,” Helen said. He heard a pan being moved in the background. “Have you eaten?”

“Yes. The food you packed was very good.”

“I knew it would be,” she said, with the calm voice of a person who had never doubted a sandwich.

Michael told her he had changed the budget and set a date to review it. She asked if he had enough money for the course fee. He gave her the number, including the extra travel cost.

“I still worry,” she said. “But I’m glad you are looking at the real costs.”

“I’m glad you told me you were worried,” Michael said. “It’s easier to hear when you don’t tell me I’m making a mistake.”

Helen was quiet for a moment. “I will try to remember that,” she said.

After the call, Michael put the phone down. He felt a little more settled. Not because his mother had stopped worrying, but because they had spoken plainly.

On Friday, he would talk to his manager. After that, he would visit the course room and decide whether to sign up. There were still questions about how tired he might feel and whether he would enjoy the lessons.

He did not need to answer those questions tonight.

Michael looked at the message from Laura once more. He sent her an update about the teacher’s answers. She wrote back, “That sounds useful. Let me know how the meeting goes.”

He put his phone on the table and washed his dinner plate. In the past, he might have waited for someone in the family to tell him which choice was best. This time, he had made a small plan, asked for help, and kept the choice in his own hands.

That did not mean he had to do everything alone. His family could listen, ask questions, and share what they had learned. They could worry or disagree. He could hear them without handing them every decision.

Michael dried the plate and set it on the shelf. His room was small, his job was still the same, and his future was not clear. But he had taken care of the next few steps.

He felt hopeful, though not certain. There was no perfect solution waiting for him. There was only a choice he could test, a budget he could watch, and people he could call.

For now, that was enough.
