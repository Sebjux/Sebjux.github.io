0 The Repair Shop

At twenty-one, Daniel Miller knew how to repair a kettle, a bicycle light, and most things with a plug. He could find a loose wire by listening to the small click it made inside a machine. He could also tell, from the sound of his father’s van, when its engine needed attention.

People in the small town often said he was lucky. His father, Robert, owned the repair shop, and Daniel had a steady job there. The shop stood on the main street between a bakery and a shop that sold cards and newspapers. Its front window was full of old lamps and radios waiting for new parts.

Daniel opened the shop each morning at eight. He swept the floor, made coffee, and wrote down what customers brought in. Then he spent the day at the long workbench, under a bright lamp. He liked fixing things, but he disliked doing the same jobs in the same order every day.

Across the street, buses came and went. Their doors opened with a hiss, and people climbed aboard carrying shopping bags and work coats. Daniel sometimes watched them through the window. He wondered where they were going and what their days would be like.

“You’re meant to be looking at that radio,” Robert said one Tuesday.

Daniel looked down. The radio lay open between them, its back removed. “I am looking at it.”

“You’ve been looking at the street for five minutes.” Robert was not angry. He was a broad man with careful hands, and he spoke as if each sentence should be useful. “The customer wants it by Friday.”

Daniel bent over the radio. “I know.”

Robert took a small screwdriver from the bench and passed it to him. “Good. Check the connection near the speaker.”

The radio began to play a quiet song. Daniel smiled before he could stop himself. For a moment, the shop seemed less dull. He turned the volume up, and a singer’s voice filled the room.

“There,” he said. “It still has something to say.”

Robert gave a short laugh. “Most old machines do. People too, if you give them time.”

At lunch, Daniel ate a cheese sandwich behind the shop. He had saved pictures of faraway places on his phone: busy streets, green hills, and long beaches. He did not know much about any of them. He liked that feeling. They were places where nobody knew him as Robert Miller’s son.

That evening, he and Robert drove home in the van. Their house was a short walk from the shop, on a quiet road with small gardens. Anna, Daniel’s mother, had made vegetable soup. She asked about the day while she set bowls on the table.

“We fixed the radio at last,” Daniel said.

“You fixed it,” Robert corrected. “I only found the problem.”

Anna smiled at Daniel. “That sounds like good work.”

After dinner, Daniel went upstairs to his small bedroom. His desk stood under the window, and his old school books filled a shelf. He opened his phone and searched bus times to the city. A bus left at half past seven the next morning. He looked at the price, then closed the page.

He was not ready to go. He had no real plan and not much money. Still, he kept thinking about the people stepping onto the bus. Their lives were moving somewhere. His, he felt, was waiting in the shop.

The next morning, Daniel arrived early. He unlocked the shop and turned on the lights. The air smelled of dust, metal, and yesterday’s coffee. He put on his work coat and read the notes beside the telephone.

A man wanted a fan repaired. A woman had called about a lamp. Daniel wrote both jobs on the board, then stood still with the marker in his hand. Outside, a bus stopped across the road. A young woman got off and walked away with a red bag.

Daniel watched until she disappeared around the corner.

Robert came in carrying two cups of coffee. “You’ll need that,” he said, setting one beside Daniel. “We have a busy day.”

“How do you know?”

“The card shop owner brought three things in yesterday. And the school called about their old radio.” Robert took off his coat. “People need us.”

Daniel picked up his coffee. It was hot and strong. “That’s good, I suppose.”

Robert looked at him. “It is good. Work is work, but it also matters. When people can’t afford to buy a new thing, we help them keep the old one going.”

Daniel knew that was true. He liked helping customers. He just wished that helping them did not mean spending every day in the same room.

At ten, a woman came in with a desk lamp. Its cable was loose near the switch. Daniel listened carefully as she explained what had happened, then promised to call when it was ready. She thanked him by name.

After she left, Robert nodded toward him. “You’re good with people when you take your time.”

Daniel shrugged. “It’s only a lamp.”

“It’s not only a lamp to her. She reads in the evening, and the main light hurts her eyes.” Robert began sorting a box of screws. “Small things can make a big difference.”

Daniel wanted to answer, but he could not think of a kind reply that felt honest. He returned to the workbench. At the end of the day, he checked the bus times again. The fare to the city was not too high. The trouble was everything after the ride.

That night, Anna knocked on his bedroom door. She had a cup of tea for him. “You seem far away lately,” she said.

Daniel sat on the edge of his bed. “I’m just thinking.”

“About the city?” She placed the tea on his desk.

He looked up quickly. “How did you know?”

“You’ve checked those bus times at breakfast, at dinner, and once while brushing your teeth.” She sat on the chair by the window. “I’m not asking you to stay here forever. I only want you to know what you’re choosing.”

Daniel took the warm cup in both hands. “I don’t know what I’m choosing. I only know I can’t keep doing this without asking what else is out there.”

Anna nodded. She did not tell him he was wrong. Downstairs, Robert closed a cupboard. The familiar sound made Daniel feel both safe and restless.

“Think about it carefully,” Anna said. “Then talk to us before you do anything sudden.”

Daniel promised he would. He meant to keep that promise, though he did not yet know how difficult it would be.

1 A Bigger Shop

Robert called Daniel into the shop office on Friday morning. It was hardly an office. It had a narrow desk, two chairs, and a calendar showing a month that had already passed. Robert stood by the window with a set of papers in his hand.

“I’ve been talking to the owner of the empty shop next door,” he said. “He’s ready to rent it to us.”

Daniel sat down. “What would we do with another shop?”

“More space. A proper place for testing machines, and a small area where people can wait. We could take larger jobs from the school and the hotel outside town.” Robert’s face showed his excitement. “It’s a chance to grow.”

Daniel studied the papers. The numbers were written in neat columns. “It will cost more.”

“Of course. I’ve checked the rent and the repairs. We can manage it if we work carefully.” Robert tapped the papers. “I’ll need you to take on more responsibility. You can run the front and learn the accounts.”

Daniel was quiet. He knew what was coming, but he hoped he was wrong.

Robert pulled out the second chair and sat. “One day, the shop will be yours. This is a good way to begin taking over.”

“Did you ask if that’s what I want?”

Robert’s smile faded. “I thought it was clear. You’ve worked here for years. You know the customers. You’re good at the work.”

“I know how to fix things,” Daniel said. “That doesn’t mean I want to do it here for the rest of my life.”

His father looked tired rather than angry. “Most people don’t know what they want at twenty-one. They learn by working.”

“I am working. I just don’t want this to be my whole future because we never talked about another one.” Daniel heard the sharpness in his own voice, but he could not pull the words back.

Robert folded the papers. “Your mother and I have built a life here. I thought you wanted to be part of it.”

Daniel stood up. “I am part of it. But I’m not sure I chose it.”

For a moment, neither of them spoke. Then the front bell rang, and Robert went to answer it. Daniel stayed in the office, looking at the empty chair across from him.

That evening, Anna made pasta with tomatoes and onions. The three of them ate at the kitchen table. Robert talked about the new space and the cost of painting it. He did not mention their argument. Daniel pushed pasta around his plate.

“You should have told me you wanted more responsibility,” Robert said at last. His voice was careful. “I thought you’d be pleased.”

“I know you did,” Daniel replied. “That’s part of the problem.”

Anna looked between them. “Maybe this is a conversation we need to have slowly.”

Robert rubbed his forehead. “I’m not trying to trap you. I’m trying to give you a good chance.”

“I understand.” Daniel looked down at his food. “But your chance might not be my chance.”

After dinner, he went upstairs. He opened the old bag he used for weekend trips and laid it on the bed. Then he shut it again. He did not know if he was brave enough to go or sensible enough to stay.

On Saturday morning, Robert was already in the shop when Daniel arrived. He showed him a list of work for the new place: cleaning, painting, and moving the heavy shelves.

“If you help me get it ready, you’ll see what the business can become,” Robert said.

Daniel wanted to say that he already knew. Instead, he carried a box of spare parts next door. The empty shop smelled of damp wood. Its front windows were dusty, and old marks showed where shelves had stood.

As he worked, he pictured a different room: a cheap room in the city, perhaps, with a window that did not close well and a table bought from a second-hand shop. The picture was not exciting in itself. What mattered was that he would choose it.

At lunch, Anna came by with soup in a flask. Robert poured it into three mugs, and they ate outside on the front step.

“The owner says he can leave the old counter,” Robert said. “That will save us money.”

Anna turned to Daniel. “Are you still thinking about going away?”

Robert paused with his mug halfway to his mouth.

“Yes,” Daniel said. “I think I need to try living somewhere else for a while.”

Robert set down his mug. “When?”

“I don’t know.” Daniel knew that this was not a strong answer. “Soon, maybe.”

“With a job arranged?”

“No.”

“A place to stay?”

“Not yet.”

Robert stared at him. “That isn’t a plan, Daniel.”

“I know.”

Anna put a hand on the flask. “Then perhaps you should make one before you leave.”

Daniel felt the warmth leave his face. He had imagined his parents telling him he was foolish, then driving him to the bus station. Their real questions were harder because they were fair.

“I can look for work when I get there,” he said.

Robert shook his head. “You can look. That doesn’t mean you’ll find it quickly. Rooms cost money. Food costs money. The bus home costs money.”

“I have savings.”

“How much?”

Daniel gave the amount. It was enough for a few days if he was careful, perhaps longer if he was very lucky. Robert did not laugh. He simply looked down at the work list.

“I don’t want to stop you,” he said after a while. “I want you to understand what you’re stepping into.”

Daniel heard the care inside the warning, though it sounded like doubt. “I do understand.”

He did not, not really. He had read prices on his phone, but he had never had to pay rent alone or choose between a meal and a bus fare. Still, the thought of waiting until he had a perfect plan made him feel as if he would never move.

That night he counted his savings twice. Then he opened the bag again and began to pack.

2 The Bus Station

Daniel did not sleep much. He packed two shirts, a warm jumper, clean socks, his phone charger, and a small tool set. He took his bank card and the cash he had saved from work. In the morning, he stood in front of the open bag and wondered if he had brought too much.

Downstairs, Anna was making tea. She looked at the bag by the door but said nothing at first. Daniel sat at the kitchen table. The clock showed six twenty.

“I’m going today,” he said.

Anna poured tea into a mug. “I thought you might.”

“I’m sorry I didn’t make a better plan.”

“You can still make one as you go. Just don’t pretend a choice has no cost.” She put the mug in front of him. “How much money do you have?”

He told her.

She went to the small drawer beside the fridge and took out a folded note. “Take this. It isn’t much, but it may help.”

“Mum, I can’t take your money.”

“You can, because I’m offering it. But don’t use it to avoid thinking. Spend carefully, and call me when you know where you are.”

Daniel took the note. He felt both thankful and ashamed. “I will.”

Robert came down a few minutes later, dressed for work. He looked at the bag, then at Daniel.

“I’ll keep your job open for a while,” he said. “I’m not promising forever.”

“I don’t expect you to.”

Robert held out a small paper envelope. Inside was the money Daniel had earned from a customer’s special repair. “You finished this yesterday. I forgot to give it to you.”

Daniel knew his father could have sent the money through the bank. The envelope felt like something more than payment, though Robert did not make a speech about it.

“Thank you,” Daniel said.

Robert nodded. “Travel safely. And read the signs before you get on the wrong bus.”

It was almost a joke. Daniel gave a small smile. “I’ll try.”

They did not hug. Robert walked back toward the stairs, and Anna followed Daniel to the door. She put her arms around him for a moment.

“Think about what you want,” she said quietly. “Not only what you want to get away from.”

At the bus station, Daniel bought a ticket to the city. The ticket cost more than he had expected, even though he had checked the price. He still had enough for food and a few nights in a cheap place, he told himself. He did not check what those nights might cost.

He chose a seat by the window. His town slipped past: the bakery, the school, the small park, and the road that led to the repair shop. He watched until the houses became fields.

For the first hour, he felt light. Nobody needed him to open a shop or fix a fan. He could get off wherever he wanted. He could eat when he was hungry and walk when he was tired. The day belonged to him.

Then the bus stopped at a road-side station. Daniel bought a hot cheese pie and a bottle of water. The food was good, but the price surprised him. He checked his money and put the bottle in his bag for later.

The city appeared slowly, first as distant blocks and towers, then as a busy line of buildings beside the road. Daniel sat up straight. He had arrived somewhere new, though he had no idea what he would do next.

The bus station was louder than the one in Daniel’s town. Announcements came from speakers above the ticket desks, and people moved in every direction with bags and cases. A woman hurried past with a sleeping child in her arms. Two workers carried a long ladder between them.

Daniel stepped outside. Cars filled the road, horns sounded, and a warm smell of fried food came from a stall on the corner. He stood with his bag at his feet and turned slowly, trying to take everything in.

His phone showed a message from Anna: Are you there safely? He wrote back that he was. Then he opened a map and looked for cheap rooms. The first place was full. The second asked for a price that would take nearly half his cash. The third did not answer the phone.

He decided to walk. The city felt made for people who already knew where they were going. Daniel passed small shops, office buildings, and a line of cafés with chairs on the pavement. He bought a small coffee and sat outside. It cost more than a coffee at home.

He looked up places to find work. A few shops had signs in their windows, but one wanted someone with experience, and another had already closed for the day. He wrote down two addresses for the morning.

By late afternoon, his feet hurt. He found a bench near a public garden and sat with his bag between his knees. The garden was full of families, office workers, and people walking dogs. Daniel felt as if everyone had somewhere to return to.

A man sat at the other end of the bench. He wore a green jacket and carried a canvas bag with a broken strap. He looked about twenty-four. After a few minutes, he nodded at Daniel.

“First day in the city?” he asked.

Daniel glanced at him. “Is it that clear?”

“A little. You keep looking up at the buildings.” The man smiled. “I’m Jack Turner.”

“Daniel Miller.” They shook hands.

Jack asked where Daniel had come from. Daniel told him about the small town, the repair shop, and the argument with his father. He left out how little money he had. Jack listened without interrupting.

“I know the feeling,” Jack said. “I’ve been on the road for years. Staying in one place makes me restless.”

“What do you do for work?” Daniel asked.

Jack leaned back. “A bit of this and that. A few days in a café, a job helping someone move, small things. I don’t like plans that turn into rules.”

Daniel liked the sound of that, though he was not sure he understood it. Jack spoke as if he had chosen freedom and never paid for it.

“Do you know somewhere cheap to stay?” Daniel asked.

“There’s a hostel across the river. It isn’t fancy, but it’s friendly. I was going to meet a friend there later. We could walk together.”

Daniel checked the map. The hostel was a long walk, but he felt better with someone who knew the city.

“All right,” he said. He picked up his bag, pleased that his first decision in the city had led to a new person.

3 Jack’s Way

Jack walked quickly, but he knew the streets well enough to stop and point out things as they passed. He showed Daniel a square where a street band played, a covered market, and a river path lined with old trees.

“You can buy anything in that market,” Jack said. “Good bread, cheap fruit, old clothes. You just have to know which sellers are fair.”

They went inside. The market was warm and full of noise. People called out prices from behind boxes of oranges and baskets of bread. Daniel bought a banana and a small roll. They were cheaper than the food near the station.

Jack bought a paper cup of tea and led him to a stall selling hot potatoes with cheese. “You can’t live on rolls,” he said.

“I’m trying to watch my money.”

“So am I. But a proper meal can make the day feel better.” Jack ordered two potatoes and paid before Daniel could offer his share.

Daniel was grateful. They sat on a low wall and ate with plastic forks. The potato was soft and hot, with plenty of cheese. Jack told stories about places he had visited, people he had met, and jobs he had left when they began to feel too much like a routine.

“Do you ever miss having a steady place?” Daniel asked.

Jack thought for a moment. “Sometimes I miss knowing where I’ll sleep. I don’t miss anyone asking me what I’ll do in five years.”

Daniel understood the second part more than the first. He had not thought much about five years. He was mostly trying to get through next week without choosing the same life as his father.

Jack’s friend did not appear. Jack checked his phone, shrugged, and said they should keep moving. He took Daniel down narrow streets lined with second-hand shops and small bars. At one shop, Daniel found a warm scarf for a low price. He bought it, although the afternoon was mild.

“Good choice,” Jack said. “The evenings get cold by the river.”

They reached the hostel just before six. A paper sign on the door said NO BEDS AVAILABLE. Jack read it and gave a small laugh.

“That’s new. They usually have space.”

“What do we do?” Daniel asked.

“There’s another place near the old station. I know the owner. We’ll find out.”

Jack said it with such confidence that Daniel did not question him. They crossed a busy road and followed him toward a row of food shops. The second place looked more like a bar than a hostel. A man behind the counter told them there were no beds there either.

Jack asked if anyone knew a room they could rent. The man pointed them toward a guesthouse farther away. Jack thanked him, then kept walking.

The guesthouse had one room left, but the price was high. Daniel looked at the money in his wallet. He could pay for one night, but not many more. He asked if the room had two beds. It had only one.

“We could share,” Jack said. “It’s a big bed.”

Daniel did not like the idea of sharing a room with someone he had met that afternoon. Jack noticed his hesitation.

“Fair enough,” he said. “I’ll keep looking.”

They walked on, and Daniel was glad that Jack did not push him. Still, the city was growing darker, and each new place seemed farther away.

Jack led them to a small café with yellow lights in its windows. He knew the woman behind the counter. She greeted him by name and asked if he had found work yet.

“Not today,” Jack said. “This is Daniel. He’s new here.”

The woman smiled at Daniel. “Then be careful with your money. It disappears quickly in this city.”

“I’m learning that,” Daniel said.

Jack asked her about a cheap room. She thought for a moment and wrote an address on a napkin. “Try this place, but call first. The rooms are simple.”

Daniel thanked her. Jack bought two coffees, even though Daniel said he did not need one. He also bought a slice of cake and put it in front of Daniel.

“You’ve helped me all day,” Daniel said. “Let me pay for something.”

“You can get the next one.” Jack smiled and turned to talk to the café woman.

Daniel looked at the bill when it came. Jack had paid for their food, but Daniel had bought the scarf, the coffee they shared earlier, and a bus ticket after Jack said walking would take too long. The ticket had been for both of them. Daniel had spent more than he meant to.

He could have asked Jack to pay his share. Instead, he told himself the money was not a problem. He had come to the city to try something new. A few small costs were part of it.

They called the number on the napkin. A tired voice answered and said all the rooms were taken. Jack said he knew another place near the station. By then, the night buses were less frequent, but there was still time.

Daniel’s feet were sore, and his shoulders hurt from the bag. “Could we go back to the first hostel?” he asked.

“We can try,” Jack said. “Maybe someone has left.”

On the way, they stopped at a corner shop. Jack bought a bottle of water and a packet of biscuits. Daniel went inside too. He bought a sandwich and a carton of milk, thinking it would be breakfast if they found somewhere to sleep.

At the hostel, the sign was still on the door. Jack knocked anyway. A worker opened it and shook his head.

“We’re full tonight. Sorry.”

“There must be somewhere else,” Daniel said. He looked at the dark street. “A cheap hotel or a late bus.”

“There are places,” Jack answered. “We just haven’t found the right one.”

That confidence was starting to sound less useful. Daniel checked his phone. A cheap room appeared on the map, nearly an hour away by bus. He asked Jack to come with him.

Jack studied the route. “It’s far. I thought I might meet someone near here, but I don’t have to.”

“You said we’d look together.”

Jack rubbed his chin. “You’re right. Let’s go to the bus stop.”

They reached the stop as a bus pulled away. The next one was due in forty minutes. Daniel sat on the bench and felt cold through his jacket. He could see Jack sending a message. Neither of them spoke for a while.

Daniel had liked the freedom of following Jack. He had not noticed how quickly he had stopped making his own choices. Now he had less money, tired feet, and no bed. The lesson was not clear yet, but the result was.

4 No Safe Bed

The bus came forty-five minutes later. Daniel and Jack got on and found seats near the back. Daniel paid for his own ticket this time. It cost more than he expected because the late service used a different fare. The driver gave him a paper ticket and told him to keep it until the end of the trip.

The bus moved through streets that Daniel did not know. Shops closed behind metal doors. A few people stood outside bright restaurants, laughing as they waited for tables. Farther out, the houses became lower, with small gardens and dark windows.

Jack leaned his head against the glass. “I’m going to close my eyes for a minute.”

Daniel looked at him. “Do you know where we get off?”

“I think so. Near the old station.”

“You think so?”

Jack opened one eye. “I’ve been here before. We’ll be fine.”

Daniel wanted to believe him, but he opened the map on his phone and followed the blue line. The bus reached a wide road with a large shopping centre. Jack sat up and looked around.

“We should get off here,” he said.

Daniel checked the map. “It says three more stops.”

Jack frowned, then looked at the street outside. “I was thinking of another place. Let’s stay on.”

Daniel felt annoyed. It was a small mistake, but it had made the evening feel longer. He did not say anything. He kept watching the map.

At the correct stop, they got off beside a closed shop and a row of empty bus shelters. The cheap room was not where the map had shown it. Daniel called the number listed online. A recorded voice said the line was no longer in use.

“That’s bad,” Jack said.

“It’s more than bad. I should have checked before we came all this way.”

“You couldn’t know the number was old.”

“I could have called earlier.” Daniel heard his voice grow hard. “We spent the whole evening walking around because you said you knew places.”

Jack looked down at the pavement. “I thought I did. I’m sorry.”

Daniel wanted to keep being angry, but the apology was plain and honest. He took a slow breath.

“I’m sorry too,” he said. “I kept following you instead of checking things for myself.”

They stood under the bus shelter. Daniel opened the travel app. There was one bus back toward the centre in half an hour. After that, the service stopped for the night. He checked the money in his pocket. A hotel was impossible unless he used most of what he had left.

“I can try my friend again,” Jack said. “Maybe he’ll answer.”

Daniel looked at the time. “Who is this friend?”

“A man I met last year. He lives somewhere near the centre.”

“Somewhere?”

Jack gave a tired smile. “I know it isn’t much of a plan.”

Daniel called Anna. He did not want to wake her, but she answered after two rings. He told her he was safe, then admitted he had not found a bed.

“Where are you now?” she asked.

He told her the name of the stop.

“Can you get back to the main station?”

“Yes. There’s a bus in thirty minutes.”

“Then go there. Stay where there are staff and lights. Call me when you arrive.”

Daniel almost asked her to send money. He stopped himself. “All right.”

“You don’t have to solve everything tonight,” Anna said. “Just make the next safe choice.”

When the bus arrived, Daniel waited for Jack to get on first. He did not feel free now. He felt tired and uncertain, and he had to decide what to do next.

They returned to the centre after midnight. The main station was still open, but only a few people sat inside. A worker in a blue jacket stood near the entrance. Daniel went over and asked if the waiting room stayed open all night.

“It closes in an hour,” the worker said. “You can wait outside after that, but the doors will be locked.”

Daniel thanked him and looked around. Bright lights made the station feel safe, but they did not make it a place to sleep. A security guard walked through the hall, checking the doors.

Jack’s phone rang. He stepped away to answer it. Daniel heard him say, “No, I’m sorry, I can’t come now,” and then, “I know. I should have said something earlier.”

When Jack returned, he looked uncomfortable. “That was my friend. He thought I was meeting him before dinner. He’s annoyed.”

“Are you going to see him?”

“I don’t think he wants to meet me tonight.” Jack put his phone away. “I’m sorry, Daniel. I made this harder than it needed to be.”

Daniel did not know what to say. He was angry, but Jack had not forced him to buy the coffee or follow him to the wrong bus. Daniel had wanted to be carried along because it felt easier than making a plan.

“I should have checked the hostel and the bus route,” Daniel said. “And I should have watched what I was spending.”

Jack nodded. “You’ve got some money left?”

“A little.” Daniel did not want to say exactly how little.

They went to a food shop that was still open. Daniel bought a small bottle of water and used the bathroom there. He had the sandwich in his bag, but it was warm now and not safe to eat. He threw it away in a bin.

The shop worker told them about a late café near the station. It had bright lights, hot drinks, and tables, but customers could not sleep there. Daniel and Jack went inside anyway. Daniel ordered tea and counted his remaining money beneath the table. He had enough for breakfast and a few bus fares, but not enough for several nights in a hotel.

He sent Anna a message saying he was back near the station. She replied at once: Stay inside until they close. Call me in the morning.

For the next hour, Daniel watched people come and go. A group of young workers ate chips at the next table. A driver in a pale shirt drank coffee while reading the news on his phone. Everyone seemed tired in an ordinary way, with a home or a bed waiting after work.

Jack rested his arms on the table. “I’ve slept in worse places,” he said.

Daniel looked at him. “I don’t want tonight to become something I have to repeat.”

“You probably won’t let it.”

Daniel wanted to believe that too. He knew one bad night did not mean his choice had been a mistake. But it showed him that wanting freedom was not the same as knowing how to use it.

At one thirty, the café worker told them the tables were closing. The main station doors were already locked. Outside, the wind moved wrappers along the pavement. A narrow covered area stood beside the station, near a bright sign and a camera. It was not comfortable, and Daniel did not feel fully safe there. Still, it was better than a dark street.

He sat with his bag on the ground between his shoes. Jack sat beside him, keeping his distance. Neither spoke for several minutes.

Daniel took out his phone and typed a message to Anna. I’m near the main station. I’m staying where it’s lit. I’m okay, but I don’t have a bed tonight.

He pressed send, then put the phone away. The city moved around them in distant sounds: a bus engine, a door closing, someone calling from across the road.

Daniel had left home because he wanted to choose his own road. Now the first night had ended with no safe bed, too little money, and no clear direction. He did not know where the road would take him. For the first time, he understood that he would have to choose each step himself.

5 A Room Above the Street

The city room was smaller than Daniel had expected. It stood above a shop that sold phone cases and cheap headphones. At night, the sign outside shone blue through the thin curtains. The room had a narrow bed, a small table and a sink with a cold tap. Daniel put his bag beside the bed and counted his money. After paying for the room and two bus rides, he had less than he wanted.

He found work three days later at a warehouse near the ring road. A man called Michael showed him where to stack boxes of kitchen goods. Michael wore a yellow vest and carried a clipboard. “The work is simple,” he said. “Read the label, put the box in the right place, and ask if you are not sure.” Daniel nodded. He wanted Michael to think he could manage on his own.

The first morning, Daniel got on the wrong bus. He noticed when the streets became narrow and the warehouses disappeared. He got off, checked his phone and hurried to the next stop. By the time he reached work, he was twenty minutes late. Michael looked at his watch.

“The bus can be late,” Michael said, “but you need to leave time for trouble.” Daniel promised to do better. He worked hard that day, though his arms ached from lifting boxes. At the end of the shift, Michael gave him a paper with his hours and told him to return the next morning.

For a week, Daniel was careful. He learned the bus times and made sandwiches in the room, using bread and cheese from the corner shop. He sent a short message to Anna to say he had a job. She replied that she was glad, then asked if he had eaten properly. Daniel wrote, “Yes,” though he had skipped lunch that day.

On Friday, Jack called. He had found Daniel’s number through a friend and wanted to know how the city was treating him. Daniel told him about the warehouse but did not mention the late bus. Jack laughed at a story about a customer from their town, and for a few minutes Daniel felt close to home. When Jack asked if he might come to visit, Daniel said he was too busy to make plans.

The trouble began the next week. Daniel stayed up late watching videos on his phone. In the morning he turned off the alarm and slept again. He ran for the bus, but it pulled away as he reached the stop. He arrived late for the second time. Michael did not shout, but he wrote something on his clipboard.

Later that day, Daniel put a box on the wrong shelf because he did not check its label. A worker had to move it before a delivery went out. Michael spoke to him beside the loading door. “You can do this job,” he said. “But you have to pay attention. Being quick is not the same as being ready.” Daniel felt his face grow hot. He said he understood, and meant it.

That evening, he bought a hot meal from a small café instead of making another sandwich. The food was good, but he watched the coins leave his hand. Back in his room, he set two alarms and put his phone across the table. Then he wrote the next bus time on a piece of paper. He wanted to prove that the city had not beaten him.

6 The Last Shift

Daniel kept both alarms on, but the warehouse did not give him much time to repair his mistakes. On Monday, he was late again. The first alarm had sounded, but he had turned it off without waking fully. He ran downstairs and reached the stop just as the bus left. At work, Michael was waiting by the entrance.

“This is the third time,” Michael said. His voice was calm, which made Daniel feel worse. “I need people I can count on. Finish today, and then we will talk.” Daniel worked carefully, but he kept thinking about what might happen. At lunch he ate his sandwich outside beside the wall. He could hear the machines inside and the traffic on the road.

In the afternoon, a delivery arrived early. Daniel was asked to bring several boxes to the packing area. He lifted them quickly, trying to show that he was useful. One box slipped from his hands and landed hard on the floor. A jar inside broke. Nothing dangerous happened, but Michael came over and looked at the broken glass.

“You were told to use the trolley,” he said. Daniel had forgotten it in his hurry. Michael helped him clear the pieces, then asked him to wait after the shift. He explained that the warehouse had no more hours for him. Daniel would be paid for the work he had done, but he should not come back the next day.

Daniel walked to the bus stop with his hands in his pockets. He was angry with Michael, then angry with himself. He had wanted to be treated like an adult, but he had not acted like someone people could depend on. That night he looked at job notices on his phone. Most needed experience or a longer journey than he could afford.

He called Robert. For a moment he nearly hung up before the call connected. He told his father that the warehouse job had ended. Robert was quiet, then asked what Daniel planned to do next. Daniel said he would find something else. Robert did not tell him to come home. He only said, “Make a plan before you spend the money you have left.”

The next morning Daniel took a bus to the edge of the city and went into a small employment office. A woman there wrote down his name and asked about his experience. When he mentioned lifting boxes, she said a farm outside town needed help for a few weeks. The owner, George Harris, was sixty-seven and wanted someone to work with him.

“It starts early,” she warned. “It is outside, and the pay is by the week.” Daniel looked at the small amount of money in his wallet. He wanted a warm room and easy work, but he did not have the choice he wanted. He said he would go.

A bus took him through the last streets of the city and out past fields. At a small stop, a broad man in a dark coat was waiting beside an old truck. George held up one hand. Daniel got off with his bag. George looked at him, then at the time on his watch. “You are here,” he said. “That is a start. We begin at six tomorrow.”

7 Before the Sun

George’s farm had a low house, a long shed and fields that reached toward distant hills. Daniel slept in a plain room above the shed. There was a narrow bed, a chair and a small window that looked over the yard. George showed him where to find clean water and a thick coat. Then he pointed to the alarm clock on the table.

“Six means six,” George said. “We eat breakfast after the first work.” Daniel wanted to ask why, but George had already gone downstairs. In the morning, the air was cold enough to hurt Daniel’s nose. He pulled on the coat and followed George to the field. They carried buckets and checked the fence before the sun came up.

The work was harder than Daniel had imagined. The ground was wet, and his boots soon became heavy with mud. George showed him how to lift a bucket close to his body and how to tie a loose wire without cutting his hands. Daniel tried to move as quickly as he had at the warehouse. He finished one task fast, then had to do it again because he had missed a corner.

George watched him start over. “Look first,” he said. “Then work.” Daniel wanted to answer that he was looking, but he kept quiet. They went inside for eggs, bread and tea. His fingers were stiff around the cup. After breakfast, he could see that the sun had reached the tops of the sheds.

The days began to follow a pattern. Daniel fed the animals, repaired small sections of fence and carried supplies. George did not praise every good job, but he thanked him when he remembered to put tools back in their places. In the evenings, they ate potatoes, vegetables and whatever meat George had cooked. Sometimes they spoke about the weather or the price of feed. Sometimes the kitchen was quiet.

On the fourth day, dark clouds came over the fields. George told Daniel to cover a stack of bags before the rain. Daniel thought he could finish one other job first. He kept working by the shed, and then the rain came hard. When he ran back, several bags were wet through. George helped move them under cover, but they were too damaged to use.

Daniel expected an angry speech. George only said, “I told you what needed doing first.” The cost would come out of the farm’s supplies, not Daniel’s pay, but he would need to work longer that afternoon. Daniel’s shoulders hurt as he carried the dry bags one by one. He wanted to blame the weather, yet he knew he had been given a clear warning.

That night he called Sophie, who had once helped him find a bed when he left home. He told her the rain had ruined some supplies. She listened without trying to make the story smaller. “Are you going to stay?” she asked. Daniel looked at the wet boots near the door. He said he did not know. She reminded him that deciding to leave was also a decision, and he should make it when he was rested.

The next morning, George gave him a mug of tea before they went outside. “You made a mistake,” he said. “That happens. What matters is what you do after it.” Daniel did not answer straight away. He was still tired and still wished he were somewhere easier. But he put on his boots, checked the sky, and picked up the right tools.

8 A Road by the Water

A week later, Daniel found a letter from home in the small post box by the farmhouse gate. Anna had written about ordinary things: a broken lamp, a meal she had made and a message from Emma. Emma had heard there might be seasonal work near the seaside and thought Daniel should ask about it. Anna did not tell him to come home or to stay away. She wrote that Robert was busy at the repair shop and had been asking how Daniel was.

Daniel read the letter twice. That evening, he asked George about the seaside work. George said he knew a man who managed a small storehouse near the harbour. They needed help moving boxes before the busy season. “It is not a promise,” George said. “You will have to ask for yourself.” He gave Daniel the man’s name and the number of the local bus that went toward the coast.

The thought of leaving made Daniel restless. He had begun to understand the farm’s daily work, and George had started to trust him with the shed keys. Still, the farm was not his future. He wanted to see what he could do in another place, this time without pretending he had everything planned.

On his last morning, Daniel finished the fence work before breakfast. He checked the wire twice and put the tools away. George gave him his pay in an envelope. Daniel counted it at the kitchen table, then put some aside for the bus and a cheap room. He had spent too much in the city; now he wanted to know exactly what he could afford.

George drove him to the bus stop after breakfast. The truck smelled of oil and damp coats. At the stop, he handed Daniel a small paper with the harbour manager’s name and number. “Call when you arrive,” he said. “Do not wait until you are worried. And if the work is not there, find out what your next step is before the money runs out.”

Daniel thanked him. He wanted to say something more, but the words did not come. George nodded as if he understood and drove back toward the farm. Daniel stood alone with his bag, watching the road. He thought of Robert’s warning to make a plan, and of the times he had heard it as a way to control him. Perhaps Robert had also known what it was like to have no money and no clear answer.

The bus carried Daniel through fields, past a town with a market square, and then along a road where the air began to smell of salt. At a larger station, he changed buses. He bought a cheese roll and a bottle of water, checked the price before he paid, and kept the receipt in his pocket. Small choices did not solve everything, but they helped him feel less lost.

As the bus came down toward the seaside community, Daniel saw roofs gathered around a harbour. Boats moved on the water, and workers were unloading crates near the quay. He did not know if the storehouse job was waiting for him. He did know he would ask for it himself, listen carefully, and arrive early.

He stepped off the bus and called the number on the paper. While it rang, he looked at the road that ran beside the water. It led past the harbour and out toward the quieter end of town. Daniel had no promise that it would take him somewhere better. When a man answered, Daniel gave his name and asked if they could speak about work.

9 The Café by the Sea

The café stood near the end of the seaside road, where the bus turned around before going back towards town. Its windows looked over a row of small boats and a grey strip of water. Daniel started work there on a Monday morning. He had a clean shirt, a notebook in his pocket and only a little money left after paying for his room.

Sophie Clark was already behind the counter. She was twenty-three and moved quickly without making the work seem hurried. She showed Daniel where to find cups, bread, tea and the cloths for cleaning tables. Then she pointed to the small clock above the kitchen door.

“The bus brings people at ten, so we need the tables ready before then,” she said. “And write down every order. I know you think you’ll remember, but you won’t.”

Daniel smiled. “I’ll remember.”

At ten past ten, a family came in, followed by two older men in wet jackets. Daniel took their orders and carried a tray of tea, soup and toast. He had not written anything down. He put the soup in front of the wrong man and gave one customer tea with milk when she had asked for it without.

“Sorry,” he said, feeling his face grow hot. He took the tea back and brought another. Sophie did not tell him off in front of the customers. She quietly placed a small pad beside him when he returned to the counter.

“Start again,” she said. “Names, food, drinks. It saves time in the end.”

By the afternoon, the rush was over. Daniel sat at a table near the window with a bowl of soup and a slice of bread. He had earned enough for the day’s pay, but his mistake had meant that he stayed late to clean the kitchen and count the cups.

Sophie sat opposite him and opened a notebook of her own. It was full of numbers: rent, travel, food and the cost of a nursing course.

“You’re studying nursing?” Daniel asked.

“I’m saving for it. I work here, and I take a class twice a week in town. It will take time, but I can see what I’m working towards.” She checked a figure with her pencil. “I don’t want to borrow more than I need.”

Daniel thought about the money in his pocket. He had been saving too, though some weeks he spent part of it on food from the station or a drink with people he barely knew. He had told himself that he would sort things out later.

“It must be hard to plan every pound,” he said.

“It is,” Sophie replied. “But not planning is harder. Then the rent comes, and you wonder where the money went.”

The next morning, Daniel wrote every order down. He still moved too slowly, and once he carried two plates to the wrong table, but he caught the mistake before anyone began to eat. Sophie showed him how to carry a tray close to his body and how to look at the table number first.

At lunch, a man asked for a job. He had been walking along the sea road and had seen the café sign. Sophie explained that there were no more hours available. The man thanked her and left without buying anything.

Daniel watched him go. “Couldn’t we have given him a few hours?”

“We have to pay him properly,” Sophie said. “And the owner has already chosen the staff. It isn’t kind to promise work we don’t have.”

That evening, Daniel put his pay into separate envelopes: one for rent, one for bus fares and one for savings. He made a mistake with the figures and had to count the money twice. Still, when he finished, he knew what he could spend.

Sophie passed him on her way out, carrying a bag of books. “You’re coming in early tomorrow?” she asked.

“Yes. I’ll bring the notebook.”

He walked back to his room above the street as the light faded over the water. The road still seemed long, and he still did not know exactly where it ended. But now he had a few figures written down and a job to return to in the morning.

10 Jack Comes Back

Jack appeared at the café on Friday afternoon. Daniel was carrying a basket of clean cups when he saw him through the front window. Jack had a new jacket and a tired look. He waved as if they had parted only yesterday.

“There you are,” Jack said when Daniel reached the counter. “I heard you’d gone to the coast.”

“I work here now.” Daniel put the cups away. “Are you staying in town?”

“For a few days, maybe. Things are a bit messy.” Jack ordered coffee and sat near the door. He told Daniel that he had left his last room without paying all the rent. He had borrowed money from a friend and had not paid it back. Another friend was angry because Jack had promised to help with a move and never arrived.

“I didn’t mean to let anyone down,” Jack said. “I just needed to get away for a while.”

Daniel brought him the coffee. Part of him was glad to see Jack. They had shared good times, and Jack could make an ordinary afternoon feel exciting. But Daniel also remembered the times he had followed him without asking what it would cost.

When Daniel finished work, Jack was waiting outside. They walked along the sea road, past a shop selling postcards and a row of houses with their windows shut against the wind.

“Come with me tonight,” Jack said. “A few people are meeting at the old hall. We can talk, have a drink. You look like you’ve become very serious.”

Daniel had an early shift. He also had only a small amount of spending money until payday. He thought about the envelope in his room marked BUS AND FOOD.

“I can’t stay long,” he said.

At the hall, there were six people and a radio playing too loudly. Jack borrowed money from one person for drinks, then told a story that made another person leave. Daniel stayed longer than he had planned. By the time he got back, he had missed the last bus and paid for a cheap taxi with money meant for food.

The next morning, he woke with a dry mouth and reached the café late. Sophie was setting out cups. She looked at the clock, then at him.

“I’m sorry,” he said. “I missed the bus.”

“The bus came,” she said. “You missed it.” Her voice was not angry, but she did not cover for him. Daniel washed his hands and went to work. He kept his head down, and the morning felt twice as long.

After work, Jack asked him for some money. He wanted to pay for a room for the night. Daniel had enough to help, but giving it to Jack would mean taking money from his rent envelope.

“I can pay you back next week,” Jack said.

Daniel remembered the earlier promises. “I can’t lend you money. I need it for rent.”

Jack stared at him. “You used to be different.”

“Maybe,” Daniel said. His hands felt cold, though the afternoon was mild. “I still want to see you. I just can’t keep saying yes to everything.”

Jack looked away towards the water. He did not shout. He told Daniel that he would ask someone else and started walking towards the bus stop.

That night Daniel counted his money and wrote down the taxi fare. He had made a poor choice, and the page did not hide it. He crossed one planned treat off his list and packed a sandwich for work the next day.

At the café, he told Sophie what had happened. She listened while she dried a plate.

“It’s hard to say no to someone you care about,” she said. “But you can care about him and still protect your own life.”

Daniel thought of Jack walking away. He felt sad and a little relieved. Both feelings seemed true. For once, he did not try to turn them into a reason to run after him.

11 A Song and a Plan

A week later, Daniel heard music from the community room behind the library. He had gone there to ask about a notice for the shelter, but a young woman was playing a small guitar by the open door. She stopped when someone dropped a pile of books nearby.

“Sorry,” Daniel said. “I didn’t mean to interrupt.”

“You didn’t,” she said. “I’m Emma Wilson.” She was twenty and wore a work shirt under a light jacket. “I work at the bakery in the mornings. I play here when the room is free.”

The song was quiet and simple. Daniel told her he liked it. Emma thanked him, then asked if he knew where she could find a cheap music stand. She had been using a box to hold her pages, but it kept falling over.

“There’s a second-hand shop near the bus station,” Daniel said. “I’ve seen stands in the window.”

They walked there together. Emma found one with a scratch on the side and paid for it with coins from her pocket. She counted the change carefully.

“Music doesn’t pay my rent yet,” she said. “So I keep the bakery job. I’m trying to get a few paid evenings, but I don’t want to pretend that everything will work out by itself.”

Daniel thought of the list beside his bed. He had been saving, though not always well. “I understand that more than I did before.”

They sat at a small café near the library. Emma bought tea, and Daniel chose a roll because it cost less than a hot meal. They spoke about work, rooms and long bus rides. Emma lived with her aunt in the city, but she travelled to the town several days a week for rehearsals and paid shifts.

“Some people say I should choose one thing,” Emma said. “They think the bakery job means music is not serious.”

“Maybe they don’t see what the money is for,” Daniel said.

“Maybe. I don’t want to spend my life waiting for a perfect chance. I want to play, and I also want a place to live.” She smiled. “Those things can both matter.”

Daniel told her he had been careless with money and had arrived late at work after going out. He did not make the story sound funny. Emma listened without telling him what to do.

“Did you decide what to change?” she asked.

“I started writing things down. And I’m trying not to say yes before I think.”

“That sounds useful,” she said. “It’s not exciting, but useful is good.”

On Sunday, Emma played at a small afternoon event in the library room. Daniel came after his shift. There were twelve people in folding chairs, a baby asleep against its mother and a man who kept checking his phone.

Emma’s hands shook before she started. She looked at the music stand, then at the first page. Her opening note was too soft, but she took a breath and began again. The second time, the song filled the room.

Afterwards, a woman who ran a local evening group asked Emma if she would play there the following month. It was not a large payment, but Emma wrote down the date and asked about the time and travel cost before she agreed.

“I’m glad you asked those questions,” Daniel said as they packed the stand.

“If I don’t ask, I find out too late,” she replied. “That happened once with a job. I spent more getting there than I earned.”

Daniel walked with her to the bus stop. He had hoped she might ask him to stay longer, but he did not want to turn a friendly afternoon into something it was not. He told her he had work early the next day.

“Then get home and sleep,” Emma said. “There will be other afternoons.”

The bus came, and she climbed aboard with her guitar and the scratched music stand. Daniel watched it leave, then crossed the road towards his room. He felt interested in her, but he also felt something else: respect for the way she made a place for her dream without pretending the rest of life would wait.

12 The Shelter Room

Daniel found the notice about the shelter on the library wall. Volunteers were needed to help prepare supper and sort donated clothes. He wrote down the address and went there after work on Wednesday.

The shelter was in a plain building near the bus station. Inside, the room smelled of soap, wet coats and vegetable soup. Michael Reed, a broad-shouldered man of thirty-five, greeted Daniel and gave him an apron.

“We start with the tables,” Michael said. “Then we serve food. If you’re not sure what someone needs, ask them. Don’t guess.”

Daniel carried bowls into the dining room. People came in slowly, some alone and some talking together. A woman in a red scarf sat near the wall. A man with a heavy bag asked if there was somewhere to charge his phone.

Daniel pointed towards a socket before checking with Michael. The man plugged in his phone, but another person needed the socket for a small medical device. Michael showed Daniel a second place where the phone could be charged.

“I should have asked first,” Daniel said.

“That’s all,” Michael replied. “You noticed the problem. Next time, ask before you move things.”

At supper, Daniel served soup and bread. A man named Peter asked for another piece of bread, then changed his mind and said he was not hungry. Daniel wondered if Peter was being difficult, but he kept the thought to himself.

Later, Michael explained that Peter had been sleeping badly and was finding it hard to eat. He did not give Daniel a long story about the man. He only said that people had different reasons for doing things.

Daniel felt ashamed. He had decided what Peter was like after two sentences.

When Peter came back to the table, Daniel asked if he wanted tea. Peter said yes. Daniel brought it with no sugar, then saw the two sugar packets beside Peter’s hand.

“Would you like sugar?” Daniel asked.

“One, please.”

Daniel gave him one packet. Peter added it to his tea and looked out of the window. They sat quietly for a moment. Daniel did not ask why he was there.

Across the room, Michael helped a woman fill in a form for a place to stay. He spoke slowly and waited while she found the right words. He did not take the paper from her or answer for her.

When the meal was finished, Daniel washed dishes with Michael. Water ran across his sleeves, and he dropped a spoon into the sink with a loud clang.

“Not your best shift,” Daniel said.

“A useful one, though,” Michael replied. “You kept working.”

Daniel told him he had once thought he could help people by giving them advice. At the shelter, he had quickly understood that he knew very little about the lives in front of him.

“Listening is part of the work,” Michael said. “So is washing dishes. Neither one fixes everything, but both can make this evening a little easier.”

Daniel asked how Michael began volunteering. Michael said he had first come to help with a repair day. He had stayed because people needed regular help, not only a new coat of paint.

On the bus back, Daniel thought about the different people he had met. He could not know all their stories, and he did not need to invent them. He could remember names, ask simple questions and do the job he had been given.

At home, he wrote the next volunteer evening in his notebook beside his café shifts and bus costs. There was not much room left on the page, so he started a new one.

The following week, Daniel arrived early. He set the tables, checked the charging places and asked Michael where each box belonged. When Peter came in, Daniel greeted him by name. Peter nodded and took a seat near the window.

After supper, Peter asked if there was any more tea. Daniel went to make it. The work was ordinary, and the room was still full of difficult lives. But Daniel no longer felt that he had to understand everything before he could be useful.

13 The Delivery That Did Not Come

The shelter room was busy before breakfast. Michael stood beside a table covered with tins, bread, soap, and folded blankets. He checked each list while two volunteers carried boxes to the door.

“The town bus can take us to the station,” he said. “But we still need a van to bring the food from the storage room. Can you organise it, Daniel?”

Daniel looked at the list. It was longer than he expected, but he did not want to seem unsure. “Yes. I’ll find a van and get everything here by ten.”

Michael gave him a small notebook. “Write down the driver’s name and the time. And remember, the food has to reach the families before the market closes.”

“I understand,” Daniel said. He put the notebook in his pocket and hurried outside.

He had seen a white van near the café the day before. Its driver, a broad man called Leon, had said Daniel could ask him for help if he was free. Daniel decided that was enough of a plan.

Leon was not near the café. Daniel walked down the road, then tried the small garage by the bus stop. The owner was working under a car and could not lend him a vehicle. Daniel could have called Michael then. Instead, he thought he could still manage.

At the station, he bought a ticket to the next town and asked a shopkeeper about local van services. The first number did not answer. The second company could only come in the afternoon, after the market closed.

Daniel looked at the clock. It was already ten past nine. He had spent the morning moving from place to place, but he had not told anyone where he was or what had gone wrong.

At last, Leon called back. His van had broken down outside the city. “I’m sorry,” he said. “I can’t help today.”

Daniel ran back to the shelter. A row of empty boxes stood by the door. Michael was there with a woman in a blue coat. She held a list and kept looking at the time.

“The food is still at the storage room,” she said. “My brother and I have waited for the delivery. We have to collect our children soon.”

Michael’s face was tight with worry. “Daniel, where is the van?”

Daniel wanted to explain the bus, the garage, and the phone calls. But none of those things changed the fact that he had promised and failed. “There isn’t one. I thought Leon would be free, but I never checked. I should have called you earlier.”

The woman, whose name was Grace, lowered her list. “We trusted you to bring it. Some families are waiting for these bags.”

Michael did not shout. That made Daniel feel worse. “We’ll take the bus to the storage room,” he said. “But we can carry only what fits in our hands.”

Grace and her brother could not stay. They had to leave for school and work. Michael asked another volunteer to watch the room while he and Daniel went to the bus stop.

They bought two tickets and carried bags of rice, tins, and soap between them. The bus was crowded. One tin fell from Daniel’s bag and rolled along the floor. A passenger picked it up and smiled, but Daniel could not smile back.

They made two trips. By the time the last food reached the shelter, the market was closing. Grace had gone, and several families had left without their bags.

Daniel put the empty boxes in a neat pile. “I did this,” he said. “I’ll stay and help replace what we lost.”

14 The Work After the Mistake

Michael unlocked the storage room the next morning. Daniel was already waiting outside with his jacket zipped up and a piece of bread in his hand.

“I have no van today,” Michael said. “The other volunteer is ill. We can still make deliveries on foot, but it will take most of the day.”

“I’ll come,” Daniel said. “I know I made trouble yesterday. I can do the work now.”

Michael studied him for a moment. “All right. First we check what is missing. Then we make a plan that does not depend on one person’s guess.”

They counted the food, wrote down each family’s address, and marked the streets on a paper map. Daniel called Grace and asked what her family had missed. He listened while she named the items, then wrote them carefully.

The first two addresses were close together. Daniel carried bread and tins in a strong cloth bag. At the second house, an older man opened the door but did not take the food at once.

“You are late,” he said. “I waited yesterday.”

Daniel could have blamed the van that never came. Instead, he said, “You’re right. I told Michael I could organise the delivery, and I didn’t do it properly. I’m sorry.”

The man looked at him, then moved aside. “Put it on the table. I have a bad knee today.”

Daniel left the food by the kitchen table. On the way out, he asked if the man needed help carrying water upstairs. The man said no, but thanked him for asking.

They walked through the warm part of the day. At a small shop, Michael bought them two cheese rolls and a bottle of water. Daniel paid for his own, then checked his money. He had enough for the bus back but not much more.

“I didn’t think about how a late delivery would affect people,” Daniel said as they sat on a low wall.

Michael opened his bottle. “You thought about your promise, not about the people waiting for it.”

Daniel felt the truth of that. He had wanted to look capable. He had not asked for help because he was afraid that asking would make him look weak.

After lunch, they took the bus to Grace’s street. She was home with her children. Daniel handed her the missing food and showed her the new delivery list. This time, her name and address were in the right place.

When they returned to the shelter, Daniel washed the empty bags and swept the floor. Michael checked the list and told him which families had been reached.

“You stayed and worked,” Michael said. “That matters. But next time, tell me as soon as a plan starts to fail. We can make another plan together.”

Daniel nodded. “I will.”

He knew saying the words was easy. He would have to remember them when he felt rushed or proud.

That evening, he found a message on his phone from Anna. It was short: “I’m worried about you. Please call when you can. Robert’s shop has had a serious problem. He hasn’t told me everything, but he may lose an important order.” Daniel read it twice. The seaside suddenly felt much farther from home.

15 The Message from Home

Daniel called Anna from the path above the beach. The wind made it hard to hear, so he walked down behind a wall and tried again.

“I’m all right,” he said when she answered. “What’s happening with the shop?”

Anna was quiet for a moment. “The machine for Robert’s biggest order has stopped working. The customer needs the parts soon. Robert paid for new supplies already, and now he may not have enough money to finish the work.”

“Can it be repaired?”

“He thinks so, but the repair will cost more than he has. He is trying to borrow money. He says he doesn’t need help, but I can hear how tired he is.”

Daniel pressed the phone close to his ear. “Is he angry with me?”

“He hasn’t said that,” Anna replied. “But he asks if I’ve heard from you. I’m worried because I don’t know when you’ll come back or what you plan to do next.”

Daniel looked toward the water. He had been telling himself that he was free because nobody could decide his days for him. But Anna’s voice brought the small town and the repair shop close again.

“I can come home,” he said.

“You can,” Anna said. “But don’t come only because you feel guilty. Think about what you want, too.”

After the call, Daniel sat on a stone step. He had imagined travelling for months, seeing new places and finding work wherever he went. He could continue along the coast. He had enough money for a few bus tickets and a cheap room. Or he could go home and face Robert.

He went to the café and found Sophie cleaning tables after lunch. She had heard about the failed delivery from Michael.

“I’m not proud of it,” Daniel said.

“You came back and helped,” Sophie replied. “But you also have to be honest about why you left. You can’t repair everything by running toward the next place.”

Daniel sat by the window. “I don’t know if going home means I have failed.”

Sophie placed a cup of tea in front of him. “It means you are choosing where to be now. That’s different.”

Later, Daniel told Michael he might leave for home. Michael listened without trying to change his mind.

“The shelter can manage tomorrow,” Michael said. “Take care of what you need to take care of.”

Daniel thanked him and promised to help with one more delivery in the morning. He wrote the driver’s number in the notebook, called to confirm the time, and asked the driver to call if anything changed. Then he showed Michael the plan.

This time, Michael added a second contact number and checked the route with him.

On the bus back to the seaside room, Daniel decided to leave the next morning. He did not know if Robert would welcome him, or if the shop could be saved. He only knew that staying away would not answer those questions.

16 The Shop Door

The journey home took most of the day. Daniel changed buses in the city and watched shops, offices, and blocks of flats pass the window. At the last stop, he walked from the station to the repair shop with his bag over one shoulder.

The sign was still there, but the front window was dark. Inside, Robert sat at the workbench with a cup of cold tea. Small metal parts covered the table. His hair looked untidy, and his shirt had a dark mark near the pocket.

Robert looked up when Daniel came in. “You’re back.”

“Yes.”

Neither of them moved for a few seconds. Daniel had prepared many things to say on the bus. Now they all seemed too large or too small.

“Anna told me about the order,” Daniel said. “Can I see what happened?”

Robert turned away from the bench. “The machine stopped halfway through the job. I should have checked it earlier. I kept putting off the service because I wanted to finish the work first.”

Daniel looked at the parts. He could see a broken belt and a metal wheel with a damaged edge. “Could someone repair the wheel?”

“Maybe. But it costs money, and the customer is already unhappy. I said the order would be ready.” Robert rubbed his eyes. “I thought I could do it all myself.”

Daniel understood that kind of pride. “I made the same mistake at the shelter. I promised Michael I could arrange a delivery, but I didn’t check anything. People waited because I wanted to look sure of myself.”

Robert looked at him. “I was angry when you left. I thought you were throwing away the life we had worked for.”

“I left without thinking about what would happen here,” Daniel said. “I wanted to get away from your plans, but I didn’t explain myself. I’m sorry.”

Robert put both hands on the bench. “I was afraid you would have no future if you didn’t stay with the shop. So I tried to choose everything for you. I called it help, but I was controlling you.”

It was not the reply Daniel had expected. He felt both relief and pain. “I don’t want to take over the shop,” he said. “But I could help for a while.”

“A while?” Robert asked.

“Until the order is finished, or until we know the next step. I also want to look for training in mechanical design or transport. I’m interested in how machines and roads work. I need to make my own choices, but I can still help.”

Robert stared at the broken wheel. “That seems fair. I can’t promise the order will save the business.”

“I know.”

“And I can’t pay you much.”

“I’ll need to agree on hours and pay. I have to cover my own food and travel.”

Robert nodded slowly. “We’ll write it down tomorrow. No guessing.”

Daniel put his bag in the small room at the back. It was dusty and crowded with old boxes, but it was a place to sleep. When Anna came in with a pot of soup, she looked at Daniel, then at Robert.

“You’re both talking,” she said. “That’s a start.” She set bowls on the table, and for the first time in many weeks, they ate together without pretending that everything was fine.

17 A Doorway Open

Daniel worked at the shop for three weeks. He and Robert agreed on a short work schedule and a small weekly payment. Daniel swept the floor, carried supplies, and helped take apart the broken machine.

Robert showed him how the belt moved the wheel. Daniel asked questions, sometimes too many, and Robert sometimes lost patience. But they stopped when the talk became sharp and started again later.

The wheel could not be repaired safely. Robert called the customer, explained the delay, and offered to finish part of the order first. The customer was not happy, but agreed to give them more time. The new part cost money they did not have, so they sold an old machine and used some of the payment to buy it.

Daniel was learning that a problem did not always disappear because someone worked hard. Sometimes the best they could do was tell the truth and find the next step.

One afternoon, Anna came to the shop with a paper in her hand. “I found this at the learning centre,” she said. “There is a course in mechanical design. It includes a short placement with a bus company.”

Daniel read the page slowly. The course began in two months. It was in the city, and the travel cost would be high, but there was a small support payment for students who qualified.

“This is exactly the kind of thing I wanted to find,” he said.

Robert leaned over his shoulder. “The bus company?”

“They plan routes and check the vehicles. I want to learn more about design, and about how people and things move between places.”

Robert gave a small nod. “Then apply. I can’t tell you whether it’s the right choice.”

Anna smiled at Daniel. “You sound different when you talk about it now.”

“I think I’m starting to understand what it takes,” he said. “It isn’t only leaving. I need to ask questions, check the money, and make a plan.”

He spent the next evening filling in the application. He listed his time at the repair shop and his work at the shelter. He did not make the failed delivery sound better than it was. Instead, he explained what he had learned from the mistake.

The form asked for a person who could speak about his work. Daniel asked Michael by phone. Michael agreed, after reminding him that he had to give notice before leaving any future volunteer job.

“I won’t make the same promise without a plan,” Daniel said.

On the last morning of his agreed work period, Robert showed Daniel how he had marked the new part for the machine. They fitted it together and tested the wheel. It turned evenly, without a shake.

“Good work,” Robert said.

Daniel cleaned the tools and put them away. He was glad the machine worked, but he did not feel that he had to stay forever because of it. The shop belonged to Robert. The next step belonged to Daniel.

Before he left, Robert handed him a small envelope with his final pay. “For the work we agreed on,” he said.

“Thank you,” Daniel replied. “I’ll tell you when I hear about the course.”

That evening, Daniel stood in the shop doorway with his bag beside him. The street was quiet. Across the road, the bus stop sign showed the times for the city and the villages beyond it.

Robert was inside, putting away the day’s tools. Anna stood near the door with a scarf around her neck.

“Are you going out?” she asked.

“I’m going to check the bus times and visit the learning centre tomorrow.”

“And after that?”

Daniel looked down the road. He did not know if he would study in the city, work in the shop again, or travel somewhere else. The open road did not promise an easy life. It offered choices, and each choice would affect other people as well as him.

“After that, I’ll decide with more care,” he said.

He stepped into the evening, leaving the shop door open behind him.
