0 A Usual Monday

Daniel Reed walked into Greenford Services at eight o'clock. The light was soft and the car park was quiet. He had his usual coffee in a thermos and a small notebook in his pocket. Daniel liked his routine. Routine made him calm. He knew the trolley routes, the depot codes and the daily checks. He liked to know what would happen next.

His colleague James greeted him by the time clock. "Morning, Dan," James said. He rubbed his hands. "You look sharp today." James smiled because he liked to tease Daniel. Daniel smiled back but he kept his voice low. He preferred to listen rather than to start long conversations.

At the desk, Michael was already on the computer. Michael was younger and quick with his hands. He played music quietly while he worked. Michael liked change. He tried new tools fast and often. He liked to talk about apps and websites. He knew Daniel did not like big changes. He admired Daniel's steady work though.

During the first hour, Daniel checked supplies and filed a few paper forms. The forms had steps written in the same order every week. Daniel read each line, ticked the boxes and filed them in the right folder. The work was careful and exact. He enjoyed the small tasks because they felt safe and clear.

At nine-thirty, Sarah Mitchell walked in. She held a folder and moved with a calm purpose. Sarah was the operations manager. She had a clear voice and a friendly way of talking. She stopped at the team area and looked around. "Good morning, everyone. Can I have two minutes?" she asked.

The team moved closer. Sarah smiled and opened her folder. She talked about a meeting she had the previous week. Daniel listened. He liked Sarah's steady approach. He respected her role and her questions. He enjoyed the order she brought to small problems.

After the short talk, everyone returned to work. Daniel answered a phone call from his sister in the afternoon. She asked if he wanted to go to her house for dinner on Sunday. He almost said yes. He hesitated and then said no. He had plans to rest and prepare for the week. His sister was gentle and asked him why he did not want to try anything different. He said it was fine and changed the subject.

Michael saw the call later and made a small joke. "You always know what comes next, Dan. You never jump." Daniel gave a small laugh. He felt the truth in the joke. He liked the safety of the predictable day. It felt like a blanket around him.

At lunch, the team sat in the canteen. Emma arrived with a bright bag and loud laughter. She sat down and told stories about a course she had taken. Emma was confident. She spoke in full sentences and asked questions without fear. Daniel listened and admired her energy. He liked how she asked for what she wanted.

The Monday was ordinary and safe. Daniel finished his sandwich and made a note in his notebook. The note was a list of small jobs to do that afternoon. He liked the list. It was neat and clear. He would work through it, one step at a time. This was his comfort. He did not know it would change soon.

1 The New System

On Tuesday morning, Sarah called a short team meeting in the office. The room had a long table and a screen. Everyone sat down. Sarah stood by the screen and smiled. "I have news," she said. "Our head office wants us to move to a digital system. It will automate some tasks and make reports faster."

A murmur spread around the table. Some people looked interested. Others were quiet. Daniel felt his shoulders tighten slightly. He liked paper forms. They were familiar. He worried that a new system would change the small routines he relied on.

Sarah explained the plan. The company would introduce a program that could track stock, orders and timesheets. It could also send alerts and create reports. "We need a small team to help us move from paper to the new system," Sarah said. "We want colleagues who know our current processes. This will help the developers set up the program correctly."

James scribbled notes quickly. Michael raised his hand and made a quick joke about change again. Emma looked straight at Sarah and said, "I can help. I want to learn." Emma's voice had a clear tone. She sounded ready. Daniel watched her. He felt a small admiration for how she offered herself without thinking twice.

Then Sarah looked at Daniel. Daniel felt the room narrow. He saw the surprise on his own face in a flash of disbelief. Sarah smiled kindly. "Daniel, you know our current processes very well. Would you consider joining the team? You are careful and you see the small details. We would like you there."

Daniel felt a rush of reasons to say no. He could think of many. He could say his workload was too full. He could say he did not like new software. He could say he had no time. He almost said something like that, but he stopped himself. He opened his mouth and then closed it again.

Emma leaned forward. "I'll do it too," she said. "I can learn fast and test things. It will be fun." Her voice had no doubt. The manager Sarah nodded. "Good. It will be a small group. Priya from IT will train us first. We need someone who knows the old way and someone who can try new ideas. That is why I asked Daniel. You are both good fits."

Daniel felt a mix of worry and curiosity. He liked the idea of helping because it mattered. He also feared making mistakes in front of people. He looked at Emma and then at Sarah. He wanted to say no and stay safe. He wanted to say yes and help. After a moment, he said, "All right. I'll help. I can do some hours." He said it in a quiet voice, not the loud reply he heard from Emma.

The meeting moved on to a schedule and some dates. Priya, the IT trainer, would arrive on Thursday for the first session. Everyone agreed to meet in the small training room. Daniel wrote the date in his notebook and tapped his pen. The rest of the day felt heavier and brighter at the same time. He kept checking the calendar.

After work, Michael asked him if he was sure. "You looked like you were going to say no," Michael said. Daniel shrugged. "I thought about it. It feels important. I can help with the details. I'll see how it goes." Michael smiled. He liked that Daniel had accepted. He also understood Daniel's quiet worry. They left together, talking about a sports match on the radio.

2 First Training

On Thursday, the team gathered in the training room at nine. The room had new chairs and a big screen. Priya arrived with a laptop and a folder. She introduced herself with a calm voice. "Hello, I'm Priya from IT. I will guide you through the new system." She smiled kindly and clicked the laptop.

The software opened on the screen. It looked neat and modern. There were boxes, menus and buttons. Daniel felt a small tightness in his chest. The menu items were new words. He could not read them with the same quick certainty as the paper forms. He listened closely to Priya.

Priya explained the logic behind the system. She showed how to log in, how to find a stock item and how to enter a timesheet. Emma tried the steps quickly. Her fingers moved on the keyboard. She asked a few questions about extra features and shortcuts. She smiled when the software did what she expected.

Daniel watched Emma and felt two feelings at once. He admired her ease. He also felt embarrassed to remain quiet. He could see that he misunderstood one instruction but he did not speak up. He told himself he would try later. For now he stayed quiet, watching and taking notes.

When it was time for a practice exercise, Priya asked each person to complete a small task on a training account. Daniel opened his folder and typed slowly. He read each field and moved the mouse carefully. He wanted to be thorough. He wanted to get it right on the first try.

He made a small mistake. He put a wrong code into the stock reference. It was an easy slip. The system accepted the change but a warning message appeared later. The warning said the item number did not match the expected category. Daniel felt his face go warm with embarrassment. He looked at the others to see if they had noticed.

Emma looked over and gave him a small, friendly smile. "It's fine. Try again," she said. Priya also walked over. "No problem, Daniel. We expect this. It's new for all of you. Let's fix it together and show you why that message appears." She spoke slowly and pointed at the screen. She did not sound annoyed.

Daniel felt relief, not because the mistake was small, but because the help was calm. Priya explained the error in a few short sentences and then asked Daniel to try again. He nodded and followed her steps. He corrected the code and the warning went away. He felt a small spark of success but it was mixed with feelings of vulnerability.

3 Practice and Mistakes

After the first session, Priya gave everyone a printed sheet with practice tasks. The tasks were simple but they had many steps. Daniel worked slowly at his desk. He opened the training account and read each instruction twice. He made deliberate clicks and took notes in his small notebook. He liked to write things in his own words.

Emma finished some tasks faster than him and moved on to optional exercises. She offered a tip about shortcuts. "If you press this key, you jump to the next field," she said. Daniel tried the shortcut and it helped. He felt gratitude toward Emma. He liked how she shared what she knew without making him feel small.

At one point, a part of the screen showed a drop-down menu with many options. Daniel did not pick the right option. The system created a duplicate record and made the later tasks harder. He opened his notebook and frowned. He felt the familiar worry when things did not go as planned. He closed the laptop for a moment and breathed deeply.

Priya came by his desk again. She did not make a big deal of the mistake. "This is a common step to get wrong at first," she said. "Take your time. Try the next task when you are ready. If you are not sure, ask a question. It helps me to know where people get stuck." Her voice invited questions.

Daniel wanted to ask but he hesitated. He feared a long explanation. He was still used to staying quiet. He remembered Sarah saying his attention to detail would help. He tried the next task and stopped at a point he could not understand. He said, "Priya, can you show me the drop-down again? I am not sure which option I should choose." His voice was low.

Priya smiled. "Of course. Let's show it one more time, and I will explain why each option matters." She took a few moments to show the menu and why some choices matched their paper records better than others. Daniel listened and nodded. The explanation made sense. He tried again and this time he avoided the duplicate entry.

When the practice hour ended, Daniel felt tired. He also felt a small change inside. The mistake had been frustrating, but asking a question had helped. Emma gave him a thumbs-up as she packed her bag. James came over and patted him on the shoulder. "Good work today," James said. "You stayed with it."

Daniel left the room thinking about the day. His notebook had a new list of small tips he had learned. He did not feel confident in the new system yet. He felt a small seed of trust that with practice and help he could learn. It was not a big change, but it was a start.

4 A Small Step

The next week moved at a steady pace. The training sessions were short and regular. Daniel came early and practiced for a half hour before his shift. He opened the training account and followed a set of tasks from his printed sheet. Repetition made the steps feel more familiar. His hands grew steadier on the keyboard.

Emma often joined him and showed a new shortcut or a helpful way to name a file. She explained how she organised items so she could find them later. Daniel tried some of her methods. He liked the order she used. It matched his own need for a clear system.

One afternoon, Sarah asked the group to test a week of entries in the training system. The team agreed to try to replicate the real workload. Daniel felt nervous because the test would show how the system handled many records. He worked carefully. He copied paper records into the software and watched the feedback messages appear.

Halfway through, Daniel realised he had chosen the wrong category for a group of entries. The software warned him, and the list did not match other reports. He could have left it and told someone later. Instead, he closed the task and walked to Priya's desk. "I think I used the wrong category for several entries," he said. He felt his heart beat faster.

Priya looked over the entries with him. She did not scold him. She pointed out the pattern of the mistake. "You used the general code instead of the specific one," she said. "It happens when people are tired. We can correct it now and add a note about it to the training sheet." Daniel felt relief. Fixing the mistake immediately seemed better than leaving it.

Priya showed him a quicker way to update multiple entries. Daniel watched and then tried it himself. He followed the steps and the entries updated correctly. When the task finished, he felt a real small success. Emma clapped lightly. It was not loud, but it meant something to him.

After work, Michael asked him about the training. Daniel told him about the mistake and how he fixed it. Michael nodded and said, "That was good. You went and got help early. That saves time and stress later." Daniel thought about what Michael said. Going to Priya had felt like a small risk. It had worked out well.

That evening, Daniel sat at his kitchen table and wrote down two things he had learned that week. He also wrote one question to ask in the next session. The question was short and simple. It felt less scary to keep a note and ask when the time came. He closed his notebook and stood up. He did not feel like a different person. He felt a little more ready for a change.

5 Old Habits, New Eyes

Daniel stayed late on Thursday to practise the new steps. He wanted to be ready for the live switch next week. He made small notes and ran the test scripts on the staging server. The new system was faster, but Daniel kept thinking about the old process. He had years of work with that old method. That history helped him see things in a different way. He did not want to stop learning the new system. He only hoped the old knowledge would help the team avoid mistakes.

On Friday morning, Priya came by Daniel's desk with a worried look. 'We saw a strange record in the test set,' she said. Michael was already looking at the logs. Daniel joined them and read the data. He noticed a field the others had not checked. The new import mapped the field differently from the old system. In the old process, the field was cleaned before import. In the new setup, it went in raw. That could cause duplicates and wrong matches later. Daniel pointed this out quietly.

Priya listened and nodded. Michael opened an error list and compared the results. They ran the import again with a cleaning step added. The duplicate did not appear. Michael looked surprised. 'Good catch,' he said. 'I did not see that when I tested the new scripts.' Daniel felt a small warm feeling. It was not pride. It was relief that his slow practice helped the team. He wrote a short note in the project log and sent a message to Sarah and Emma. The problem was small now. The fix was also simple.

At the midday meeting, James Turner spoke first. James had worked with the old process for many years. He always had strong opinions about change. 'Why are we changing this?' he asked. 'We spent money and time for no clear gain. This new system breaks some simple things.' His voice was steady. A few people looked uncomfortable. James did not like new methods. He felt the old way was safer. His concern was real. Some parts of the new system did add steps that could cause trouble for users who were used to the old way.

Sarah answered calmly. 'We asked for the change because the old system could not scale. We know there are rough parts now. That is why we test and fix them. Daniel found one such issue this morning. He showed how a field mapping missed the cleaning step. We fixed it in staging.' James frowned and did not seem fully satisfied. 'I am glad you caught that. But we need more safety checks before going live,' he said. His tone was not hostile. It was practical. He wanted to protect users and reduce rework.

During the discussion, Daniel felt his hands shake a little. He had a chance to speak. Sarah looked at him and said, 'Daniel helped find that mapping issue. He can show us the steps in the next meeting.' Daniel felt his face go warm. He had not planned to speak in front of the whole project. He liked to do the work quietly. But Sarah's look gave him a gentle push. The team agreed to let him show the steps next Tuesday. The meeting moved on to other tasks.

After the meeting, Emma came to Daniel and offered to help him prepare. 'You do not have to do it alone,' she said. 'We can practise together. Keep it simple. Use the example of the duplicate.' Daniel felt better. He wrote a short plan for his talk. He would explain the old cleaning step, show the new mapping, and then show what he did in the test. He felt nervous, but he also felt a steady thought growing: practice helps, and asking for help is part of the work. This thought carried him into the weekend as he reviewed his notes.

6 Speaking Up

On Tuesday, the small meeting room filled with people and a tray of coffee. Daniel sat next to Emma. He had his notes and a short slide. His hands were a little cold. Sarah gave him a smile of support. Priya, Michael, and James were at the table, plus two people from support. Daniel had rehearsed with Emma the night before. He had practised saying the steps out loud. He had also written three clear questions to ask the team during his talk.

When Daniel began, his voice was quiet at first. He used short sentences and a clear example. He showed the old cleaning step and then the new mapping. He pointed to the log where the duplicate appeared. He explained how he reran the import with the cleaning step and how the problem disappeared. Sarah added a note about why the cleaning had been important in the old system. The room listened. Daniel felt the nervousness fade a little with each point.

He then asked his first prepared question. 'Do you think we should add this cleaning step to the new import or change the mapping?' he asked. The question was simple. Emma suggested adding an automated cleaning script so the new mapping could stay. Priya mentioned the support team's needs. Michael asked if the cleaning would slow the import too much. James leaned back and folded his arms. He still had doubts but now they were more specific. 'If we add another step,' he said, 'we need clear rules on who checks it.'

People offered different ideas. The team talked about test cases and checking points. Daniel listened and wrote notes. The meeting felt more like a workshop than a lecture. When it was Daniel's turn to finish, he wanted to show the new run. He asked Michael to watch the live transition. 'If I run the updated import, can you watch the logs with me?' he asked. Michael agreed and opened his laptop. Daniel felt the room watching him. He started the script and followed his notes carefully.

At first, everything looked normal. But when the import moved from staging to the shared test area, Daniel clicked the wrong button on the interface. He accidentally selected the production feed rather than the test feed. The script began to push data toward a set of files used by the wider team. Michael saw it and called out. 'Stop, that is the production endpoint!' He quickly halted part of the process, but some files had already moved. Priya opened the file list and saw the extra records. A quiet tension filled the room.

Daniel's stomach fell. He felt a sudden shame that made him want to leave the table. He had practiced the steps many times. He had watched the buttons before. But his nervousness and the pressure of speaking made his hand choose wrong. He could see extra work ahead for support and the testers. James crossed his arms and said, 'Why did we not have a guard to stop that?' The tone was sharp. Daniel heard people asking reasonable questions about safety, but he also heard the sting of blame in his own head.

After the meeting, Daniel sat at his desk and considered leaving the project. He thought of his mistake often in the next hour. Sarah came by and sat opposite him. She did not scold. She asked calm questions: 'What happened? Tell me step by step.' Daniel told her honestly. He felt small but he told the truth. Sarah said, 'We will fix this. We must see what went through and who needs to act. But this is not about blame. It is about learning and protecting users.' Her words were steady. Daniel did not feel alone anymore. He agreed to work with her on the investigation.

7 Tracing the Error

Sarah and Daniel started the investigation on Wednesday morning. They gathered Michael and Priya and opened the logs. The team looked at timestamps and file paths. Daniel explained exactly what he had clicked and what he meant to click. He did not try to hide the mistake. He wrote the steps and where the interface was unclear. Priya pointed to a label that had two similar names. 'This could confuse anyone in a hurry,' she said. The change of tone helped. The team moved from blame to fact-finding.

They found three files that had been moved into the production area. The data did not reach real customers, but the support environment had to clean the test records. Michael said the clean-up would take a couple of hours. He did not sound angry. He sounded practical. Sarah suggested a short schedule to fix the files and to notify the small list of people who had seen the changes. Daniel and Michael prepared the clean-up steps. Priya wrote a quick email to the support team explaining what had happened and what they would do next.

Next, the team discussed the interface. Emma suggested a confirmation screen that showed the endpoint name in a bold line. Priya recommended a small delay or a second confirmation for production-level actions. James listened and then spoke up. 'I still worry that adding steps will make the process slower,' he said. 'But a clear guard for production sounds sensible. We do not need extra work for every user, only for the risky actions.' His view was practical. Everyone agreed that a targeted guard would be best.

Daniel felt both relief and a sense of responsibility. He had caused the extra work, but the team accepted that the interface could be improved to prevent similar errors. Sarah emphasized that responsibility worked both ways: users must be careful, and designers must make the interface safer. 'We will fix the words on the screen and add a confirmation for production runs,' she said. 'Daniel, thank you for being honest. That helped us find a good solution.' She spoke without praise, but with clear approval.

As they wrote the plan, Daniel asked his first clearer question: 'If I run a test again, who should be watching the logs step by step?' He wanted to avoid another mistake. Michael said, 'I will watch the logs and Priya will check the output. We will pause at the endpoint step and confirm.' Daniel wrote this down. The team agreed that any transition involving production would now require two people to confirm. This new rule felt practical and fair.

After lunch, Daniel and Emma practised the transition with the new confirmation screen on a local copy. Daniel read the screen out loud before he clicked. He asked Emma to repeat the endpoint name and count the files with him. The practice felt slow, but it reduced his nervousness. Daniel told Sarah he would make a short checklist and share it with the team. Sarah's reply was simple: 'Good. Make it clear and short. Then ask anyone to read it back to you once before they run the process.'

By the end of the day, the team had a clear plan. The clean-up was done. The confirmation screen had words that made it harder to click the wrong feed. Daniel still felt some shame, but it was lighter now. He had watched the logs, written the checklist, and practised with Emma. He had also learnt to ask precise questions about who would watch and when. This careful approach did not fix everything. It did make his next steps steadier.

8 A Small Repair

On Thursday morning, Daniel walked into the project room with a short checklist in his hand. He had left space on the page for signatures and time stamps. The new confirmation screen was live on the test server. Michael and Priya were ready to watch the run together. James dropped by, curious. He had not smiled much in recent days, but he listened to Daniel explain the updated steps. Daniel was careful and spoke slowly. He read the checklist aloud and asked Michael and Priya to confirm each step.

The run went slowly and carefully. Daniel paused before the endpoint selection and waited for Michael and Priya to confirm. 'Endpoint confirmed: test server,' Michael said clearly. 'Files ready: three files in staging,' Priya added. The confirmation screen required two clicks now, one by the runner and one by the watcher. Daniel felt the support of his colleagues with each check. The import completed, and the logs showed no errors. Daniel relaxed a little and allowed himself a small smile. The team then tested a simulated production run with the confirmation barrier in place.

After the safe run, James spoke in a different tone. 'This change looks sensible. The guard is a good balance,' he said. 'It slows things a little, but it reduces risk. I still have questions about scale, but this is a good start.' His words were cautious praise. Daniel felt his heart lift. He did not need to be perfect, he just needed to be careful and honest. He thanked James and the others. He also shared the short report of the event and the clean-up steps. The team agreed to bring the confirmation screen to the next deploy.

Later, Daniel sat with Sarah and Emma to write the new process page. He used simple language. He added the checklist steps and the new confirmation points. He also wrote a line about who should watch the logs. Sarah read it and suggested one sentence be clearer. Daniel rewrote it. He did not try to make the text clever. He wanted it useful. As he edited, he felt that asking for help was now a normal part of the job. He still felt nervous in big meetings. He still had to remember the checklist. But he now had a habit of asking clear questions before he acted.

That afternoon, Daniel got a short message from James. 'Good work on the fix and the checklist,' it read. Daniel felt a quiet pride. The message was not loud praise. It was a short note from a colleague who had been hard to please. Daniel replied, 'Thanks. I will keep practising and keep the checklist up to date.' He did not promise to be perfect. He promised to be careful and to ask for help when he needed it. That felt honest and strong.

At the end of the week, the team had a small celebration with tea and a cake slice in the kitchen. People talked about the runs and the new confirmation. Daniel stood with his cup and listened. He thought of the mistake and the clean-up and the way Sarah had helped him. He also remembered the first day he found the mapping issue. Things had not changed overnight. He had small steps forward and small slow repairs. He had practice, friends who asked questions back, and a checklist that kept him steady.

Daniel left the office that evening feeling tired but calmer. He knew he would still make errors in the future. He also knew he would ask for help and practise the steps before he ran anything large. The project still had work ahead. But Daniel had learned to speak up, to ask simple questions, and to accept help. That made the work feel less heavy. It did not fix everything, but it made the next week seem manageable.

9 Explaining to Learn

The morning at Greenford Services started like any other. The vans came in. The phones rang. Daniel sat at his desk with his laptop open. He had been working with the new system for a few weeks. He felt better than at the start, but he still met surprises. At ten, James came in with a worried face. James had been on the scheduling team for two years. He knew the old ways well. The new software was harder for him.

James sat on the visitor chair. He put a printout on Daniel's desk. 'This route won't print the job slip,' he said. 'I tried every step I knew. The driver came back without work.' Daniel looked at the printout. He had seen a similar issue last week during the small repair. This time the problem seemed different. He felt a small knot of doubt. He could ask Sarah or Michael, but he wanted to help James first.

Daniel stood and walked James through the screen. He spoke slowly. He showed James where to click and why. 'Try this menu first,' he said. 'Then check the route box here.' James followed. The screen showed a fault message. It was not the same as last time. Daniel explained what he thought the message meant. He spoke clearly, but he also asked James to explain his steps. James talked. He showed a detail Daniel had not noticed in the log file.

That moment changed things. As James explained, Daniel repeated the steps out loud. He said what each message meant. He used simple words. James nodded. Daniel found it easier to see the pattern when he explained it. He noticed an odd time stamp in the log. He did not know its exact role, but he guessed it mattered. He told James, 'I think this time stamp links to the scheduler. If the time is wrong, the print job skips.'

They ran a test. Daniel set the time to match the driver's local clock. The print came out. James smiled. 'So it was the time,' he said. 'I did not see that.' Daniel felt pleased, but not triumphant. He knew guesswork was risky. He said, 'We should log this with a note, and check another route.'

Michael walked by. He had been watching the team learn new tasks all month. He stopped and listened to their explanation. When the print job worked, he said, 'Good work. These small fixes build knowledge.' He did not expect instant mastery. He put a hand on Daniel's shoulder and said, 'Keep asking why. It helps.'

Daniel wrote a note on the ticket. He added the exact steps and the log line that had the strange time stamp. He told James to call if it happened again. James left looking less worried. Daniel sat down. He thought about how explaining the problem helped him see what he missed. He had to admit he did not know everything. But the act of teaching James had made his own ideas clearer.

Later, Sarah dropped by with a tea cup. 'You explained that well,' she said. 'You were calm and clear.' Daniel smiled. He was still nervous about speaking to managers one day, but small moments like this helped. 'It feels better when I say it out loud,' he said. 'I spot things I miss in my head.'

Sarah agreed. 'We learn by talking. Even when we are not teachers.' Daniel felt the truth of that. He decided to keep notes on his own explanations. He would write the steps and the words he used. He knew practice mattered. He would practice more, not to be perfect, but to be useful.

Before lunch, Emma appeared at the door. 'Do you have five minutes?' she asked. Emma often helped with presentations and practice. Daniel agreed. He had a small list of topics he wanted to try explaining. He opened his notebook. This time he felt steadier. He was ready to practice again.

Emma sat down and listened. Daniel talked about the time stamp and the scheduler. He explained each screen. Emma asked simple questions that a manager might ask. Daniel answered. Sometimes he hesitated. He told Emma when he was not sure. 'I will check that detail and get back to you,' he said twice. Emma nodded and suggested a clearer phrase for one step.

After the practice, Emma said, 'You are getting better at this. You find the key point and hold to it.' Her words made Daniel think of the earlier week. He remembered the tracing of the error and the small repair that had fixed a machine. The new system still had soft spots, but the team was learning them together.

Before leaving, Emma added, 'When you explain, use the example like you did with James. Managers like to hear the actual test. It gives them confidence.' Daniel wrote that down. He felt a small change in himself. He was not suddenly confident. He was simply more aware of how to practice and when to ask for help.

As the day ended, Daniel cleaned his desk. He read the notes he had made from the day's explanations. The green light on the printer flashed once and then stopped. The small repair and the day's work felt connected. He had helped James and learned more at the same time. He walked home thinking of the next chance to explain something and learn more.

10 The Presentation

Michael had asked Daniel to give a short talk to a group of managers. It was a small step. The managers wanted to know how the new system was working on the ground. Daniel felt nervous. He knew his work on the floor. He did not know how to say it to managers. He made notes on a pad. He wrote three points: the common errors, the small fixes, and the training needs. He kept them short.

Emma and Priya agreed to help him prepare. They both had good skills in speech and clarity. 'We will make this simple,' Priya said. 'Managers like clear facts and a few examples.' Emma nodded. 'Show a short demo if you can,' she added. Daniel liked the idea of a demo. He practised the demo twice.

They met in the training room after lunch. The room had a whiteboard and a small screen. Daniel stood in front of the board. Emma and Priya sat with pens. Daniel read the first point aloud. His voice shook a little. He stopped and asked, 'Does that make sense as a start?'

Emma smiled. 'Yes. But open with the problem. Tell them what their teams see.' Priya added, 'Then show the fix and the next step.' They helped him change the order. Daniel rewrote his notes. He used three short sentences for each point. He practised saying them without looking. He still looked at his notes a lot, but he felt more organized.

They role-played. Priya was a manager. She asked a tough question about long-term costs. Daniel answered with what he knew. He said, 'The fix is quick, but we will need a small update next month.' Priya responded like a real manager. 'How sure are you of that? Can you give a timeline?' Daniel paused. He did not have a precise timetable. He said honestly, 'I will check the logs and the vendor schedule. I will get you a date by Friday.'

Emma nodded. 'Good answer. Honesty is fine. Say what you will do and when.' They practised the timing. The talk should last ten minutes. Daniel timed himself. The first run was twelve minutes. 12 Seeing the Pattern

The next day began with a briefing. The vendor had sent a preliminary fix plan and a date for the patch. The team would run tests after the update. Michael wanted a clear test list. He asked Daniel to lead the test session. Daniel felt the weight of that request. It was another step. He had led small things before. Now the test would check the system the whole depot used.

During the test, Daniel watched closely. He read screens and logs aloud. When a result did not match the sheet, he said so. 'This one moved faster than the others,' he told Emma. She asked, 'Do you see a pattern in the user actions?' Daniel paused. Then he remembered the odd time stamp he had seen before and the explanation he had given to James weeks earlier.

He said, 'It may not be just the minute. I think it's the order of edits and the way the scheduler queues tasks. If a route is edited and then assigned within the same minute, the scheduler might try to lock both the route and the assignment. That double lock conflicts.' He wrote the idea on the board. Emma nodded and added a note about the queue timestamps.

James ran a new sequence. He edited a route and immediately assigned it to a driver. The system reacted differently. The log showed two lock attempts in quick succession. The vendor's engineer, watching on the remote call, asked a clarifying question. 'When you assign immediately, do you also edit the assignment fields?' she asked. Daniel checked the log and answered carefully. 'Yes, the assignment shows both the route and the driver change.'

The engineer typed rapidly and then said, 'We see the conflict. Our handler tries to take both locks in a fixed order. If two changes try to take the locks in the opposite order, we get a deadlock. We will change the handler to request a global lock for the route first. That should stop the deadlock.' The explanation sounded technical. Daniel felt the pieces fall into place.

He had not known the exact term 'deadlock' before. He listened as the vendor explained. He repeated the idea in plain words for the team. 'A deadlock is when two parts wait for each other,' he said. 'If editing a route asks for lock A then lock B, and another user asks lock B then lock A, they both wait and the system stops.' The team nodded. The plain words helped them see the solution.

After the call, Daniel wrote down the exact steps the vendor would take. He added a local test to make sure the handler now prevented deadlocks. He also suggested a small change in the team's practice: always change routes first, then assignments, when possible. It was a small process change, but it reduced risk until the patch arrived.

Michael read the test plan and the process suggestion. He said, 'Both are sensible. Good thinking, Daniel. You have started to see the pattern.' Daniel felt a warm, measured approval. It was not a celebration. It was recognition. He knew he would make more mistakes, but he could also make useful observations.

Later that week, the vendor pushed the patch to a test server. Daniel ran the planned tests with Emma and James. They tried many combinations. The system did not lock. The deadlock that had stopped drivers' work was gone in the test. Daniel felt relief, but he also felt careful. He remembered the managers' mixed feedback from his presentation. He knew real work still lay ahead to move the patch to production and to update staff processes.

The production patch was scheduled for the weekend. The team prepared notes and a small rollout plan.
