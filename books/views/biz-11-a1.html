0 A Quiet Morning

Tom Wilson was twenty-six. He worked at Brightway Solutions in a British town called Millford. The office was on a busy street, above a small bookshop. Tom got there at eight thirty each morning.

He hung up his coat and put his lunch in the kitchen fridge. Then he sat at his desk and turned on his computer. He read his emails before he began work. There were questions from customers and notes from his team.

"Good morning, Tom," said Emma Brown as she came past his desk.

"Good morning," Tom said. He smiled, but he did not say more.

Tom was good at his work. He checked numbers and made clear documents for customers. He saw small mistakes that other people did not see. But he did not often speak in meetings. He had ideas, but he kept them in his head.

At ten, the team went to the meeting room. Sarah Miller, their manager, sat at the head of the table. Mark Davis was there too. He had worked at Brightway Solutions for many years.

"We need a better way to send our weekly reports," Sarah said. "What do you think?"

Tom had an idea. The team could use one short form for all the reports. He looked at his notes. Mark spoke first, then Emma. Tom waited. Soon Sarah moved to the next question.

After the meeting, Tom went to the kitchen. He filled a cup with tea. Emma came in to wash her cup.

"You had a good idea in the meeting, didn't you?" she asked.

Tom looked at her. "How do you know?"

"You wrote it in your notes. I saw."

"I was not sure when to say it," Tom said.

"You can say it next time," Emma told him. "People need to hear your ideas."

Tom took his tea back to his desk. He opened the report files and checked them one by one. Before lunch, he found two small mistakes. He sent a kind email to the team and showed them how to fix the mistakes.

In the afternoon, he answered more emails and put new papers in the right files. At five, most people went home. Tom checked his desk, put on his coat, and left too. On the way home, he thought about Emma's words. He wanted to speak more, but he did not yet know how.

1 A New Project

The next morning, Tom came into the office at his usual time. He put his lunch in the fridge and read his emails. One email was from Sarah. It said, "Can you come to my office at nine thirty?"

Tom looked at the time. It was nine twenty-five. He took his notebook and walked to Sarah's room. She was at her desk, reading a paper.

"Good morning, Tom," she said. "Please sit down."

"Good morning," he replied. He sat and held his notebook on his knees.

"We have a new customer," Sarah said. "Their name is Green Street Cafe. They want a new way to plan staff hours and keep simple sales records. We need to start soon."

Tom knew the work. He had helped other customers with staff lists and sales papers. He knew how the computer tools worked too.

"I want you to lead this project," Sarah said.

Tom blinked. "Me?"

"Yes. You know this kind of work. You listen well, and you check details. You can learn the things you do not know."

"I am not sure I can lead a project," Tom said. His voice was low.

Sarah leaned forward. "You do not have to know everything now. A leader asks questions and helps the team work together. I will help you."

Tom looked at his notebook. He felt both proud and afraid. "I have a question," he said. "When does the customer need the first plan?"

"Next Friday," Sarah said. "We will have a team meeting tomorrow. Mark and Emma will work with you."

"All right," Tom said. "I will try."

On the way back to his desk, Tom read his notes again. He wrote down the customer name and the date. Then he opened the work files. He began to think of all the things that needed to be done.

Emma came by with a cup of tea. "Sarah told me about the project," she said. "That is great news."

"I hope I can do it well," Tom said.

"You can ask us for help," Emma said. "We are a team."

Tom nodded. But when he returned to his screen, he began to make a long list in his notebook. He wanted to be ready for the meeting. The new work felt big, and he wanted to do everything right.

2 The First Meeting

On Wednesday morning, Tom came to work early. He put his coat away and read the notes he had made the night before. He had a list of questions for Green Street Cafe and a simple plan for the first week.

At ten, he went to the meeting room. Sarah was already there. Emma sat beside her. Mark came in last and put his phone on the table.

"Good morning," Sarah said. "Tom will lead the Green Street Cafe project. Tom, can you tell us your first ideas?"

Tom looked down at his notes. His hands felt warm. He took a breath.

"First, we should ask the cafe about its staff hours and sales," he said. His voice was quiet, but everyone heard him. "Then we can make a simple plan for the computer tools."

"Can you say that again, please?" Mark asked. "Are you ready to lead this? It is an important customer."

Tom stopped. He looked at the paper in front of him.

"Mark," Sarah said, "Tom knows this work. We are here to help the project go well."

"I think Tom's first idea is good," Emma said. "The cafe needs to tell us how it works now. Then we can make a plan that fits."

Tom looked at Emma and then at Sarah. "Thank you," he said.

"What do you think about asking the cafe to send one week of sales records?" Tom asked. This time, his voice was a little stronger.

"That will help," Emma said.

Mark looked at his notes. "Yes. We also need to ask who will use the new tool."

"Good point," said Tom. He wrote it down. "Can you help me make a list of questions, Mark?"

Mark was quiet for a moment. Then he nodded. "Yes. I can do that."

Together, they made a short list. Emma wrote down the staff questions. Mark added questions about sales. Tom checked each one and put the questions in a clear order.

"Let's make a plan," Tom said. He spoke more clearly now. He gave Emma the job of writing to the cafe. He asked Mark to check the work tools. Tom would put the answers and the steps in one document.

The meeting ended at eleven. Tom walked back to his desk with the notes. He felt nervous still, but he also felt pleased. His team had useful ideas. He did not have to find every answer alone.

3 Work for One

After the meeting, Tom began the project work. He wrote the questions for the cafe and made the first document. Then he checked the work tools, even though Mark had said he would do that.

At lunch, Tom ate a sandwich at his desk. Emma came to ask him to join her in the kitchen, but he shook his head.

"I need to finish this," he said.

"You have time," Emma told him. "We have only just started."

Tom smiled and went back to the document. He wanted the first work to be very good.

In the afternoon, he made a table for the staff hours. He checked the customer notes and wrote an email to Sarah. Then he read Mark's list of questions and changed some words to make them clear.

At five thirty, people began to leave. Mark put on his coat.

"You can check my tool notes tomorrow," Mark said. "I will send them this evening."

"Thank you," Tom replied. He was tired, but he kept working.

At six, the office was quiet. Tom read the customer papers again. He found that one date in his document was wrong. He had written Thursday, but the cafe meeting was on Friday.

Tom felt bad. He changed the date and sent Sarah a note. "I'm sorry I made a mistake. I have fixed the date in the document."

Sarah came to his desk a few minutes later. "I saw your note," she said. "Thank you for fixing it. But why are you still here?"

"I want to finish everything," Tom said.

"You cannot do everything alone," Sarah replied. "A leader shares work. Mark can check the tools. Emma can write to the cafe. You can bring the work together and make sure we all know what is next."

Tom looked at his desk. It was covered with papers.

"I don't understand," he said. "If I give work to other people, how do I know it will be right?"

"You can talk to them, check the work, and help if there is a problem," Sarah said. "Sharing work does not mean you stop caring about it."

Tom read his task list. He had written his own name beside every job.

"What do you think?" Sarah asked.

Tom crossed out his name beside the tool check and wrote Mark's name. He wrote Emma's name beside the cafe email. He kept the main plan for himself.

"I think this is better," he said.

"Good," Sarah said. "Now go home. You have done enough today."

Tom packed his papers and put on his coat. He sent Emma and Mark a short message about their tasks. Then he left the office.

He was tired, but the work felt lighter. Tomorrow, he would listen to his team and ask for help when he needed it. That was one small step up.

4 A Mistake in the Report

Tom opened the report on his computer. It was for the new town guide project. He had worked on it for most of the morning. He checked the pages and the numbers. Then he sent it to David Clark, the office manager.

A few minutes later, David came to Tom’s desk. He held a printed page in his hand. “Tom, can you look at this number?” he asked. “The cost for printing is wrong. We need a new report today.”

Tom looked at the page. He saw his mistake at once. He had put the cost for one thousand books, not five hundred books. “I’m sorry,” he said. “I made a mistake. I will make a new report.”

David nodded. “Thank you for telling me. Please check all the numbers before you send it again.” Then he went back to his office.

Tom felt bad. He looked at the screen, but his mind felt busy. Sarah came to his desk with a cup of tea. “You look worried,” she said. “What is wrong?”

“I sent David a report with a wrong number,” Tom said. “He needs a new one today. I should have checked it more carefully.”

Sarah sat beside him. “It is good that you told him. Now we can fix it. Can you help me check the new report?” Tom asked. “Yes,” she said. “Let’s do it together.”

Tom changed the cost and checked each number again. Sarah read the report from the first page to the last. She found one more small mistake in a date. Tom changed that too.

When they finished, Tom printed the new report. Sarah checked the numbers on the page. They were right. Tom sent it to David.

David read the report at his desk. Then he came back. “This is clear, Tom. The numbers are right now. Thank you for fixing it so quickly.”

Tom felt much better. “Thank you for checking it with me,” he said to Sarah. “I learned that I need to check every number, even when I am in a hurry.”

Sarah smiled. “We all make mistakes. The important thing is to be honest and put them right.” Tom saved the report in the project folder. Then he opened his task list and began the next piece of work.

5 Good Ideas

The next morning, Tom looked at the project task list. There was a lot to do. The team needed to check local places, write short notes and plan the pages of the town guide.

Tom wanted to know what everyone could do. He went to Mark’s desk. “Can I ask you about the work?” Tom said. “What have you done on projects like this before?”

Mark turned from his screen. “I have worked on two town guides,” he said. “I know how to check place details. I can also call the shops and ask for the right names and times.”

“That will help us,” said Tom. He wrote Mark’s name beside the place checks. Mark showed him a list from an old project. It had the names of places and phone numbers. “We can use this to start,” Mark said.

Tom thanked Mark and went to Emma’s desk. She had a paper plan beside her computer. Each task had a day and a time next to it.

“Emma, how do you keep your work in order?” Tom asked. “I have a question about our plan.”

Emma showed him the paper. “I put small tasks into the week. I leave time to check the work at the end. Then I can see if we are late before it is too late.”

Tom looked at the plan. It was easy to read. “That is a good idea. Can we use this way for the team?” he asked. Emma nodded. “Yes. We can put the tasks on one shared list.”

Tom asked Sarah what she thought. She said, “We should put the most important work first. Then we can see what needs help.”

Tom wrote the team’s ideas on the list. Mark would check places and call shops. Emma would set up the week plan. Sarah would check the words and dates. Tom would bring the work together and speak with David.

They looked at the list together. Mark pointed to the place checks. “I can start with the town centre today,” he said. Emma added times for each task and a check at the end of the week.

“Thank you,” Tom said. “Now we know who is doing what.” The team began work. Mark called the first shop. Emma shared the plan. Sarah checked the notes from the last meeting. Tom felt more sure about leading the project.

6 The Late Delivery

On Wednesday morning, Tom checked the project list. The team needed new photos of shops and town places. A local photo shop was making the picture cards for the guide. The cards were due that day.

Mark called the shop. He listened for a moment, then put down the phone. “The cards are late,” he said. “They will not come until Friday.”

Tom looked at the calendar. The team had to finish the guide on Monday. They needed the cards to check the pictures and names. “Can we use the files on the computer?” Tom asked.

Mark shook his head. “Not for all the pictures. The shop has one set of files, and they are not ready. We cannot finish the picture pages today.”

Tom went to Sarah and Emma. “The cards are late. We have a short time left, and one part cannot continue,” he said. “Let’s make a plan together.”

The four team members sat around a table. Tom placed the task list in the middle. “What can we do while we wait?” he asked.

Sarah looked at the page list. “We can check the words on the pages that do not need photos,” she said. “I can do that.”

Emma pointed to her week plan. “We can also check the place times and phone numbers now. Then we will not need to stop for that later.” Mark looked at the list. “I think we should wait for the cards. Changing the order may make more work,” he said.

Tom listened. “I understand,” he said. “What do you think will happen if the cards are still late on Friday?” Mark was quiet for a moment. “Then we will have less time to check the guide,” he said.

“Could we do the other checks now and keep the picture pages for Friday?” Tom asked. “That way, the team can keep working.”

Mark looked at Sarah and Emma. “Yes, that could work. I can call the shop each morning. I can also check the shop names while I do my other calls.”

The team agreed. Emma changed the plan. Sarah took the words. Mark took the calls and place checks. Tom wrote to David to explain the late cards and the new plan. David replied, “Good. Keep me up to date.”

7 A Plan That Works

On Thursday morning, Tom stood by the team table. He had the new plan on a sheet of paper. “Good morning,” he said. “The cards are still late, so we will keep to our plan.”

He gave Sarah the pages to check. He gave Emma the list of place times and phone numbers. Mark would call the photo shop and check the names of the shops in the guide. Tom would check the work and speak to David.

“Can you check this list when you finish?” Tom asked Emma. “Yes,” she said. “I will mark each place when I check it.”

Sarah opened her pages. “I have a question,” she said. “Do we write ‘open at nine’ or ‘opens at nine’?” Tom read the line. “Use ‘opens at nine’ for the shop. Thank you for asking.”

The team began. Tom walked between the desks to see how the work was going. Mark was on the phone to the photo shop. “They say the cards will come tomorrow morning,” he told Tom.

Emma had checked the first list. She found two wrong phone numbers and changed them. Sarah checked the words and found a date that did not match the plan.

Tom wrote each change on the task list. “Can you tell me when your part is ready?” he asked. Sarah and Emma both said yes. Mark added the new delivery time to the plan.

At lunch, Tom checked the list again. Most of the work was done. He sent David a short message with the news. David replied, “Thank you. This is a clear update.”

On Friday, the cards arrived. Mark brought them to the team table. “They are here,” he said. The team checked each picture against the names and notes.

One card showed a shop with an old name. Mark called the shop and got the right name. Sarah changed the words. Emma added the final check to her plan. Tom looked over the pages and made sure each task was marked done.

David came to see the finished guide. He read several pages. “This is ready for me to send to the printer,” he said. “Good work, everyone.”

Tom looked at his team. “Thank you for your hard work,” he said. Mark smiled. “Your plan gave us work to do while we waited.” Tom felt proud. He had listened, shared the tasks and checked the work. Now the guide could go to the printer on time.

8 Ready for the Customer

On Monday morning, the team met at Brightway Solutions. The office was in a small town in England. Rain ran down the windows, and the kettle made a soft sound in the kitchen.

“Good morning,” said Tom. He put his bag by his desk. “Is the final report ready?”

“Nearly,” said Sarah. “We need to check the last page and make the slides for the meeting.”

Mark brought a list of the work. Emma opened the report on her computer. David came over from his office.

“The customer will be here tomorrow,” David said. “Please show them what we did and how the plan helped.”

Tom looked at the notes in his hand. He was glad to be part of the meeting, but his stomach felt tight. He had not spoken to a customer before.

Emma saw his face. “Are you nervous?” she asked.

“A little,” Tom said. “I want to do well, but I may forget what to say.”

“We can practise together,” Emma said. “Can you help me check the slides first?”

They checked each page. Sarah found a date that was not the same in the report and the slides. Mark checked the list of jobs and found that every job was complete.

“I'm sorry, I made a mistake with this date,” Tom said. “I will change it now.”

“Thank you for telling us,” said Sarah. “Now the two pages match.”

David read the report again. “The work is clear. Let's make a plan for the talk. Tom, you can explain the first part. Sarah can talk about the dates, and Mark can answer questions about the job list.”

Tom nodded. “I have a question. What if the customer asks about a number I do not know?”

“Do not guess,” said David. “Say you do not know, then tell them how we will find the answer.”

After lunch, Emma stood by the screen with Tom. He tried his first part. He spoke too fast and missed a page.

“I don't understand this part when you speak so quickly,” Emma said kindly. “Can you try again?”

Tom took a breath and began again. This time he used short sentences and stopped at each page.

Sarah listened from her desk. “Speak slowly and use simple words,” she said. “People need time to follow you.”

Tom tried once more. It was better. He still felt nervous, but now he knew what to do next. He went home with his notes and read them aloud before bed.

9 The Presentation

The next morning, the team set out the chairs in the meeting room. They put water on the table and turned on the screen. The customer, Ms Green, arrived at ten.

“Good morning,” said David. “Thank you for coming to Brightway Solutions.”

“Good morning,” Ms Green said. “I am pleased to see your work.”

Tom stood beside the screen. His hands felt cold. He looked at Emma, who smiled, and then at Sarah, who gave him a small nod.

“Good morning,” Tom said. He spoke slowly. “We made a plan for your office jobs. First, we wrote down each job. Then we chose who would do it and when.”

He showed the next page. “This list helped us see what was done and what still needed work. We checked it each day. When one job was late, we spoke about it and changed the plan.”

Ms Green looked at the screen. “That is clear,” she said.

Tom felt a little calmer. He turned to the next page and explained how the team shared news each day. He did not use long words. He gave one example from the work.

At the end, Ms Green asked, “How many hours did the new plan save us last month?”

Tom looked at his notes. The number was not there. He felt the old fear return, but he remembered David’s words.

“I don't know that number now,” Tom said. “I do not want to give you a wrong answer. We can check the work records and send you the number tomorrow.”

“That is fine,” Ms Green said. “I would rather have the right number.”

Sarah then explained the dates in the report. Mark showed the full list of jobs and how the team checked each one. David answered a question about the next month.

Ms Green looked over the pages again. “Your work is easy to follow,” she said. “The daily list helped our staff know what to do. We are happy with the plan.”

Tom felt his shoulders relax. The meeting was not over, but the hard part was done.

“Thank you,” said David. “We will check the hours and send them tomorrow.”

After Ms Green left, the team put away the chairs. Tom sat down and drank some water.

“I was nervous,” he said, “but I could still speak.”

“You did,” Emma said. “And you gave an honest answer.” Sarah smiled. “That is what people need to hear.”

10 A Step Forward

On Wednesday, Tom checked the work records with Sarah. They found the right number of hours and sent it to Ms Green. She wrote back to say thank you.

At the team meeting, David stood by the white board. “The customer is happy with our work,” he said. “The report was clear, and the meeting went well.”

“Thank you,” said Sarah. “Everyone did their part.”

David looked at the team. “The daily notes and the clear plan made a big difference. We knew what was done, and we could see what was next.”

Mark turned to Tom. “You did a good job in the meeting,” he said. “You prepared, and you kept going when you did not know the number.”

Tom smiled. “Thank you. I was worried I would forget everything.”

“You worked hard,” Mark said. “I respect the effort you put in.”

Tom felt proud, but he knew there was still more to learn. He had needed help from Emma, Sarah, Mark and David. He was glad they had worked as a team.

Sarah looked at the notes from the first day of the project. “We have learned a lot,” she said. “At the start, our job list was not easy to follow. Now we check it every day, and we tell each other when a problem comes up.”

“I like that we speak sooner now,” said Emma. “It gives us time to fix small problems.”

David asked, “What do you think we should do better next time?”

Tom took a moment. “We can ask the customer more questions at the start. Then we will know what they need before we make the plan.”

Sarah wrote this on the board. “Good idea,” she said. “We can use it for our next job.”

Later, Tom spoke with David in the hall. “I would like to learn more about talking to people and leading a small team,” Tom said. “Can we make a plan for that?”

“Yes,” said David. “You can help lead our next short meeting. I will be there to support you, and Sarah can give you advice after.”

“Thank you,” Tom said. “I want to get better, but I know I have more to learn.”

“That is a good way to start,” David said. “A manager listens, plans, and learns from the team. You do not need to know everything today.”

Tom went back to his desk. He was not a perfect manager, and he did not expect to be one soon. But he had spoken clearly, helped the team, and taken on a new task. It was a small step, but it was the first step up.
