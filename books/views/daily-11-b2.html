0 A Fresh Start on Monday

By Saturday afternoon, Emily Parker had carried the last box up three flights of stairs and into her new flat. It was small enough that she could see the kitchen sink from her bed, but the windows looked out over a busy street in the city. Buses sighed at the stop below. People walked past with paper cups and shopping bags, moving as if they already knew exactly where they were going.

Emily set the box marked KITCHEN beside the counter and sat on it. Her legs ached. She had left her small town that morning with two suitcases, a houseplant and a plan to begin again. The plan had seemed clear while she was at home. Now the flat smelled of cardboard, and she could not remember which box held the kettle.

Her phone buzzed. It was a message from her mother, Lucy: How are you settling in? Have you eaten something?

Emily smiled. She typed, Getting there. I’ll unpack and find a supermarket. She nearly added, I’m not sure I’ve made the right choice, but deleted the words before sending the message.

The kettle was in the second box she opened. She made tea, then used her phone to find the nearest supermarket. The map showed that it was a twelve-minute walk away. The route took her down a crowded street, past a barber’s shop, a pharmacy and a row of cafés. At each crossing, people seemed to move before the lights changed. Emily waited until she was sure, then hurried after them.

Inside the supermarket, she took a basket and tried to shop for a whole week. The shelves seemed to offer too many choices. She compared two kinds of bread, chose the cheaper one, and then spent five minutes looking for milk. At the checkout, she realised she had forgotten washing-up liquid. The cashier told her she could buy it at the small shop next door. Emily thanked her, feeling silly for having to ask.

Back at the flat, she put away her food and unpacked one more box. The room began to look less temporary. She placed her books on the narrow shelf and set the plant on the windowsill, where it could catch the afternoon light. Her mother called while Emily was folding clothes.

“You sound tired,” Lucy said.

“I am. But I’ve got the important things done.”

“Good. You don’t have to solve the whole city this weekend.”

Emily laughed softly. “It feels as if I should.”

“You’ll learn it one street at a time.”

After the call, Emily opened a message from her old friend Ben. He had written, First night in the big city! Are you being brave? She answered, I bought milk without getting lost. That counts.

On Sunday morning, she tried the bus route she would use for work. The first bus arrived full, so she stood near the door and watched the city pass through the window. At the next stop, she got off too early and had to walk the last few streets. She was annoyed with herself until she found Northbridge Solutions. The company occupied two floors of a glass building among several similar offices.

Emily checked the entrance on her phone and took a picture of the sign, as if she might need proof that she had found it. Then she followed the route back to her flat. Later, she cleaned the kitchen, prepared a simple meal and ironed a blouse for Monday.

That evening, the flat was quiet. Emily could hear a neighbour’s television through the wall and the steady sound of traffic below. She put her alarm on, checked it twice, and set her work bag beside the door.

Monday was close now. She felt nervous, but beneath the nerves was something else: the small, steady sense that she had arrived.

1 The First Morning

Emily woke before her alarm on Monday. She dressed carefully, ate toast at the kitchen counter and left with time to spare. The bus came quickly, but it filled at the second stop. She stood between a man with a bicycle helmet and a woman reading messages, holding the rail as the bus turned through traffic.

At Northbridge Solutions, the glass entrance reflected the grey morning sky. Emily arrived twenty-five minutes early. In the lobby, she checked the floor number from the welcome email and went to reception. A receptionist called Priya smiled when Emily gave her name.

“You’re early,” Priya said. “That’s better than getting lost.”

Emily laughed, relieved that someone had made the first comment. Priya called upstairs, then pointed her towards a row of chairs. Emily sat with her bag on her knees and watched people pass through the lobby, holding travel mugs and speaking into headsets.

A few minutes later, a woman with dark hair came down the stairs. “Emily? I’m Sarah Mitchell. Welcome to Northbridge.” Sarah spoke warmly and shook her hand. “I’m your manager. Come up and I’ll show you where everything is.”

The office was open plan, with rows of desks, low screens and meeting rooms along the windows. The air smelled of coffee and warm computers. Sarah introduced Emily to James Wilson, who sat at the next desk. He was looking at a large spreadsheet on two monitors.

“James knows where all the strange numbers are hiding,” Sarah said.

“Only because they keep hiding from me,” he replied. He stood to greet Emily, then moved a stack of papers from the spare chair. “Make yourself comfortable.”

Olivia Chen joined them on the way to the kitchen. She wore a green cardigan and carried a mug with a chipped handle. “I’m on the client support team,” she said. “If you get lost, ask me. If you get lost in the shared drive, ask James.”

“That’s a large area to get lost in,” James called after them.

Sarah showed Emily the kitchen, where a calendar listed team meetings and who had brought biscuits. She explained the coffee machine, the fridge and the cupboard for spare stationery. Then she pointed out the meeting rooms and the printer. Emily tried to remember every instruction.

At the desk opposite the aisle, Michael Brooks was speaking quietly to someone on a video call. Sarah introduced him when he finished. He was the senior administrator and had been with Northbridge for several years. He greeted Emily politely and told her that he kept a list of useful phone extensions beside his screen.

“It’s not elegant,” he said, tapping the handwritten page, “but it works.”

A man from another team stopped by with a laptop under his arm. Sarah introduced him as Daniel Foster, who worked in operations. He nodded to Emily and said he was sure they would cross paths when her team needed information from his.

Sarah opened the company calendar on Emily’s computer and talked through the week. There was a Monday team meeting, a client review on Wednesday and a report deadline on Friday. She showed Emily the team chat and the shared documents folder. The folder names looked clear at first, until Sarah opened several at once.

“You’ll get used to the system,” Sarah said. “There’s a lot to take in, so don’t worry if it feels like a flood today.” Then, almost immediately, she began explaining which client folders were current, how the weekly report was named and where old versions were stored.

Emily nodded and wrote quickly in her notebook. She wanted to ask Sarah to slow down, but her manager had already moved to the next screen. Emily told herself she could read her notes later.

At half past nine, Sarah asked her to join the team meeting. Emily carried her notebook into a glass room and chose a chair near Olivia. The city moved beyond the windows, bright with the morning rush. Emily opened to a clean page and waited for the meeting to begin.

2 A Sheet of Numbers

The Monday meeting began with a list of updates. Sarah asked each person to say what they were working on and whether anything might delay it. James described a set of account figures he was checking. Olivia mentioned two clients who needed answers before Wednesday. Michael said the new records system was still sending some forms to the wrong folder.

“I can look at the weekly client sheet,” Sarah said. She turned to Emily. “You can update the contact details and prepare a short report for the Friday review. It’s a good way to learn our files.”

Emily’s stomach tightened. She had used spreadsheets at her last job, but not this company’s system. She looked at the document title on Sarah’s screen and tried to remember which of the folders had been called Current and which had been called Current Final.

“Can you take a look at the figures for the Northstar accounts too?” Sarah added. “Some rows are incomplete. The report uses the same totals.” She gave a short explanation about filters, colour codes and the monthly comparison. People nodded as if the details were familiar.

Emily opened her notebook. Her page already held a dozen hurried lines, written at different angles. She could have asked what counted as an incomplete row, but the conversation had moved on to a question from Olivia. Emily decided she would work it out once she was at her desk.

Back in the open-plan area, she found a team chat message from Sarah with links to the shared files. Emily clicked the first one. The spreadsheet opened in a browser window with coloured tabs, several filters and columns of dates, names and account values. A note at the top said, Please use the live version.

She read the note twice. Then she noticed another file beside it with almost the same name, followed by the words old layout. Emily assumed the live version was the correct one. She opened a second document, a client report template, and began filling in information from the spreadsheet.

At first, the task felt manageable. She checked each company name against a contact list and corrected two phone numbers. After an hour, she had completed three pages of the report. She was pleased to be working quickly.

Then the figures became harder. Some accounts had no value in the current month but did have one in the previous month. Others showed a dash. Emily could not tell whether a dash meant zero, missing information or something else. She thought about asking James, but he was on a call. Sarah was in a meeting room with the door closed.

Emily remembered a formula Sarah had mentioned. She copied a calculation from one row into the rows below. The totals changed, and the numbers looked sensible. She did not notice that one filter was still hiding several accounts.

Just before lunch, Olivia stopped by with her mug. “How are you getting on?”

“Fine, I think. The contact details are updated. I’m checking the totals now.”

Olivia looked at the screen. “Which version are you using?”

Emily pointed to the live file.

“Good. Make sure you don’t change the source figures without checking them. James normally handles those. The report can make a blank look like a zero if the formula isn’t set up right.”

Emily felt heat rise to her face. “I haven’t changed the source figures,” she said, though she had copied the formula into the sheet.

Olivia glanced at the screen again. “All right. I’ll get James to have a look when he’s free. It’s easy to miss something in these files.” She did not sound angry, which made Emily feel worse, not better.

At lunch, Emily sat with Olivia and Michael in the kitchen. She listened as they discussed a delayed delivery and a film they had seen. She wanted to tell them she had made a mistake, but she was no longer sure what the mistake was. Michael asked how she was finding the city.

“Busy,” Emily said. “I’m still learning the buses.”

“It takes a while,” he said. “I still check the route when I go somewhere new.”

His ordinary answer eased her embarrassment. After lunch, Emily returned to her desk and opened her notes. She found the words source sheet—ask James, written near the bottom of the page. She had written them during Sarah’s quick tour and forgotten them.

When James came back from his call, Emily watched him open the spreadsheet. She could ask then. Instead, she waited until he had finished and started another task. By the time she turned to speak, he was already reading an email.

The afternoon passed with the unanswered question sitting between Emily and her screen. At four o’clock, Sarah messaged the team: Please send me a brief progress update before you leave. Emily stared at the chat box. She had work to report, but she did not know whether it was right.

3 The Wrong Version

Emily wrote that the contact details were updated and the report was in progress. She considered adding that she had questions about the figures, but the sentence seemed too long. She sent the shorter message and waited. Sarah replied with a thumbs-up and, Great, thanks.

That night, Emily made pasta in the small kitchen and ate at the narrow table beside her unpacked boxes. Her phone buzzed with a message from Lucy: First day finished. What was the best part? Emily looked at the quiet flat and the open spreadsheet still in her thoughts.

She typed, People were kind. Then she added, I have a lot to learn. Lucy replied, Everyone does on their first week. Don’t judge yourself after one day.

Emily wanted to believe her. She put her phone down and washed the dishes. Outside, buses crossed the junction, their lights moving across the ceiling. She decided she would ask James about the spreadsheet first thing in the morning.

On Tuesday, James was already at his desk when Emily arrived. She waited until he finished reading a message, then walked over with her notebook.

“Could I check something about the Northstar sheet?” she asked. “I’m not sure what the dashes mean, and I copied a formula into a few rows yesterday.”

James turned from his screen. “Thanks for telling me. Let’s look together.” He opened the file and checked the changes. His face was serious, but not unfriendly. “You’ve overwritten the formulas in these rows. It’s fixable, but we need to see what the original figures were.”

Emily felt her cheeks warm. “I’m sorry. I thought I was helping complete the sheet.”

“I understand why you thought that,” James said. “The file doesn’t make the difference clear. The source sheet is protected, but only in some columns. I’ll restore the formulas from the earlier version and check that nothing else changed.”

He opened the shared documents folder and found two versions with nearly identical names. He studied their dates, then frowned slightly.

“The old layout was updated after the report template changed. Sarah may have sent you the wrong link by mistake. It happens when we’re moving quickly.”

Emily felt a small relief, followed by another wave of worry. She had still copied the formula without asking. “I should have checked with you.”

“Yes,” James said. “But you have now. That’s what matters.” He showed her how the team marked input cells and where the instructions for the spreadsheet were stored. The instructions were only a page long, and a note explained that dashes meant information had not yet been confirmed.

By mid-morning, James had restored the affected rows. He asked Emily to compare the account list with the current contact details and to write down anything that did not match. She did so carefully, checking each line twice. This time, when she found an unclear entry, she added a comment in the shared document instead of changing it.

At the team meeting, Sarah asked whether the report was on track. Emily felt the familiar urge to say yes. She looked at James, who was reading his notes, then decided not to wait for someone else to notice the problem.

“The contact details are updated,” she said. “I made a change to some formula rows yesterday because I misunderstood the sheet. James has restored them, and I’m checking the figures against the current list. I still need to confirm how we treat unconfirmed accounts.”

Sarah paused. “Thank you for raising it. I should have explained the source sheet more clearly. The blank and dash entries should stay unconfirmed in this report. James, can you confirm that the restored rows are all right?”

“I’m checking now,” he said. “I’ll let you know by lunch.”

Sarah looked back at Emily. “If a task is unclear, ask before you spend too long guessing. I sometimes give too much information at once, especially when I’m thinking about three deadlines. Please stop me.”

Emily was surprised. She had expected a warning. “All right,” she said. “I’ll ask sooner.”

The meeting moved on to Wednesday’s client review. Olivia explained that Northstar had requested an updated list of contacts, while Michael mentioned that two client forms were still missing. The discussion gave Emily a better sense of why the report mattered. Someone would use it to decide whom to contact and what information they still needed.

After the meeting, Sarah sent Emily a message with the correct link to the report template. James confirmed that the formulas were restored. Emily checked the list again, adding three comments for entries that needed confirmation. She did not feel confident yet, but the work now had clear steps.

At the end of the day, she sent Sarah a fuller update: the contact list was checked, the formula issue had been corrected with James, and three entries still needed answers. Sarah replied, Thanks for being clear. I’ll ask Olivia about those accounts tomorrow.

Emily packed her notebook and shut down her computer. The mistake had taken time from the team, and that mattered. But it had also become a conversation instead of a secret she had to protect.

4 A Better Question

Wednesday began with a delay on the bus. A road had been closed, and the driver took a longer route through streets Emily did not know. She checked the map twice and arrived at Northbridge Solutions seven minutes later than she had planned. She expected to feel flustered, but Sarah was still speaking to Priya at reception when Emily came upstairs.

“Traffic?” Sarah asked when she reached her desk.

“A road closure. I left early, but the bus had to turn back.”

“Thanks for letting me know. The city likes to keep us guessing.” Sarah pointed towards the meeting rooms. “We’re starting the Northstar review in ten minutes.”

Emily sat down and opened the correct report. She had checked the figures with James and added comments beside the three accounts that needed confirmation. One item still troubled her: a client had two contact names, and she could not tell which person should receive the next update. The old notes suggested one name; the most recent email suggested the other.

For a moment, she thought about choosing the newer name and moving on. Then she remembered the time she had spent trying to fix the spreadsheet after guessing. She walked to Olivia’s desk.

“Could you help me with one Northstar contact?” Emily asked. “The shared list has Helen Marsh, but the latest email is signed by Ravi Patel. I can’t tell who should get the report.”

Olivia looked up from her screen. “Good question. That account changed hands last month, but the records weren’t updated. Let me check the email thread.” She searched the shared folder, then found a message from the client confirming that Ravi was the new contact.

“You’re right to flag it,” Olivia said. “I’ll update the list after the meeting. Don’t send the report to either person until we’ve confirmed which version they need.”

In the meeting room, Sarah sat beside James, while Michael joined with a printed page of account notes. Daniel Foster arrived from operations with a tablet. He was there because his team managed the process that sent forms to clients.

Sarah introduced the purpose of the meeting. She said Northstar needed a clear update before Friday, but the company should not send unconfirmed information simply to meet the deadline. Daniel explained that some contact changes arrived through an operations form and were not automatically copied into the team’s spreadsheet.

“So the form can be complete in one place and missing in another,” Emily said.

Daniel nodded. “Exactly. We’ve been trying to improve the handover, but the change takes time. If you find a gap, send it to me with the account name and the date you noticed it.”

Emily wrote that down. The process sounded frustrating for everyone, not only for her. Michael added that he had received several forms late, after clients had already sent follow-up emails. No one blamed one person. They began listing the points where information could be delayed.

Sarah asked Emily to show the report. Emily shared her screen and explained that she had checked the contact details, restored rows with James’s help and left three comments for items that still needed confirmation. She also mentioned the Helen Marsh and Ravi Patel question.

“That’s useful,” Sarah said. “We can correct the list before we send the final copy.” She asked James to verify the totals once more and Olivia to confirm the client contacts. Daniel offered to check whether the operations forms for the three accounts had arrived.

The meeting ended with work divided clearly. The deadline remained Friday, but Sarah agreed that the team would send a short note to the client if one answer was still missing. Emily felt the pressure of the deadline, yet it no longer seemed like a single test she had to pass alone.

After the meeting, Daniel stopped beside her desk. “I’m glad you spotted that name. We need people to question the records, not just accept them.”

“I wasn’t sure if I was making trouble.”

“You weren’t. A clear question saves time. A guess can create more work for everyone.” He smiled. “I’ve learned that the hard way.”

At lunch, Emily sat with Olivia and Michael in the kitchen. Olivia told a story about getting on the wrong bus during her first week in the city. Michael admitted that he had once sent a calendar invitation to the whole company instead of his team. Emily laughed more freely than she had on Monday.

“The city still surprises me,” she said. “But I managed to find a small café near my flat last night.”

“That’s a good discovery,” Olivia said. “There are plenty of places to try once you know your way around.”

Ben sent Emily a message asking whether she was free at the weekend. She replied that she was, and they began making a plan to meet in the city centre. It felt good to have something beyond work to look forward to.

On Thursday, the team completed the missing checks. Olivia confirmed Ravi Patel as the correct contact. Daniel found one delayed operations form and explained why it had not appeared in the shared folder. James checked the report totals, and Sarah reviewed the wording before it went to the client.

Emily read the final document once more. She had not written every part of it, but her careful contact checks had helped prevent a wrong message. Before sending it, she left one final comment for Sarah about a date that looked different in the email thread.

Sarah checked the source and corrected the date. “Good catch,” she said. “Send it now.”

When Emily clicked the button, the report went out on time. She did not feel suddenly certain of herself. There were still unfamiliar files, quick meetings and crowded buses ahead. But she had asked a clear question, explained a mistake and helped the team make a better decision.

That evening, she walked home instead of taking the bus. The street was busy, but she recognised the bakery on the corner and the turning that led to her flat. She messaged Lucy: Work is getting easier to understand. I’m meeting Ben this weekend.

Lucy replied, One step at a time. Emily looked up at the lit windows above the shops and continued towards home. Monday had not given her a fresh start without problems. It had given her a place to begin.

5 The Format Sarah Expected

By Thursday morning, Emily had begun to recognise the different sounds of Northbridge Solutions. The open-plan office had a low, steady hum: keyboards, quiet conversations and the coffee machine near the kitchen. When the meeting-room door opened, the noise seemed to rise for a moment, then settle again.

Sarah had asked Emily to prepare an update for the weekly client meeting. It was the first time Emily had worked on the report without someone beside her. She had the figures, the notes from Daniel and the previous week's document. She also had Sarah's brief message: “Please use the usual format and have it ready before the meeting.”

Emily opened the shared folder and found several reports with similar names. She chose one that looked recent, copied its headings into a new document and began adding information. There were columns for project, progress, risk and next step. It seemed clear enough. She made the wording neat, checked the dates and felt a small lift of pride when she finished.

At eleven, she looked at the calendar. The meeting was in forty minutes. Emily checked the document again and noticed a second file in the folder. Its name included the words “client update” and a date from the previous week. She opened it.

The layout was different. Sarah's reports used a short summary at the top, followed by three sections: completed work, current concerns and decisions needed. Emily's version was not wrong in itself, but it did not answer the questions the meeting would cover. Worse, she had included a figure from an old spreadsheet. The latest number was in a separate tab that Daniel had updated yesterday.

Emily's face grew warm. She could ask Sarah, but Sarah was speaking to a client in a glass meeting room. The meeting was close, and the document was already late. Emily decided she could fix it herself. She copied the new headings, moved the content, checked the figures and changed the old number. Each change seemed to reveal another small problem.

James rolled his chair over. “You look as if that document is giving you trouble.”

“I used the wrong format,” Emily said. “I think I've fixed it, but I'm not sure about this figure.”

James looked at the spreadsheet and asked which tab she had used. Emily showed him. He pointed to a note beside the newest figure. “Daniel marked this one as confirmed. The other tab is there for reference, but it isn't the one we use in the update.”

He did not sound annoyed. Still, Emily felt embarrassed. “I should have checked sooner.”

“Perhaps,” James said. “But there's a process for these reports. The last one is saved as a template. Sarah usually expects us to start there, not from an older general report.” He showed Emily where the template lived. “And if you aren't sure, ask before you rebuild the whole thing. It saves time for everyone, including you.”

Emily worked quickly while James checked the figures. She finished just before the meeting. Sarah glanced through the report on her screen and nodded. “This is much easier to follow. Thanks for getting it ready.” She did not mention the earlier version, and Emily did not know whether she had seen it.

After the meeting, James returned to his desk. Emily thanked him, but his calmness made her worry more. Perhaps he was being kind because he thought she was incompetent. She watched him reply to messages, then forced herself to turn back to her own work.

That evening, the train was crowded and warm. In her small flat, Emily heated soup and ate at the narrow table by the window. The report had been accepted, but she kept thinking about the time she had lost by trying to fix everything alone. She opened her notebook and wrote one question: “Where is the correct template?” Then she added another: “Which spreadsheet tab is final?”

The questions looked simple on the page. Asking them at the right time seemed harder.

6 A Message Without a Smile

On Friday morning, Emily arrived early and found the office almost empty. She opened the shared drive, located Sarah's template and saved a shortcut to the folder. Then she checked the report one more time. It was already in the meeting folder, but she wanted to be certain she had not missed anything.

At nine, a message appeared from Olivia. “Why isn't the update uploaded yet?”

Emily read it twice. Her shoulders tightened. The words seemed sharp, and the question felt like an accusation. She had uploaded the report the previous afternoon. Perhaps Olivia had seen the earlier version. Perhaps someone had moved the file. Emily immediately opened the meeting folder and saw the document there.

She began typing: “It is uploaded. I already did this yesterday.” Before she could send it, Olivia's status changed to busy. Emily deleted the message and checked the folder again. The file name was correct, but the document was in the client meeting folder, not the main team folder. Olivia might be looking in the usual place.

Emily did not know whether to move it. If she did, someone might use the wrong version. If she left it, Olivia could think she had ignored her. She sent a short reply: “I uploaded it to the client meeting folder yesterday. Are you looking for a copy in the team folder as well?”

Olivia replied a few minutes later. “Yes, please. I couldn't see it in the team folder. We need it there for the wider group.”

The message had no angry words, but Emily still felt foolish. She copied the report into the team folder and added “FINAL” to the file name. Then she stopped. The shared drive already had rules about file names, and she could not remember them. She removed “FINAL” and used the standard date format instead.

At lunch, she sat with Lucy in the small café downstairs. Emily told her what had happened while they waited for sandwiches.

“Was Olivia angry?” Lucy asked.

“I don't know. Her message sounded angry to me.”

“Did she say she was angry?”

“No.”

Lucy smiled. “A short message can sound like anything if you're already worried. You could ask her, or just see what she does next.”

Emily stirred her tea. “I don't want to keep asking people to explain every little thing.”

“You don't have to ask about every little thing,” Lucy said. “But if you need information to do the job, asking is part of doing the job.”

Back at her desk, Emily noticed that Olivia had added a note to the shared document: “Thanks—this is the copy I needed.” It was brief, but it changed the shape of the morning in Emily's mind. Olivia had needed the file in a different place. That was all.

Later, Olivia came over to Emily's desk to discuss a deadline. She spoke in her usual direct way, but she was not cold. “The team folder is the first place people look,” she explained. “The meeting folder is useful for the people in the room, but the wider group needs access too.”

“I see,” Emily said. “I thought the meeting folder was enough.”

“That makes sense. I should have said where I wanted it.” Olivia glanced at the screen. “The name is good. The date makes it easy to find.”

The conversation lasted less than a minute. Emily had spent much of the morning expecting a problem that did not exist.

On her train home, she watched the city slide past the window: shops closing, people hurrying along the pavement and lights coming on in office buildings. In her flat, she wrote a new line in her notebook: “Check the folder location before uploading.” Beneath it she added, “A short message is not always criticism.”

She still wanted to do well. But perhaps doing well did not mean never feeling uncertain. It might mean checking what the uncertainty was really about.

7 The List on Her Desk

By the following Monday, Emily had made a list in the back of her notebook. It was not long, but it was growing. Under “Questions” she had written: Who needs this document? Which folder should it go in? Which spreadsheet tab is current? Under “Deadlines” she had added the time for the weekly update and the day the client figures were due.

She kept the notebook open beside her keyboard. When Sarah asked her to prepare a new report for Friday, Emily paused before saying yes.

“Could I check two things?” she asked. “Should I start from the client update template, and do you want the finished version in both folders?”

Sarah smiled. “Good questions. Start from the template, and save it in the team folder. Then add a link to it in the meeting notes. We only need one main copy.”

Emily wrote that down. Sarah went on to explain that the report needed to be ready by Thursday afternoon, not Friday morning. Emily had understood the date in Sarah's earlier message as the day of the meeting. By asking, she had caught the difference before it caused a late delivery.

The work was not easy. Daniel sent new figures in a spreadsheet, and one of the totals did not match the previous update. Emily checked the notes and saw that a small project had been moved into a different category. She could have changed the number without comment, but she asked Daniel first.

“You've spotted a real change,” he said. “The total is higher because this item is now included. I'll add a note so the next person doesn't have to guess.”

That afternoon, Emily joined a meeting with Sarah, James, Olivia and Daniel. Sarah led the discussion, while Olivia described what the client needed next. Emily kept notes and marked the actions with names and dates. When a deadline sounded unclear, she asked whether it meant the draft or the approved version.

There was a short pause. Then Sarah said, “The approved version. Thanks for checking.” The meeting moved on.

Emily's questions did not make the discussion longer than it needed to be. In fact, when the meeting ended, everyone knew who was doing what. James collected his notes and gave her a quick nod. “That helped,” he said.

In the kitchen, Michael was waiting for the kettle. He had worked at Northbridge Solutions for several years and knew where most things were stored. Emily mentioned her list.

“That's a useful idea,” Michael said. “There are written procedures for some things. The rest is what people learn by watching. Neither is always obvious when you start.”

He told her about an earlier job where he had sent a report to the wrong team. He had been new, and the file had a name that made it seem like the right one. A colleague corrected him, but he still remembered feeling foolish.

“What did you do?” Emily asked.

“I asked them to show me the right route, then I wrote it down. After that, I tried to make it easier for the next new person.” Michael shrugged. “We all miss things. The important part is what we do when we notice.”

He showed Emily the staff page where the team kept useful links and basic processes. It did not answer every question, but it explained how shared documents were named and how meeting notes should be stored. Emily added the page to her list.

After work, the train was delayed, and the platform filled with tired commuters. Emily used the extra time to review her notes. Her list was not a sign that she knew nothing. It was a record of what she had learned so far.

At home, she ate pasta at the small table and called Lucy. They talked about ordinary things: a film Lucy wanted to see, the noisy upstairs neighbour and the weather. Emily did not spend the whole call discussing work. That felt like a small success too.

Before bed, she added one last heading to her notebook: “What I have learned.” Under it, she wrote: “Ask early. Check the owner. Save one main copy.” Then she closed the book and left it ready for the next day.

8 More Than a Desk

On Wednesday evening, Emily left the office later than she had planned. She had spent the last hour checking the report against Daniel's spreadsheet and confirming that the notes matched Sarah's instructions. This time, she had asked Sarah to look at the first section before she completed the rest.

Sarah had replied with two small changes and one question. Emily answered the question, made the changes and finished the report without rebuilding it from the beginning. She felt tired, but the tiredness was different from the panic she had felt after her first mistake.

The train was crowded. A man with a large shopping bag stood near the door, and Emily held her notebook against her coat so she would not lose it. She got off a stop later than usual because she had been thinking about tomorrow's deadlines.

Outside her building, her neighbour Ben Taylor was trying to balance a paper bag and a set of keys. A loaf of bread was sticking out of the top.

“Long day?” he asked.

“A full one,” Emily said. “I'm still learning how everything works.”

Ben lived in the flat across the landing. They had exchanged polite greetings before, but they had never talked for more than a minute. He was about her age and worked in a different part of the city. He asked if she had had a chance to explore beyond her journey to work.

“Not really,” Emily said. “I know the station, the office and the supermarket.”

“That's not much of a city,” he said. “I'm going for a walk on Saturday. There's a riverside market and a bookshop nearby. You could come along, if you like.”

Emily almost said she had too much to do. Then she remembered that her report was due on Thursday, not Saturday. She checked the time in her calendar before answering. “That sounds nice. What time?”

Ben suggested meeting at eleven. They agreed to walk from the station and find somewhere for lunch. Emily felt pleased by the plan, though she was also aware of a quiet voice telling her she ought to spend Saturday catching up on work.

The next morning, she completed the report early and sent Sarah a link to the main copy. She included a short note saying which spreadsheet she had used and where Daniel had confirmed the changed total. Sarah replied, “Thanks, clear and easy to follow.” Emily read it once, then turned to her next task instead of looking for hidden criticism.

At lunch, James asked whether she wanted to join him and a few others in the kitchen. They talked about the weather, a football match and the building work near the station. Olivia told a funny story about getting on the wrong train after a late client meeting. Even Michael joined in, explaining that he once travelled to the end of a line because he was reading a document.

For once, Emily did not feel she had to prove that she belonged by talking about her work. She listened and added a story about missing her stop during her first week. The group laughed with her, not at her.

On Saturday, Ben was waiting downstairs with a small map folded in his pocket. The city looked different in daylight. They passed a row of shops, a narrow street of cafés and an old public building with stone steps. At the market, Ben introduced Emily to a stallholder who sold fruit and flowers. They bought coffee and sat near the river.

“I used to think I needed to know where I was going before I left home,” Emily said. “Now I'm learning that sometimes you can find things by looking around.”

Ben nodded. “That's how I found this place. I took a wrong turn.”

They walked to the bookshop and then shared lunch at a busy café. Emily heard about Ben's job and the way he had changed teams the year before. He had been nervous about asking questions too, especially when everyone else seemed busy.

“Most people are busy,” he said, “but that doesn't mean they want you to guess.”

Back at the flat that evening, Emily was tired in a pleasant way. She prepared food for the next day and set her work bag by the door. Her life was still full of deadlines and unfamiliar details, but it was becoming wider than the journey between home and the office.

9 The Cost of Guessing

On Monday, Emily's list saved her time. She checked the calendar before starting, confirmed the owner of a spreadsheet and sent a question to Daniel about a figure that had changed. His reply came quickly. The number was right, but he had forgotten to update the note beside it.

“Thanks for catching that,” he wrote. “I'll correct the note now.”

Emily felt quietly pleased. She had not avoided work or passed a problem to someone else. She had checked the detail that mattered before it reached the report.

That afternoon, Sarah asked Emily to prepare the first draft of a project summary. She explained the purpose and the deadline, then had to join a call. Emily listened carefully and wrote down the main points. One part was less clear: Sarah said to “use the latest figures,” but she did not say whether she meant the figures from Daniel's spreadsheet or the summary Olivia had shared.

Emily looked at both documents. The totals were different. She could have asked Sarah, but the call had started. She could have asked Daniel, but his status showed that he was presenting. The deadline was not until the next day, and Emily told herself she could work it out.

She chose the summary Olivia had shared because its date was newer. She built the draft around those figures, made a clear table and added notes about the main changes. Near the end of the day, Olivia came by to ask whether the summary was ready to review.

Emily showed her the table. Olivia studied it for a moment. “These are the figures from my planning note. Are they confirmed?”

Emily's stomach tightened. “I thought they were the latest.”

“They are the latest estimate,” Olivia said. “Daniel's spreadsheet has the confirmed figures. For this summary, we need to show both, but we have to label them clearly.” She did not sound angry, only concerned. “Otherwise, someone might treat an estimate as a final number.”

Emily looked again. The difference was not enormous, but it mattered. She had tried to avoid interrupting anyone and had created work that now needed to be changed.

“I'm sorry,” Emily said. “I should have checked what ‘latest’ meant.”

Olivia nodded. “It happens. Let's correct the table now, and then you can ask Sarah whether she wants the same format for the next one.”

They worked together for ten minutes. Olivia explained the difference between an estimate and a confirmed figure, while Emily updated the labels and added the correct number. She did not feel comfortable, but she did not defend her choice. By the time they finished, the summary was accurate and easier to understand.

Sarah returned from her call and looked at the revised draft. Emily explained what had happened. Sarah thanked Olivia for checking the figures and told Emily that she had made a reasonable choice with the information she had. Then she added, “Next time, flag the uncertainty in the document or ask me. You don't need to make the decision alone.”

That advice stayed with Emily after she left the office. She had believed that asking questions would make her seem unprepared. Now she could see that quiet guessing had its own cost: time, confusion and the chance that someone would act on a number that was not final.

On the train, she wrote down the experience in her notebook. Under “Processes,” she added: “Estimate and confirmed are not the same.” Under “Questions,” she wrote: “When figures differ, ask which one the reader needs.”

At home, her flat was quiet. She made tea, opened the window for a few minutes and let the sounds of the city come in. Then she called Lucy. She told her about the mistake, including the part where she had chosen not to ask.

“What will you do next time?” Lucy asked.

“Ask earlier,” Emily said. “Or mark what I don't know instead of pretending I know it.”

Lucy said that sounded sensible. They talked about meeting for dinner later in the week, and Emily put the date in her calendar. She did not need to fill every evening with work to prove she was serious.

Before bed, she looked at the list on her desk. It was longer now, but it did not feel like a record of failure. It showed her where she had been uncertain and what she could do next. On Tuesday, there would be more work, more conversations and probably another detail she did not yet understand. This time, she knew she could ask.

10 A Table at the Window

By Thursday, Emily had learned that lunch in the Northbridge Solutions office could mean several different things. Sometimes people ate at their desks while answering messages. Sometimes they went downstairs for sandwiches. On this particular day, Olivia appeared beside Emily’s chair and pointed towards the lifts.

“Come outside with us,” she said. “If I eat another sandwich over my keyboard, I may become one.”

Emily saved her document and joined Olivia, James and Lucy. They walked to a small café across the street. The city was busy around them, with buses stopping and starting and office workers crossing at the lights. Inside, they found a table beside a window.

James talked about a football match. Lucy described a film she had seen the night before. Olivia listened, but she checked her phone twice before the food arrived.

“Is everything all right?” Emily asked when James went to collect their drinks.

Olivia put her phone face down. “I’m fine. It’s just the Greystone project. The client keeps changing what they want, and the deadline hasn’t moved.”

“I thought you were waiting for their figures,” Lucy said.

“We are. But they also want us to explain the figures we already have, even though two departments sent different versions.” Olivia gave a tired laugh. “I’m trying to be patient. They’re under pressure too. Still, it’s a lot to hold in my head.”

Emily remembered how she had felt when she first joined Northbridge: afraid that asking a question would show she did not belong. Olivia was more experienced, but that did not mean she had no worries.

“Would it help to list which figures are confirmed and which are still waiting?” Emily asked. “I could make a simple table if you want.”

Olivia studied her for a moment. “That might help. I’ve been thinking about the final report, not how to make the current mess clearer. Could you help me check the source files this afternoon?”

“Yes. Let’s agree which folders to use first, so I don’t repeat the problem with the wrong version.”

Olivia smiled. “Good idea. I’ll show you the client’s latest email when we’re back.”

They talked about other things while they ate. Olivia asked about Emily’s flat, and Emily described the narrow kitchen and the noisy pipes. James recommended a nearby market where he bought cheap vegetables. The conversation felt different from the careful talk Emily had practised in her head. Nobody was pretending that work was easy, but nobody was blaming anyone either.

On the walk back, Olivia told Emily more about Greystone. She had promised the client a clear summary before the next meeting, but the client had not decided which figures it wanted included. Olivia was worried that a late change would make her team look careless.

“I can see why you’re under pressure,” Emily said. “If I help with the table, I’ll mark anything uncertain instead of filling in a gap myself.”

“That would be useful,” Olivia replied. “And I’ll tell you if the client changes direction again.”

Back at the office, Olivia opened the project folder on the shared drive and showed Emily the files. They agreed that the latest client email and the approved spreadsheet were the starting points. Emily wrote the file names in her notebook before she began.

The figures did not match in two places. Instead of choosing one, Emily added a note beside each difference and sent Olivia a short message. Olivia checked with the project lead and found that one figure had been updated in an email but not in the spreadsheet.

“You’ve caught something that could have caused a difficult question in the meeting,” Olivia said.

“I only noticed because I checked both sources,” Emily replied. She felt pleased, but not as if she had solved everything. Olivia still had to confirm the figure with the client.

At the end of the day, Emily closed the shared document and made sure her notes were saved. She was tired from the commute and still had to buy food on the way to her flat, but her mind felt quieter than it had the day before.

Olivia stopped at her desk. “Thanks for listening at lunch. I don’t always say when I’m worried.”

“I’m glad you did,” Emily said. “It made it easier to ask what you needed.”

Olivia gave her a small wave and headed for the lift. Emily packed her bag. The work had not become simple, and the Greystone deadline was still close. But she had learned something useful about the people around her: a clear question could make a difficult day feel less lonely.

11 A Quick Conversation

On Friday morning, Daniel came to Emily’s desk with a notebook under his arm. Around them, the open-plan office hummed with phones, quiet conversations and the sound of chairs moving across the floor.

“Have you got a minute?” he asked.

Emily turned away from her screen. “Yes.”

Daniel was preparing a presentation for a client called Westlake. The meeting was scheduled for Monday afternoon, and the client wanted a short review of recent project results. Daniel had the main slides, but he needed a clear set of figures to support them.

“Could you pull together the service totals and the response times from last month?” he asked. “Just the main numbers, not a full report. It would help if you could send them over before the end of Monday morning.”

Emily nodded. She had work to finish for Sarah, but the request sounded manageable. “I can do that.”

“Brilliant. I’ll put the numbers into the deck once I get them.” Daniel tapped his notebook. “The client mainly wants to understand where we’ve improved.”

Then Sarah called him from the meeting room. Daniel raised one finger to Emily, as if to say he would be back, and hurried away.

Emily turned to her screen. She did not write the request down. Daniel had said it casually, and she thought she could remember the details. She also assumed he would send a message with the exact file names.

The morning became busy. Sarah asked Emily to check a spreadsheet for a different project, and a message arrived from Michael about an invoice that needed a small correction. Emily dealt with both tasks, then joined a short meeting about the team’s Friday deadlines.

At lunch, she saw Daniel near the coffee machine. He was speaking to James about the Westlake meeting. Emily considered asking him to repeat the request, but he seemed to be discussing the client’s questions, so she returned to her desk.

On Monday morning, Daniel passed her desk again. “How are the figures coming?” he asked.

Emily paused. “I’m starting them now. I thought I had until later today.”

“I need them before lunch if possible,” he said. “The presentation is at two.”

“I’ll do my best.”

Daniel looked concerned, but not angry. “Thanks. I know it’s short notice.”

Emily opened the shared drive and searched for last month’s service totals. Several spreadsheets appeared, with dates in their names. One had a label that suggested it was a draft. Another seemed to be a report from the previous month. She had not asked Daniel which source he wanted, and now she felt embarrassed to ask.

She chose the folder marked Westlake and began comparing the figures. Some of the response times were recorded in hours, while others were recorded in days. She made a note to check the difference, then sent Daniel a message asking which total he wanted included.

His reply came a few minutes later: “Use the figures from the approved monthly report. Sorry, I should have specified.”

Emily found the approved report and checked its date. She copied the key figures into a new summary sheet, adding a short note about how the response times had been measured. Before sending it, she asked Michael to check whether the service totals matched the finance report.

“They do,” Michael replied after a few minutes. “But make sure Daniel knows that the response-time figure covers working days, not calendar days.”

Emily added the note and sent Daniel a link to the summary. She also wrote a message explaining which report she had used and which figures he should treat with care.

Daniel came over soon afterwards. “This is helpful. I’ve added the totals to the slides. I wish I’d asked you sooner, but we should be all right.”

“I should have written down what you needed,” Emily said.

“And I should have put it in a message,” Daniel replied. “I thought I’d remember to follow up. Then the morning got away from me.”

They checked the slide together. One figure looked different from the version Daniel had already typed, so they opened the report and confirmed the correct amount. Daniel changed the slide and thanked her.

For a moment, Emily felt relieved. The task was finished, and no one seemed upset. Then Sarah asked her to bring a folder to the meeting room. As Emily stood, she noticed that the summary link she had sent pointed to a document with a blank title. She corrected the title and checked that Daniel still had access.

It was a small mistake, but it reminded her that a finished task was not always a clear task. She made a note in her notebook: “Westlake figures sent—approved report; response times in working days.” This time, she wanted the important details somewhere she could see them.

12 The Missing File

On Monday morning, the Westlake meeting began in a room with glass walls overlooking the office floor. Sarah sat beside Daniel, while Emily joined them with her laptop. The client had connected by video, and their faces appeared on the large screen at the end of the table.

Daniel opened the presentation. The title slide appeared, followed by a summary of the project and several charts. Then he reached the page with the service totals.

The slide was blank.

Daniel clicked back and forward, as if the figures might appear. “That’s not right,” he said quietly.

Emily opened the shared folder on her laptop. The summary sheet she had sent was no longer in the Westlake folder. She searched the recent files, then checked the meeting folder. She could not find the version Daniel had used.

The client waited on screen. Sarah turned towards Daniel. “Do you have a local copy?”

“I thought Emily was putting the final information into the presentation,” Daniel said. He looked at her, not accusingly, but with real surprise.

Emily’s face grew warm. “I thought you were adding it. I sent the summary on Friday.”

Neither of them had recorded their agreement clearly. Daniel had expected a completed slide. Emily had understood that he would use her figures and add them himself. The blank page was not one person’s fault, but the client was still waiting.

Sarah addressed the screen. “We’re having a technical delay. We’ll be ready in a few minutes.” She paused the meeting and asked Daniel to take the call from a different room while the team checked the files.

When Daniel had left, Emily searched the shared drive again. She opened the folder history but could not see a clear record of what had happened. The file might have been moved, renamed or saved in a different place.

“I thought you’d uploaded the final figures,” Daniel said when he returned. His voice was tight with worry. “I was working from the deck this morning.”

“I thought you were using the summary I sent,” Emily replied. “I didn’t know you needed me to build the slide.”

Sarah lifted a hand. “Stop for a moment. We need to find the information first. We can work out how the misunderstanding happened afterwards.”

Emily felt a strong wish to say nothing more. She could pretend that the file had disappeared for reasons she did not understand. She could hope Daniel would find it. But Sarah’s calm face made it harder to hide from what had happened.

“I didn’t write down what we agreed on Friday,” Emily said. “And I didn’t check who was responsible for the slide. I thought Daniel would message me, but I never asked. I’m sorry.”

Daniel rubbed his forehead. “I should have made the request clear too. I didn’t send the details in writing, and I assumed you knew what I meant.”

Sarah nodded. “Thank you both. Right now, let’s recover the figures. Emily, can you look for the earlier version in the shared folders? Daniel, check your presentation history. I’ll tell the client we need another ten minutes.”

Emily searched folders with names such as “Working,” “Archive” and “Client Review.” She checked the dates and opened each file carefully, avoiding the temptation to change anything before she knew what it contained.

At first, she found only old reports. Then she remembered that the team had saved a copy of the approved monthly report in a folder used for the previous meeting. She opened it and checked the figures against her notes.

The totals matched. The response times were marked as working days, as Michael had explained. Emily sent Sarah a message with the file path and a short summary of what she had checked.

Daniel found an earlier version of the presentation in his history. It contained a chart with last month’s figures, but the labels were incomplete. Emily helped him compare the chart with the approved report. They corrected the labels and added a note about the time period.

“Is this safe to use?” Daniel asked.

“It matches the approved report,” Emily said. “But the chart is an earlier version. We should explain that we’ve checked it, not pretend it’s a new file.”

Sarah agreed. She returned to the call and told the client that the team had recovered the earlier chart and verified the figures against the approved report. The client accepted the explanation and asked them to continue.

Emily watched Sarah close the meeting-room door. She was still embarrassed, but she had not stayed silent. The problem was not completely solved, and the meeting had started late. At least now the team knew what they were showing and why.

13 Ten Minutes to Repair

The Westlake client asked more questions than Daniel had expected. They wanted to know why one service had improved while another had taken longer to respond. The earlier chart did not answer everything, and the team had only a short time before the next call.

Sarah ended the meeting after agreeing to send a corrected summary later that afternoon. “We’ve got until four,” she said as the screen went dark. “The client needs a clear version before the end of the day. Let’s keep the numbers accurate and explain what we can’t yet confirm.”

Daniel opened the presentation on his laptop. “I’ll revise the slides. Emily, can you check the figures and the notes?”

“Yes. Which version should I work from?” she asked.

Daniel looked at her, then smiled slightly. “The one on my screen. I’ll share it with you now, and I’ll put the link in a message.”

That simple step made Emily feel calmer. She opened the link and checked that it led to the correct presentation. Sarah asked Michael to confirm the latest service totals. Olivia offered to review the wording because she had worked on a similar client report.

Nobody made a speech about teamwork. They simply divided the work and began.

Emily compared each figure on the slides with the approved monthly report. She worked slowly, reading the labels as well as the numbers. One chart showed a percentage but did not say what the percentage was measured against.

“This label is too short,” she told Daniel. “Someone might think it’s the number of requests rather than the share completed on time.”

Daniel checked the source. “You’re right. I copied the chart from the earlier deck and didn’t notice.” He changed the label and added a sentence explaining the comparison.

Michael sent confirmation of the latest total. It was slightly different from the amount in the earlier chart because one late entry had been included after the report was first saved. Daniel asked whether to use the updated figure.

“We should,” Emily said, “but we need to change the date on the slide too. Otherwise it will look as if the figure came from last month’s approved report.”

Sarah read the message over Daniel’s shoulder. “Good catch. Let’s be exact about what we can support. We can say the total includes the late entry, and give the date it was checked.”

Emily added the note to the shared document. Daniel reviewed it before making the change. They could both see the edits, and neither had to guess which file was current.

Olivia read the revised wording. “This is clearer. It doesn’t hide the difference, but it doesn’t make it sound like a crisis either.”

Emily was glad to hear that. Earlier, she might have tried to make every sentence sound certain. Now she could see that a careful explanation was more useful than a confident guess.

At half past three, Sarah asked the team to stop editing. She wanted a final check before anything went to the client. Daniel read the slides aloud while Emily followed the figures. Olivia checked that the message was polite and direct. Michael confirmed that the total matched the latest report.

One number on the final slide had been typed incorrectly. It was a small difference, but Sarah caught it before they sent the presentation. Daniel corrected it, then waited while Emily checked the new version.

“That’s right now,” Emily said. “I’ve checked it against the report and Michael’s confirmation.”

Daniel sent the presentation to the client with a short note explaining that the team had updated the figures and clarified the dates. He did not blame the missing file or the late start. He simply described the changes.

The client replied that the presentation was clear and asked one follow-up question for the next day. Sarah thanked everyone and closed the meeting room.

As they packed up, Daniel turned to Emily. “I’m sorry I assumed you were building the slide. I should have checked.”

“I’m sorry I didn’t check either,” Emily said. “I agreed to prepare information, but I didn’t confirm what that meant.”

“Next time, let’s put the task and deadline in the shared project notes,” Sarah said. “Not because anyone is in trouble. It’ll save us all time.”

Emily returned to her desk and added the agreed task to the project notes, including who would prepare the figures and who would build the slides. Before she left for the day, she checked that Daniel could see the update.

The commute home was crowded, and rain spotted the bus windows. In her small flat, Emily heated soup and told a friend about the stressful meeting. She did not describe herself as the cause of everything or as someone who had done nothing wrong. Both stories were too simple. She had made an assumption, admitted it and helped repair the result.

14 What We Agreed

On Tuesday morning, Emily arrived earlier than usual. The rain had stopped, but the pavements were still wet. She bought tea on the way from the station and carried it carefully through the busy office entrance.

At her desk, she opened the shared project notes from the day before. The task list was clear: Daniel would prepare the client slides; Emily would check the figures against the approved report; Michael would confirm any updated totals. The deadline was written beside each task.

Emily read the notes once, then began her own work. She did not feel that she had to ask a question about every small choice. She could use her judgement, but now she knew when a choice affected other people and needed to be shared.

Sarah stopped beside her desk. “How are you feeling about yesterday?”

“A bit embarrassed,” Emily said. “But less than I was last night. I kept thinking I should have known what Daniel meant.”

“It’s useful to notice what you might do differently,” Sarah said. “It’s not useful to expect yourself to read minds. We all need to be clear.”

Emily nodded. She had heard similar words before, but they meant more now that she had experienced the cost of not checking.

Later that morning, Daniel came over with a new request. “The client has asked for one extra chart. Could you check the figures by three? I’ll make the slide, as long as we’re both happy with that.”

“Yes,” Emily said. “I’ll check the approved report and send you the figures by half past two. If anything doesn’t match, I’ll message you before I change it.”

Daniel smiled. “That’s exactly what I need. I’ll put it in the project notes.”

He added the task while they stood together. Emily watched him include the deadline and the name of the source report. Then she opened the same note on her screen to make sure it had saved.

The rest of the morning went smoothly. Emily checked the chart figures, then found that one result had been collected over a shorter period than the others. Instead of leaving it out or presenting it without explanation, she sent Daniel a message with the detail. He asked her to add a note showing the dates covered.

By half past two, the figures were ready. Daniel built the chart, and Emily checked it before he added it to the presentation. The extra work took time, but it did not turn into an emergency. They had agreed on what each person would do.

At the end of the day, Olivia came to Emily’s desk with a paper cup in each hand. “Tea?” she asked. “I’m going downstairs before the next meeting.”

They walked to the café, where they found seats near the window. Olivia told Emily that the Greystone client had finally confirmed which figures they wanted. The deadline was still challenging, but the team now had a clear list of what remained.

“I’m glad we talked at lunch last week,” Olivia said. “I was trying to solve the whole project in my head. It helped to break it into questions.”

Emily held her cup in both hands. “I’m glad too. I’ve been trying to look confident even when I’m unsure. But sometimes the more useful thing is to say what I’m unsure about.”

“As long as you also say what you’ve checked,” Olivia said. “Then people can help without starting from the beginning.”

They returned to the office and worked for another hour. Emily then packed her bag and checked the project notes one last time. The next day’s tasks were listed, and Daniel had confirmed that the new chart was saved in the shared presentation.

On the journey home, Emily stood on a crowded train beside a woman carrying a bunch of flowers. Her shoulders ached, and she still had washing to do in her flat. Yet the day felt different from the first Monday at Northbridge, when every request had seemed like a test she might fail.

She had made mistakes. She had also asked better questions, listened to her colleagues and spoken up when silence seemed easier. At Northbridge Solutions, deadlines would keep arriving, files would keep changing, and people would still misunderstand one another sometimes. Emily could not control all of that.

What she could do was check what had been agreed, make her work visible and tell someone when she needed help. It was not a perfect plan. It was a fresh way to begin the next day.

15 The Numbers in the Room

By nine o’clock, the meeting room was already warm. Emily set her notebook beside the laptop and checked that the shared presentation opened correctly. Sarah sat at the end of the table, while Michael arranged printed copies of the client report. James had brought coffee for himself and a glass of water for the visitors.

“The latest spreadsheet is linked in the final slide,” Emily said. “I checked the totals against the summary tab.”

Sarah glanced at her. “Good. We’ll explain the figures, then ask the client what they need from us next.”

Emily had spent the previous afternoon checking the report with Michael. She felt nervous, but not in the same blind way she had felt at her first meetings. She knew which numbers she could explain and which questions she might need to take away.

When the clients arrived, James introduced everyone. Their operations manager, Ms Patel, thanked the team for the clear report. The conversation moved through delivery dates, staff numbers and the client’s plans for the next quarter. Emily followed her notes and answered a question about how the team had grouped the results.

“We compared the monthly totals, rather than treating each site separately,” she explained. “That makes the overall pattern easier to see, though we can provide a site-by-site view as well.”

Ms Patel nodded. “That would be useful. We have different needs at each location.”

Emily made a note. She was beginning to ask what a client actually wanted instead of assuming that one neat answer would suit everyone.

Then Ms Patel pointed to a figure on the second slide. “This says that our response time improved by twelve per cent. Is that based on all the cases?”

Emily looked down at her notebook. The number had come from the spreadsheet, but she could not remember whether Michael had included cases that were still open. She felt the familiar pressure to answer quickly. The room seemed to become quieter.

“I can see where the figure comes from,” she said, “but I’m not certain whether the open cases are included. I don’t want to give you the wrong answer. We’ll check the calculation and send you a clear note this afternoon.”

Sarah gave a small nod. Michael opened his copy of the spreadsheet, but he did not try to answer from memory either.

Ms Patel smiled. “That’s fine. I’d rather have a checked answer.”

The meeting continued. The clients asked for the alternative site view and agreed to send the team a list of their priorities. Nothing went perfectly, but the discussion was useful, and no one was left with a promise the team could not keep.

After the visitors had gone, Emily and Michael opened the working spreadsheet. They found that the twelve per cent figure had been calculated from completed cases only. That was a reasonable measure, but the slide did not say so.

“The calculation isn’t wrong,” Michael said. “The label is too broad.”

“Then we should make that clear,” Emily replied. She changed the note in the shared document, and Michael checked it before they sent Sarah the revised wording.

Sarah read their note and approved it. “Good response in there, Emily. You didn’t guess, and you didn’t hide the uncertainty. We can send the client the definition with the updated slide.”

On the train home, Emily thought about how she had expected success to feel. She had imagined leaving the meeting without any questions or mistakes. Instead, success had meant noticing a doubt, saying so and following it through.

Her small flat felt unusually quiet that evening. She made pasta, then opened her notebook to write down the tasks for tomorrow. The list was short: finish the client note, ask Michael to confirm the revised label, and check the new deadline with Sarah.

She added one more sentence beneath the list: “A careful answer is still an answer.” It looked a little like something from a poster, so she laughed and closed the notebook.

The next morning, the client replied to the note. Ms Patel thanked the team and said the explanation was helpful. Emily read the message twice, then forwarded it to Michael and Sarah.

Sarah appeared beside her desk a few minutes later. “You handled that well. Let’s talk about the last few weeks after lunch. I’d like to review how things are going.”

Emily felt a small pinch of worry. She had learned that a review could contain criticism as well as praise. Still, she closed the client document, wrote the meeting time in her calendar and returned to her work.

16 A Clearer Conversation

Sarah chose a small meeting room for their review. She brought Emily’s recent work on her laptop, but she did not sit behind the desk. She took the chair opposite Emily and placed a notepad between them.

“First, I want to say that your work is improving,” Sarah began. “You check details more carefully, and you’re letting people know when something is uncertain. That makes it easier for the rest of us to help.”

Emily had expected to hear a list of problems. The praise made her pleased, though she did not know what to do with her hands.

Sarah continued. “I also know my communication hasn’t always been clear. At the beginning, I thought I was giving you space to work things out. Sometimes I was too brief, and you were left guessing what mattered most.”

Emily looked up. “I thought you might be disappointed if I asked too many questions.”

“I can see how you got that impression,” Sarah said. “When I’m busy, I sometimes answer quickly. That isn’t a signal that you shouldn’t ask. I should have made that clear.”

Emily remembered the days she had spent choosing between tasks without knowing which one was urgent. “Could we agree on the priority when there are several deadlines? Even a quick message would help. And it would be useful to know what you want changed when you review my work.”

Sarah wrote both points down. “Yes. I can give you a short priority list in our Monday check-in, and if something changes, I’ll tell you directly. For feedback, I’ll try to explain the reason behind a change, not just make the change myself.”

“That would help,” Emily said. “I’ll also tell you sooner if I can’t fit everything in.”

They looked at the client report together. Sarah showed Emily where one paragraph was too detailed for the main report. It was not a general complaint that Emily wrote too much; Sarah explained that the client needed the decision first and the background afterwards.

“I can move the explanation into the appendix,” Emily said.

“Exactly. The detail is useful. It just needs the right place.”

They also discussed Emily’s habit of editing a shared file late in the evening. Once, she had changed a heading while Daniel was preparing another section. Neither of them had lost work, but the document had been confusing the next morning.

“I thought I was helping by finishing my part,” Emily said.

“I understand,” Sarah replied. “But the rest of the team needs to know when a section is ready and when you’re still working on it. Use the comments or tell Daniel before you make a late change.”

Emily added that to her notes. The conversation did not make her feel suddenly confident, but it did make the rules less mysterious.

At the end of the review, Sarah asked, “What do you think you need to work on next?”

Emily considered her answer instead of saying whatever sounded best. “I want to keep checking figures, but I also need to get quicker at asking about priorities. Sometimes I wait until I’ve already spent too long on the wrong task.”

“That’s a useful goal,” Sarah said. “Let’s check in again in a month.”

Back at her desk, Emily sent Daniel a message: “I’m changing the heading in my section now. Please let me know if you’re editing nearby.” He replied with a thumbs-up and told her he would finish his paragraph first.

Later, Sarah sent the Monday priority list she had promised to use. It named the client note as urgent, the internal figures as important but not due until Thursday, and the meeting minutes as something Lucy could support with.

Emily saved the message. She did not need to pretend that the office had become easy. It was still noisy, deadlines still arrived together, and she still worried about making mistakes. But now she had asked for something specific, and Sarah had answered with something specific.

Before leaving, Emily checked tomorrow’s tasks and sent Sarah one question about a deadline. The answer came back in a few minutes. Emily shut down her computer and joined the crowd moving towards the station, feeling lighter—not because every problem had disappeared, but because she no longer had to solve every uncertainty alone.

17 Two Names on the Document

On Wednesday morning, Olivia stopped beside Emily’s desk with a printed brief in her hand.

“Sarah has asked us to prepare a short options paper for the new service review,” she said. “We need to compare three ways of organising the support team.”

Emily read the brief. The paper was due the following Tuesday, and the information would come from several departments. Olivia had worked on similar reviews before, while Emily knew the latest client figures.

“Would you like me to draft the comparison table?” Emily asked.

“Yes, and I can write the introduction and collect comments from the department leads,” Olivia said. “Then we can review each other’s sections.”

They opened a shared document and divided the work. Emily created a table with columns for cost, response time and possible difficulties. Olivia wrote the opening section. They agreed to meet after lunch on Friday to check the whole paper.

The arrangement felt clear at first. Olivia knew the project history; Emily understood the numbers. They both had real responsibilities, and neither was expected to do the other person’s job.

By Thursday afternoon, the spreadsheet was becoming complicated. One department had sent figures in a different format, and a second had not replied. Emily entered the available information and added a note beside the missing figures.

Olivia came over with a message from Sarah. “She wants to know whether we can include a cost estimate by the end of today.”

Emily looked at the incomplete data. “I can make an estimate from what we have, but it will need a warning beside it. We don’t have the staffing figures from the other department yet.”

“I agree,” Olivia said. “I’ll ask them again and explain that we need the numbers by tomorrow morning.”

Emily added the estimate to the spreadsheet and labelled it “provisional”. She wrote a comment in the shared document explaining which figures were confirmed and which were not. Olivia checked the wording and suggested a clearer sentence.

The work took longer than expected. Emily had to correct a formula that included a blank row, and Olivia found that one department had counted temporary staff differently from the others. They did not blame each other. Instead, they added a note about the difference and decided to ask the department lead to confirm the figures.

At five o’clock, Emily saw Olivia still reading the paper. “Do you want me to finish the table tonight?” she asked.

“No,” Olivia said. “We agreed to check it together tomorrow. Please leave a comment with what’s still uncertain, and I’ll handle the missing reply first thing.”

Emily wrote the comment and left the spreadsheet open in the shared folder. Before she closed it, she checked that her edits had saved. She also sent Olivia a short message summarising the two questions that needed answers.

On Friday, the department lead confirmed the missing staffing figure. The estimate changed, but not by much. Emily updated the table and marked the new number as confirmed. Olivia revised the introduction so it explained the choices without suggesting that one was already decided.

When they read the paper together, Emily noticed that Olivia had moved her note about the different staff categories into a small section on assumptions.

“That makes it easier to follow,” Emily said.

“Your table helped me see what needed explaining,” Olivia replied. “I don’t always know which details matter to the people who use the numbers.”

Their paper was not finished, but it was taking shape. They sent it to Sarah with a brief note about the remaining checks. Sarah replied that the work was on track and asked them to keep the assumptions visible.

In the kitchen, Olivia offered Emily a biscuit from a packet beside the kettle. “It’s easier when we say who’s doing what,” she said. “On my last project, I thought someone else was asking for the figures. They thought I was. We lost two days.”

Emily thought of how often she had once tried to be helpful by silently taking on extra work. “I’m learning that clear isn’t unfriendly,” she said.

“Exactly,” Olivia replied. “It saves everyone time.”

Emily returned to her desk and added one final comment to the document, then left the responsibility for the next step where they had agreed it should be: with Olivia. It felt strange not to keep checking, but she trusted their arrangement enough to let the work continue without her.

18 A Walk After Work

The office was busy on Monday, and Emily was trying to finish the service review before the Tuesday deadline. Daniel leaned over the divider between their desks.

“I’ve updated the chart in the shared file,” he said. “I’ve also changed the title because the old one was misleading.”

Emily opened the document. The new chart looked right, but its numbers were different from the figures she remembered. She was about to message Michael when Daniel added, “I copied it from the earlier version. I think it’s the same data.”

Emily felt a familiar tension in her shoulders. In the past, she might have quietly corrected the chart and hoped Daniel would notice. Instead, she asked, “Can you confirm which version you copied it from? The earlier sheet still had provisional figures.”

Daniel paused. “I’m not sure. I should have checked.”

They opened the version history and found that he had used a chart from the previous week. The difference was small, but it affected one of the comparisons.

“Thanks for checking,” Daniel said. “I’ll replace it with the current chart and leave a note in the document.”

Emily stayed beside him while he made the change. They checked the label and source together, then told Sarah that they had found and corrected an old figure.

Sarah thanked them and asked Daniel to confirm the source whenever he added material from another file. He nodded. His face was a little red, but he did not argue or make excuses.

“I was trying to save time,” he said to Emily afterwards. “I didn’t think the older chart would still be there.”

“I know,” Emily replied. “I’ve made that kind of guess too.”

The paper went to Sarah that afternoon. It still needed a final review, but the team had found the problem before it reached the department leads. Emily noticed that she was not as frightened by the mistake as she might have been a month earlier. The important thing was that they had raised it, checked it and told the right people.

At six, Ben sent her a message. He had asked earlier whether she wanted to join a local walking group that met near a café every Monday evening. Emily had been unsure because she did not know anyone there.

“They’re a friendly lot,” Ben had said. “Some people walk quickly, some just come for the café afterwards. You can leave whenever you like.”

Now Emily looked at her nearly finished work and at the dark window beyond the office lights. She could take the train straight home, eat alone and think about tomorrow’s tasks. Or she could go.

She wrote to Ben: “I’ll meet you at the station entrance.”

The group was waiting beside a small café in a busy neighbourhood. There were seven people, including Ben’s neighbour and a woman who worked at a bookshop. Ben introduced Emily, and nobody asked what her job title was. They talked about the cold weather, a new bakery and which streets were quiet enough for walking.

The walk took them through a park and along a canal. Emily did not know everyone’s name, and she did not need to. She listened as two people discussed a film and told the group about a small concert they had enjoyed at the weekend.

Afterwards, they went into the café for tea. Emily found herself laughing at a story about someone who had taken the wrong bus and ended up on the other side of the city. The story was ordinary, but it made the evening feel easy.

“You should come next week,” Ben said as they left.

“I’d like to,” Emily replied. She meant it, though she knew she might feel shy again before the next meeting.

On the train home, she thought about the difference between her working day and the evening. At work, she had asked a question that stopped an old chart from becoming a bigger problem. Outside work, she had gone somewhere without needing to prove that she belonged. Both things mattered.

In her flat, she made a cup of tea and set out her clothes for the morning. The service review would need more work, and she still worried about how Sarah would respond to the final version. But the worry did not fill the whole evening. She had a place to go next Monday, and a few people whose names she was beginning to remember.

19 The Last Monday of the Month

The final week of the month arrived with a row of meetings in Emily’s calendar and a deadline for the service review. She began Monday by checking Sarah’s priority list. The client note was complete, the review paper needed its final comments, and the internal figures could wait until Wednesday.

Emily and Olivia sat together to read the paper once more. Olivia checked the introduction and the list of options. Emily checked the numbers and the notes about assumptions. They found a sentence that sounded too certain about the cost estimate.

“We should say that this is based on the figures received so far,” Emily said. “The departments may still send changes.”

Olivia agreed, and they adjusted the wording. Then they sent the paper to Sarah, clearly marked as a draft for review.

Sarah returned it before lunch with several comments. One asked for a shorter explanation; another asked them to show which option had the quickest response time. Emily felt a brief sting when she saw the amount of red on the screen, but she read each note carefully.

“The main point is clear,” Sarah said when they spoke. “The next version just needs to bring the comparison forward. The detail can stay, but the reader should see the choices sooner.”

Emily and Olivia reorganised the pages. The changes took an hour, not a day. They sent the revised paper by the agreed deadline.

At the afternoon check-in, Sarah thanked them both. “This is a useful first version. We’ll use it to guide the discussion, not to make the decision for the departments.”

The words relieved Emily. She had once believed every piece of work had to be perfect before anyone could see it. Now she was beginning to understand that a draft could be honest about its limits and still help people move forward.

“Olivia, you did a good job collecting the department comments,” Sarah said. “Emily, your notes made the numbers much easier to check.”

“We clarified who was responsible for each part,” Olivia said. “That helped.”

Daniel joined the conversation from the doorway. He had started adding a short note whenever he used material from a shared file. “I’ve also stopped copying charts without checking the source,” he said. “It’s slower for about thirty seconds and faster afterwards.”

Everyone laughed. Daniel was not pretending that he would never make another mistake. He was showing that he had learned from one.

On Friday, Sarah met Emily again for a short monthly check-in. She said Emily had become more reliable about raising questions before a task was nearly finished. She also asked Emily to keep working on planning her time across several deadlines.

“I still find that difficult,” Emily said. “But I’m getting better at telling you when two things can’t both be urgent.”

“That’s exactly the conversation I want you to have,” Sarah replied. “You don’t have to solve every conflict by working later.”

After work, Emily took the train to the neighbourhood where Ben’s walking group met. She recognised the café and two people from the week before. One of them waved her over to the table.

The group had made plans for a longer walk on Saturday, followed by lunch. Emily agreed to join them. She could not remember everyone’s name yet, but she was starting to feel comfortable asking.

On the way home, she thought about the month. She still doubted herself sometimes. A quiet message from Sarah could still make her wonder whether she had done something wrong. A large spreadsheet could still make her feel lost. She had not become the fastest person in the office, and no one had offered her a new title.

But she had asked for clearer priorities and received them. She had admitted uncertainty in front of a client instead of pretending. She had taken feedback, made changes and learned to explain what she needed. She had worked with Olivia without trying to carry the whole assignment, and she had helped Daniel correct a mistake without turning it into a personal failure.

At home, Emily put her bag by the door and opened her notebook. She wrote down Monday’s tasks, then added a note to ask Sarah about the next project’s deadline before beginning the first draft.

The small flat was still small, and the city was still noisy beyond the window. Yet her life no longer seemed to be divided into work that frightened her and evenings spent recovering from it. There were familiar faces at the office, and now a few outside it too.

Emily closed the notebook and made tea. She did not know exactly what the next month would bring. She knew she would make mistakes, and she knew some days would feel harder than others. But she had learned that she could ask questions, accept help and keep going. For now, that felt like a fresh start of its own.
