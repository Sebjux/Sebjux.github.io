0 The Flat on Birch Street

On Monday morning, Maya Collins found a damp patch spreading across the ceiling above the kitchen table. She stood beneath it with her coffee and watched one drop gather, hesitate, and fall into Daniel Reed’s empty cereal bowl.

“The ceiling is crying,” she said.

Daniel looked up from his phone. “It’s probably rain getting in around the old window. I’ll tell Mrs Bennett.”

“You told her last month.”

“And she sent a plumber.”

“Who said it wasn’t a plumbing problem.”

Daniel put his phone down. “Then I’ll tell her again.”

Maya smiled. She liked that about him: he complained in a practical way, as if the right email might repair any problem. Daniel was thirty-one, an IT support specialist, and he treated most of life as a series of tickets waiting to be closed. Maya was twenty-nine and worked as a marketing coordinator for a small food company near Deansgate. She believed that some problems needed a spreadsheet, while others needed a walk, a chat, or a very good sandwich.

They had shared the two-bedroom flat on Birch Street for two years. The rooms were narrow, the kitchen cupboards were crowded, and the bus stop outside was useful when the rain was not coming sideways. The location, between a busy road and a row of small shops, suited them both. Maya could get to work without changing buses, and Daniel could cycle to his office when the weather allowed.

Their friendship had begun through a mutual friend who needed someone to take a room quickly. It had survived a broken washing machine, Daniel’s habit of leaving cables on the sofa, and Maya’s habit of buying groceries without checking whether they already had three jars of mustard. They shared the ordinary work of a home. Daniel dealt with bills; Maya usually did the food shop. They split the rent and other shared costs equally.

On the fridge, Daniel kept a small whiteboard with the bills written in neat blue marker. The current rent was £1,450 a month. Shared utilities, Internet and contents insurance came to about £310, though the amount moved slightly with the seasons. Groceries averaged £420. Daniel updated the board when a bill changed. Maya added magnets shaped like fruit.

That evening, Maya returned with two paper bags from a café near her office. One held a soup and a warm roll; the other held a slice of lemon cake. She had meant to bring lunch from home, but a meeting had run through her break, and the café had been close.

Daniel was at the kitchen table, looking at a set of replacement bicycle lights on his laptop.

“You could have made soup,” he said, glancing at the bag.

“I could have. I could also have spent my lunch break making soup, but I chose to eat it instead.”

He nodded. “Fair.”

Maya put the cake between them. Daniel took a small piece, then turned his screen around to show her the bicycle lights. They cost £38. He said his old ones were unreliable in the dark. She thought he had a point, though she also knew he had recently bought a new set of headphones for his computer.

Later, they sat on the sofa with the heating turned low and a film playing. Maya told him she was planning to see a band with her friend Priya next month. Daniel told her he had signed up for another month of a specialist cycling video service. Neither of them sounded guilty. Their lives were not rich, but they were comfortable enough to make small plans.

At the end of the film, Daniel got up to close the kitchen window. He saw the damp patch had grown. The next morning, he would write to Mrs Bennett. For now, the flat still felt like home.

1 A Letter from Mrs Bennett

The letter arrived on Wednesday, pushed through the flat’s front door with the electricity bill. Daniel picked it up on his way to work and noticed Mrs Bennett’s name on the envelope. He carried it upstairs rather than opening it in the hall.

Maya was standing by the sink, trying to rinse a travel mug before leaving. Her phone was propped against a tin of tea, playing a message from her mother.

“It’s probably about the damp,” Daniel said.

He opened the envelope. His face changed before he had finished reading.

“What?” Maya asked.

Daniel passed her the paper. “The rent.”

The notice said that, from the first of the next month, the rent would rise from £1,450 to £1,560. Mrs Bennett described the new amount as a response to higher costs and said she hoped they would understand. The letter gave the required notice period and asked them to confirm that they planned to stay.

Maya read it again. “One hundred and ten pounds more every month.”

“Fifty-five each, if we keep splitting it evenly.”

“I know how division works, Dan.”

He looked at her, surprised by the sharpness in her voice. She set the letter on the counter and reached for her phone. Her mother had asked whether Maya could help with a prescription payment. The amount was not large, but the message sat beside the rent notice like another bill waiting to be paid.

“Are you going to reply?” she asked.

“Not before we check our options. We should look at similar places nearby and work out whether moving would actually save money.”

Maya knew that was sensible. She also knew what moving involved: a deposit, van hire, time away from work, and the possibility of finding a cheaper place that needed repairs or sat far from a bus route. The current flat was imperfect, but it was familiar. She looked at the damp patch above the table.

“We can ask her to explain the increase,” she said.

“We can. But the letter sounds final.”

At work, Maya tried to concentrate on a campaign for a new range of ready meals. The figures on the screen became blurred whenever she thought about the extra £55. Her salary arrived regularly, but it did not stretch as far as it had the previous year. Her food shopping cost more. Bus fares had gone up. The dentist had recently replaced an old filling, and she still had part of that bill on her credit card.

That evening, Daniel cooked pasta while Maya sat at the table and scrolled through rental listings. A small flat near Salford Quays looked cheaper, but the photographs showed one bedroom and no space for a desk. Another place had a lower rent but was almost an hour from Maya’s office by public transport.

“If we move, we might pay more for travel,” she said. “And we’d need money for the deposit before we got our deposit back here.”

“Exactly. I’m not saying we should move. I just want real numbers before we decide.”

Maya closed the listing. “Sometimes a real number is still bad news.”

Daniel set a bowl in front of her. “That doesn’t make it less useful.”

They ate quietly. Outside, buses passed along the wet road. Maya thought of her mother’s message, the card payment, and the two concerts she had already planned for the month. She did not want to cancel everything she enjoyed. She also did not want to pretend the rise would not matter.

After dinner, Daniel took the letter and placed it beside the whiteboard. In blue marker, he changed the rent from £1,450 to £1,560 and drew a line underneath it. The new figure made the kitchen look different, though nothing else had changed.

2 The Morning Balance

On Friday, Maya checked her bank balance while she waited for the kettle to boil. Her wages had arrived the day before. So had the rent payment, the phone bill, and the monthly card payment she had set up after the dentist visit. She refreshed the screen, hoping the numbers might settle into a more comfortable shape.

They did not.

Maya had £214 left in her current account until payday. She still needed food, bus travel, and a few things for work. Her credit-card balance was £486. The prescription money for her mother was due next week, and the rent increase would begin the following month.

She sat down. The kitchen chair gave a small squeak.

When Daniel came in, he saw her looking at the phone. “Everything all right?”

“I’ve got two hundred and fourteen pounds until payday.”

He put his mug on the counter. “Is that after the direct debits?”

“Yes.”

“Then we should look at your spending. You’ve had a lot of café lunches lately, and the concert tickets—”

“I know what I’ve spent.”

“I’m not saying you can’t go out. I’m saying the numbers might show where the pressure is.”

“You make it sound as if I’ve done something stupid.” Maya put her phone face down. “I’ve paid my share of the bills. I help Mum because she needs it. I’m not buying a new bike every month.”

Daniel went quiet. He looked hurt, then a little angry. “I’m not judging you. But I track what I spend because it helps me. If we’re going to make a plan, we need to know where the money goes.”

Maya knew he was partly right. She also knew that his careful voice could feel like a door closing. “I don’t want to talk about it before work.”

“All right,” he said. “Tonight, then?”

She nodded, but the rest of the day passed with the conversation still between them. She ate the apple she had brought from home and bought a sandwich anyway because a meeting ended late. In the afternoon, her manager asked if she could stay longer to finish a report. Maya said yes, then took a bus rather than walking the last part of the journey because her knee was sore.

At six, she bought pain-relief tablets from a pharmacy. She had been putting off seeing her doctor about the knee. The pain was not severe, but it had lasted for weeks, and long walks sometimes made it worse. The tablets cost £5.40. It was a small amount, but the day had filled up with small amounts.

At home, Daniel had cleared the kitchen table. His notebook lay open beside the whiteboard, but he had not written anything yet. A plate of toast sat near Maya’s usual chair.

“I’m sorry I brought it up like that,” he said. “I was worried, and I sounded like I’d already decided what your problem was.”

Maya pulled out the chair. “I don’t always know where my money goes. But when you list cafés, it sounds as if lunch is the reason rent is rising.”

“It isn’t,” Daniel said. “The rent rise is much bigger than one coffee. I don’t think skipping coffee solves it. I think we need to see the whole picture.”

That answer helped. It did not fix the problem, but it made the table feel less like a courtroom.

Maya took out her phone again. “Okay. Let’s write it down. But we write all of it down. Your subscriptions too.”

Daniel gave a small, embarrassed smile. “Fair.”

They moved their mugs aside. The damp mark above them remained, spreading slowly across the ceiling.

3 The Kitchen-Table Budget

Daniel opened his notebook, but Maya took the pen from him. “You can write the numbers. I’ll make sure we include the things people forget.”

“That sounds like a fair division of labour.”

“Good. Put our take-home pay at the top.”

Their monthly income came to £4,400: Maya received £2,050 after tax, and Daniel received £2,350. They checked the amounts against their payslips rather than guessing. Then they listed shared costs. The new rent would be £1,560. Utilities, Internet and contents insurance averaged £310. Groceries averaged £420.

“That makes the shared total £2,290,” Daniel said. “If we keep splitting it equally, that’s £1,145 each.”

Maya wrote the calculation in the margin. The rent was £780 each, the other shared bills came to £155 each, and groceries came to £210 each. Together, that was £1,145 per person. She noticed how much space that took from her own income.

Next came transport. Maya’s monthly travel costs averaged £140, including buses to work and occasional late trips home. Daniel spent about £95 on buses and cycling costs. He had a bicycle, but it still needed repairs and equipment.

“We should separate shared costs from individual ones,” Maya said. “Otherwise we’ll argue about a bus ticket as if it belongs to both of us.”

They added their personal regular expenses. Maya paid £38 for her mobile phone and £45 toward the dentist bill on her credit card. She sent her mother £120 each month. Daniel paid £42 for his phone, £48 for a cycling video subscription and other hobby services, and £35 toward equipment bought earlier in the year.

Daniel looked at the list. “The video service is more than I thought.”

“You said it was £30.”

“The other £18 is the race-analysis app. I can cancel one.”

Maya studied her side. “The card payment doesn’t clear the balance.”

“No,” she said. “I used the card for the dental work. I’m paying it down, but the interest means it moves slowly.”

They carried on, adding personal estimates for work lunches, household items not covered by the grocery shop, clothes, and going out. They wrote each number in pencil because some costs changed from month to month. Maya included gifts and train journeys to see her mother. Daniel included bike repairs and games with friends.

When they were finished, Daniel added the regular shared and individual costs. Shared costs were £2,290. Individual transport was £235. Their known personal commitments—phones, Maya’s card payment and family help, Daniel’s hobby services and equipment payment—came to £328. That left £1,547 for both of them to divide among clothes, medical costs, meals away from home, savings and other personal spending.

“It looks like there’s room,” Daniel said, then stopped himself. “Before flexible costs.”

“And before anything goes wrong,” Maya replied.

She showed him the pharmacy receipt and explained the knee pain. She had not booked a doctor’s appointment because she worried about taking time off and about what treatment might cost if she needed to pay for it. Daniel admitted that his bike needed a service soon. He had delayed it, but the brakes were making a noise.

They wrote both items down. The budget did not make their income larger, but it made their choices visible. It also showed that the rent increase was only one part of the pressure: transport, health and family support all needed space.

Maya looked at the paper. “I can reduce some café lunches. I don’t want to pretend that will cover everything.”

“It won’t,” Daniel said. “We’ll need to check the bills, ask the landlord about the repairs, and decide what we can change without making life miserable.”

For the first time that week, Maya felt they were facing the same problem rather than accusing each other of having one.

4 What the Numbers Miss

On Saturday morning, Maya and Daniel made a fresh pot of tea and looked at the budget again. In daylight, the kitchen ceiling seemed worse. The damp patch had reached the edge of the light fitting, and Maya was no longer willing to treat it as an old mark.

Daniel took photographs while she wrote a list of the problems in the flat. The kitchen leak, the loose handle on the bathroom window, and the front door that sometimes stuck. He sent the pictures to Mrs Bennett with a polite request for a repair visit. He also asked whether the rent increase was connected to any planned improvements.

“Do you think that’s too direct?” he asked.

“No. We’re paying more. It’s reasonable to ask what happens next.”

They checked their grocery spending. The £420 average included cleaning products and the occasional prepared meal, so it was not a perfect measure of food alone. Maya suggested planning four evening meals each week, not seven. She often worked late, and a plan that expected her to cook every night would fail by Wednesday.

“We could make two large meals,” Daniel said. “Then leftovers become lunch.”

“If we both like them.”

“I like most things.”

“You once called my lentil stew a serious challenge.”

“It was a serious challenge to the lentils.”

They laughed, and the small joke made the planning easier. Maya did not want a life organised around never spending money. She liked meeting Priya after work and occasionally buying lunch when she was tired. Daniel liked cycling with a group on Sunday mornings. They agreed that any plan had to leave space for something enjoyable, or they would stop following it.

After lunch, Maya called her mother, Susan. Susan had recently reduced her working hours at a small clothing shop because of pain in her hands. She received a pension and paid her own rent, but some months were difficult. Maya’s £120 helped with food and prescriptions.

“You don’t have to send it this month,” Susan said after Maya explained the rent notice.

“I don’t want you worrying about it.”

“That’s not the same as being able to afford it.”

Maya looked at the budget. She could reduce the amount for one month, but the prescription cost would still be there. “Could we talk about what you need each month, rather than me sending the same amount automatically?”

There was a pause. “I’d rather do that than have you put it on a card.”

Maya felt a familiar mix of relief and guilt. She had thought that regular help was kinder than asking questions. Now she understood that it had also stopped them discussing what her mother actually needed and what Maya could manage.

When she ended the call, Daniel was checking the cycling subscription. He had cancelled the race-analysis app, saving £18 a month. He planned to keep the video service until the end of its current period, then review whether he still used it enough. He had also put the bicycle service on a list of necessary costs, not a luxury.

“I can’t cut the repair,” he said. “The brakes are a safety issue.”

“Agreed.” Maya showed him the doctor’s appointment page on her phone. “I’ve booked an appointment next week.”

“Good.”

They talked about the dentist balance and agreed Maya would contact the clinic to ask for a clear statement of what remained. She would keep paying it, but she wanted to know how much of each payment reduced the debt.

By the end of the afternoon, they had not found an easy answer. Their spending could be adjusted, but the largest new cost—the rent—was not theirs to control. Nor could they simply remove family needs, health, or transport from the budget.

Daniel wrote the date of the repair request beside the damp patch on the whiteboard. Maya added a note to review the grocery total after the next shop. Their lists were small actions, not a solution. Still, they were actions they could take together.

5 A Reasonable Reply

Mrs Bennett replied on Monday afternoon. She thanked them for the photographs and said she would arrange for a contractor to inspect the kitchen ceiling. About the rent, she wrote that her own costs had increased and that the new amount reflected the market in the area. She did not offer a reduction.

Daniel read the message twice. “At least she’s sending someone.”

“That doesn’t make £110 disappear.”

“No. But we can still ask whether there’s any room to agree a smaller increase for the first few months.”

Maya was wary of making a request that might sound like a threat. Daniel reminded her that they had been polite and had paid on time. They drafted a short reply together. They explained that they valued the flat and wanted to stay, but that the full increase would be difficult while repairs were outstanding. They asked whether Mrs Bennett would consider a temporary increase of £60, followed by a review after the contractor’s visit.

Maya read the message aloud. “It doesn’t sound demanding.”

“It’s a question,” Daniel said. “She can say no.”

They sent it. The answer did not arrive that day.

At work, Maya noticed how often she had been treating each new cost as a private failure. Her colleague mentioned that their bus fare had risen again. Another coworker was sharing childcare costs with an ex-partner and trying to find a smaller flat. None of their situations were the same, but the pressure was not hers alone.

That evening, she told Daniel that her appointment about the knee was booked. The practice nurse had said the appointment itself would not cost anything and could help decide whether she needed further care. Maya had been afraid to look into it because she imagined a large bill before she knew what was wrong.

“I’m glad you called,” Daniel said. “Pain gets harder to manage when you ignore it.”

“I know. I was also worried about losing a morning’s work.”

“Did they give you a time?”

“Just after nine. I’ll make the time up later.”

They checked the household accounts. Their shared bills were paid from a joint account, and each transferred £1,145 to cover half of the new monthly total. Until the increase began, they would continue paying their existing shares. They agreed not to use the joint account for personal spending, even when one of them bought something for the flat. Those purchases would be discussed first.

Maya had one café lunch planned for the following week. She decided to keep it, but she would take lunch from home on the other workdays. Daniel would cycle to work when the weather was safe, but he would not count that as guaranteed savings because rain, repairs and late shifts could change the plan.

“That’s better than pretending we’ll never take a bus,” Maya said.

“Or pretending I’ll ride a bike with bad brakes.”

They set a small monthly amount aside for emergency costs, starting with £80 between them if their other spending allowed. It was not much, and some months they might need to use it. They agreed that building a reserve slowly mattered more than claiming they could save a large amount they did not have.

Before bed, Maya checked her banking app. Her balance was still low, and the credit-card amount had not changed much. She felt the old pinch of worry, but it was no longer mixed with the belief that one failed coffee budget had caused everything.

On Tuesday, the contractor called to arrange a visit. Mrs Bennett had not yet answered their request about the rent, but she had confirmed that the inspection would happen. Daniel added the appointment to his calendar. Maya put a reminder on the fridge to call her mother and discuss the actual prescription costs.

The flat remained damp, the new rent remained uncertain, and their incomes had not changed. But they had stopped hiding the difficult parts from each other. For now, that was enough to make the next decision possible.

6 The Small Charges

On Monday evening, Maya opened the banking app she had avoided all weekend. The rent letter lay on the table beside her notebook. Mrs Bennett’s new figure, £1,560 a month, still looked unreal, even though they had already sent a reasonable reply asking whether the increase could be delayed.

“We should check everything that leaves our accounts,” she said. “Not because a streaming service will pay the rent. Because we need to know what is happening.”

Daniel put his phone beside hers. “I have already cancelled the music app I barely use.”

“Good,” Maya said. “I’m not asking you to live without music.”

He gave her a doubtful look. She understood it. During their earlier budget conversation, her careful lists had made his ordinary life sound like a series of mistakes. She had not meant them to, but good intentions did not change how a sentence landed.

They searched their statements month by month. Daniel found a video subscription for £8.99, a cloud-storage plan for £2.49 and a fitness app for £6.99. He used the storage plan for photographs and the fitness app when his knee was well. The video service, he admitted, had not been opened since Christmas.

Maya found a language-learning subscription costing £9.99. She had begun it when she thought she might apply for a job abroad. She had not studied for four months. There was also a recipe service for £4.49, which she had forgotten she was paying for.

“I can cancel these two,” she said. “That saves £14.48.”

Daniel tapped his screen. “The video app saves £8.99. The rest is not all waste.”

They wrote the total savings, £23.47, in the margin. It was useful, but it would not make the new rent manageable on its own. Maya was relieved that neither of them pretended otherwise.

The next surprise was delivery fees. Daniel had ordered food twice during a late week at work. The meals had cost £31.60, but service charges and delivery brought the total to £39.10. Maya found three smaller orders of groceries and household things. Delivery charges had added £12.50 across the month.

“I can see how it happens,” she said. “You get home tired, and the app makes dinner look easy.”

“You say that as if you never do it.”

“I do. I’m not innocent.”

They agreed to plan one shared food order each week, with collection when possible, and to put delivery charges in the notebook rather than hide them under groceries. They did not set a rule that nobody could order food. They set a rule that they would notice the real cost first.

Afterward, Daniel looked at her list. “You make it sound simple to plan ahead.”

“It is simple on paper,” Maya said. “Doing it after a full day is different.”

For once, the sentence did not end in an argument. Outside, a bus passed along Birch Street in the rain. They looked again at the rent letter, then at the much smaller numbers on their screens. Knowing them did not solve the problem. It did make the problem harder to mistake for personal carelessness.

7 The Date on the Policy

On Tuesday, Maya found the annual contents-insurance renewal email while searching for the recipe subscription confirmation. The policy would renew in three weeks for £180. She had always thought of it as a yearly bill, not part of her monthly spending.

“That’s £15 a month if we put something aside,” Daniel said, checking the calculator.

“It is my policy, so it belongs in my individual costs,” Maya replied. “But I should not act as if it appears from nowhere every year.”

They added a line to the notebook: annual insurance, £15 monthly provision. Maya set up a separate savings pot for it. She also wrote down her annual eye test and glasses, which were not due yet but had cost £96 the last time. Daniel added his bicycle service and replacement lights. The costs were not identical, but both were real.

They went through the other irregular expenses they could remember: birthdays, winter clothes, medicine, small repairs, and travel to see family. The list grew faster than either expected.

Daniel’s rail trips to visit his sister varied from month to month. Maya regularly sent money to her mother, whose hours at work had been cut. The amount was usually £140, though it had been £180 in December.

“I can’t promise my mum that it will always be exactly the same,” Maya said. “If her heating bill is high, I help.”

“I understand,” Daniel replied. “But I need to know what is usual, or I’ll plan around money that isn’t really free.”

Maya felt defensive, then noticed that he had written “family support: usually £140, sometimes more” instead of calling it optional. “That is fair,” she said. “I need to be honest about the range.”

They agreed that the notebook would show regular commitments and a separate allowance for irregular ones. Neither would use an unusually cheap month as proof that the next month would be easy.

Maya called Sophie on her walk home. Sophie listened, then said, “You cannot make every uncertain expense disappear by giving it a smaller name.”

“I’m trying to make a budget that doesn’t scare Daniel.”

“Then make one that tells the truth. He can decide what he thinks about the truth.” Sophie’s voice was warm, but direct. “And don’t pretend helping your mum is a hobby you can cancel.”

That evening Maya showed Daniel the revised notes. He read them carefully. “I’m not asking you to stop supporting her. I’m asking us to include it before we decide what you can contribute.”

It was a reasonable distinction, and Maya was glad he had made it. She had sometimes treated any question about the money she sent home as a judgement on her loyalty. Daniel had not called it that.

They set aside £15 a month for Maya’s insurance and £20 for her eye care and other health costs. Daniel put £25 aside for transport beyond his usual commute and bicycle costs. They also agreed that the amounts were starting estimates, not promises. A real month could be more expensive.

The budget now looked less tidy. It also looked more like their lives. The number at the bottom was smaller than before, and neither tried to improve it by pretending that a renewal date or a family need did not exist.

8 A Week of Receipts

On Wednesday, Maya began preparing lunches. She cooked rice, vegetables and eggs after work, then packed portions into containers. It felt sensible and satisfying until she realised it was nearly nine o’clock and she still had laundry to do.

Daniel came into the kitchen as she was wiping the counter. “You don’t have to make five lunches.”

“I know. I said I would try.”

“You look exhausted.”

She was annoyed at the concern, though not because it was wrong. “I’m trying to see what things actually cost. I don’t want us to spend £40 on takeaway because we had no plan.”

“Neither do I. But the plan also costs you time.”

Maya put the clean containers away. She had counted the price of the food, not the work of preparing it. That did not mean the lunches were a bad idea. It meant they were not free.

They kept receipts for the rest of the week. On Thursday, Daniel bought a sandwich near the office for £6.40 after a delayed meeting left him no time to eat the lunch he had brought. On Friday, Maya picked up soup and bread for £5.80 after an appointment ran late.

On Saturday they shopped together. Their groceries came to £102.70. They noticed that the total included food for a meal with friends, so they marked £18.20 separately rather than treating all of it as ordinary shared groceries.

“That is the sort of detail I miss,” Daniel said, studying the receipt.

“I miss it too. I’m good at making a plan and bad at noticing when the week has changed.”

That afternoon, an unexpected cost appeared: Maya’s travel card needed replacing after she lost it on the bus. The replacement and remaining journeys cost £28. Daniel had assumed her travel spending was fixed. She had assumed the same about his.

On Sunday, they sat at the kitchen table with the week’s receipts. Daniel had made a strict plan for the week: no unplanned spending, packed lunches, one shop. It had failed by Wednesday, and he had been cross with himself.

“I thought if I made the rules strict enough, I wouldn’t have to think,” he said. “But then a meeting changes, or I’m tired, or something breaks. I treat that as proof I’ve failed.”

“A plan should leave room for life,” Maya said. “I forget that too. I keep thinking a cheaper choice is always the best choice, even when I’m too tired to manage it.”

They kept the idea of packed lunches, but changed it. Maya would prepare two portions on Sunday, not five. Daniel would make his own lunch on the evenings he had the energy. They agreed that buying food at work sometimes was an expense to plan for, not a moral failure.

The receipts did not offer a dramatic answer. They showed small decisions, changing days and the cost of having little time. Maya put the week’s total into the notebook. Then she added a note beside it: “A budget must be usable when we are tired.” Daniel read it and nodded.

9 Two Ways to Divide

They returned to the question they had left unfinished: how to divide the shared costs if the rent increase went ahead. Their monthly shared budget was now clear. Rent would be £1,560, bills £310 and groceries £420. Together, those came to £2,290.

Divided equally, each person would pay £1,145. The other option was to divide the bill according to their take-home incomes. Maya received £1,850 a month and Daniel £2,120. Together, that was £3,970. Maya’s share was about 46.6 per cent and Daniel’s about 53.4 per cent.

They checked the calculation twice. Maya’s proportional share was about £1,066, and Daniel’s about £1,224. The rounded amounts added to £2,290.

“Equal feels simple,” Daniel said. “But it takes a bigger part of your income.”

“And proportional feels fair to me,” Maya replied. “But I don’t want you to pay more just because you earn more, as if your money belongs to both of us.”

Daniel explained that his rail pass and visits to his sister were expensive. He also spent money on climbing, which mattered to him because it gave him exercise and a social life. He knew he could reduce those costs, but he did not want every personal interest to become an argument.

Maya told him about the money she sent her mother. It was usually £140 a month and sometimes more. She did not expect Daniel to take responsibility for it, but it reduced what she had available after shared expenses.

“Those are not the same kind of cost,” Daniel said. “My climbing is a choice. Your support for your mum is an obligation, even if it is not fixed.”

“It is still your choice to help your sister with travel sometimes,” Maya said. “I don’t want us to decide that one person’s family matters and the other’s does not.”

They sat quietly. Each had made a fair point, and neither point made the arithmetic comfortable.

They tested both methods against the rest of the budget. For this first version, they included individual costs as well as shared ones. Maya’s individual monthly costs were £140 for support to her mother, £110 for travel, £55 for her phone and other personal bills, £20 for health costs, and £15 for annual insurance. That was £340 in all.

Daniel’s individual costs were £180 for travel, £45 for his phone and other personal bills, £25 for transport and bicycle costs, and £90 for climbing and equipment. That was also £340.

At equal sharing, Maya’s outgoings were £1,145 plus £340, or £1,485, leaving £365 from her £1,850 income. Daniel’s were also £1,485, leaving £635 from £2,120.

At income-based sharing, Maya would pay £1,066 plus £340, leaving £444. Daniel would pay £1,224 plus £340, leaving £556. Neither version left much room for savings, emergencies, or an unusually expensive month.

“My list still misses things,” Maya said. “I’ve put a figure next to health costs, but it won’t always be enough.”

Daniel looked at the two totals. “I don’t want us to call one method fair and stop listening. I think we need to decide what the money is for, not only what the percentages are.”

Maya agreed. They did not choose that night. They wrote both choices down, including the money left over, and promised not to treat the difference as a test of affection. The numbers were a starting point for a decision, not a verdict on either person.

10 The Saturday Invitation

On Monday, Daniel received a message from Alex, a friend from work. Alex was organising a weekend trip to a music event outside the city. The ticket was £68, the coach would be about £32, and food there might cost another £25. Daniel read the message twice before telling Maya.

“It sounds good,” she said. She had been looking forward to a quiet weekend, but she also felt the heavy weeks gathering around them. “I’d like to go somewhere too.”

“It’s nearly £125 before anything unexpected.”

“I know.” She looked back at the figures on their table. “I want a social break. I don’t want our whole life to become receipts and choices we can’t afford.”

Daniel was relieved she had said it. He had expected her to tell him that spending anything on the event was irresponsible. At the same time, he worried that agreeing to a weekend away would make the new rent feel easier than it was.

Maya considered her own spending. She had been putting money aside for a visit to her mother, and the visit mattered. She could not simply spend that money on a day out. Daniel’s trip was his decision, but she did not want to pretend that only his leisure had a price.

“I don’t want to stop you going,” she said. “But I want us to have a small amount for doing things together as well. Not every month needs to be expensive.”

“Could we go to the free gallery on Saturday, then have lunch at home before we leave?” Daniel asked.

“That sounds good. And you can decide about the event from your personal money.”

They agreed that shared essentials would not pay for the trip. Daniel checked his own account and chose a spending limit of £125. He would go only if the coach and food stayed within it. He did not ask Maya to cover a shortfall.

The conversation changed when Daniel admitted he had been avoiding his climbing group’s message. A monthly membership cost £52, and he had paid it without using the wall much during a busy month. His other £38 for climbing equipment was not monthly, but he had treated it as if it were a regular cost.

“I can pause the membership for one month,” he said. “But I don’t want to give it up. It keeps me active, and I’ve made friends there.”

“You shouldn’t have to give up everything that makes life feel like yours,” Maya said. “But you can’t put equipment and membership into the same monthly number if one of them changes.”

They revised Daniel’s personal budget. His regular climbing membership remained £52; equipment would come from an irregular-cost pot rather than being assumed every month. His previous £90 estimate had mixed the two.

Maya also admitted that she had bought a £24 notebook and pen set while trying to organise their money. “I wanted to feel in control,” she said. “It wasn’t a sensible purchase.”

Daniel smiled, not unkindly. “You already had three notebooks.”

“I know.”

They both had reasons for what they spent, and reasons did not always make a purchase wise. Daniel decided to go to the event and keep within his limit. Maya would visit the gallery with him. They also planned a low-cost evening with friends rather than treating social life as something they could afford only when money was easy.

The choice did not fix their monthly budget. It did remind them that a life made only of avoiding costs could become another kind of pressure.

11 A Budget That Can Move

By Thursday, the flat felt calmer, though the rent letter still sat on the table. Maya and Daniel had not received a final answer from Mrs Bennett. They decided to make a budget for the likely increase, while leaving room to change it if the rent figure or their circumstances changed.

They wrote the shared costs clearly: rent £1,560, bills £310, and groceries £420. The total was £2,290 a month. They wrote beside it that groceries were a planned amount, not a guarantee that every month would be identical.

The equal split was £1,145 each. The income-based split, using Maya’s £1,850 and Daniel’s £2,120 take-home pay, was approximately 46.6 per cent and 53.4 per cent: £1,066 for Maya and £1,224 for Daniel.

“The percentages are rounded,” Daniel said. “But the pounds add up exactly.”

“And neither method is a complete description of what we each carry,” Maya replied.

They looked again at individual costs. Maya’s regular estimate was £340: £140 for her mother, £110 for travel, £55 for her phone and personal bills, £20 for health, and £15 set aside for annual insurance. Her mother’s needs could make the first figure higher. Her travel could rise when she had appointments across Manchester.

Daniel’s regular estimate was £340 too: £180 for travel, £45 for his phone and personal bills, £25 for transport and bicycle costs, and £90 for climbing. They changed the climbing line to £52 for membership plus £38 saved towards equipment and other irregular climbing costs. His transport figure also needed watching because rail fares did not always fit neatly into a monthly average.

“We should keep emergency savings separate from these,” Maya said.

“Yes. Otherwise we’ll call money for a broken boiler ‘spare’ and spend it on something else.”

They added modest savings targets but agreed not to rely on reaching them every month. A week with extra travel, medicine, or family support could change the result.

Sophie came round after work. She listened while they explained the two ways of sharing the common costs. Then she asked, “What would each of you choose if you were not afraid of sounding selfish?”

Daniel thought for a moment. “I prefer the income-based split, but I don’t want Maya’s family support used to justify me paying more without a conversation.”

Maya nodded. “I prefer it too, because equal payments leave me less room. But I don’t want Daniel to feel that his higher salary means I get to decide how he spends it.”

Sophie said, “Then say that to each other, and review the plan. Fairness can be an agreement you keep checking, not a number you discover once.”

They chose the income-based split for the next two months: Maya would pay £1,066 into the shared account and Daniel £1,224. They would review it if either person’s income or major costs changed. Their individual expenses would stay individual, and neither would quietly take money from the shared account to cover personal spending. They would keep a small record of shared purchases and discuss an unusually high bill before it became an argument.

Maya still had limited money after her regular costs. Daniel had more room than she did, but not enough to make the rent increase feel harmless. Their calculations showed pressure coming from several directions at once: housing, bills, food, travel, health, family responsibility, and the cost of having too little time.

That evening, James, a colleague of Daniel’s, asked how the flat search was going. Daniel told him they were facing a rent increase and trying to plan carefully. James shared, without giving details he did not want to discuss, that he had once used credit to cover ordinary bills and had spent months paying interest afterward.

“I thought borrowing would get me through one difficult month,” James said. “The next month was difficult too.” He did not tell Daniel what to do. He only said that knowing the cost before agreeing to it had helped him avoid making the problem larger.

On the walk home, Daniel told Maya. They agreed not to borrow to make the new rent appear affordable. They would keep talking with Mrs Bennett, examine other housing options if necessary, and make decisions from real figures rather than panic.

Maya closed the notebook. It did not promise that the coming months would be easy. It showed what they could carry, what they could not control, and which choices belonged to them. For now, that was a more honest kind of comfort.

12 The Invitation

Alex’s message arrived while Maya was on her lunch break. It showed a photograph of a table covered in small plates and candles. Under it, Alex had written: “Birthday dinner on Saturday. I’ve booked the place we talked about. Please come — it’ll be lovely to have everyone together.”

Maya knew the restaurant. She had once looked at its menu and calculated that a main course, a drink and the service charge would cost about £58. The bus there and back would add another few pounds. Her pay had arrived that morning, but the balance in her account already had jobs waiting for it.

She typed, “I’d love to, but I can’t afford it just now.” She stared at the sentence, then deleted it. Alex had recently posted pictures from a weekend in Edinburgh, with a bright hotel room behind her. Comparing lives was not fair, Maya knew, but she did it anyway. Alex had a better-paid job and no family member who needed regular help.

At last Maya wrote, “Thank you for inviting me. I’m sorry, but I have to miss this one. I hope you have a great birthday.” She pressed send quickly, before she could change her mind.

The reply came at once: “Oh, that’s a shame. Are you sure? We can split the bill. Everyone will be there.”

Maya put her phone face down. The word split made her feel worse. She could imagine the others paying without thinking, while she measured the cost against the electricity bill. She had done nothing wrong, yet shame settled on her like a coat.

At home, Daniel was at the kitchen table with his laptop open. A spreadsheet filled the screen, its rows coloured green, amber and red. He had added a column for “optional spending” and was checking the figures with a pencil.

“Alex’s birthday?” he asked, seeing the message on Maya’s phone.

“I’m not going.”

“Good decision,” Daniel said. “That’s nearly sixty pounds saved.”

Maya looked at him. “I didn’t save it. I just didn’t go.”

“I know what you mean.” He turned the laptop slightly. “I’m trying to make the numbers less frightening. If we can see what’s coming, we can choose.”

She sat down, still holding her bag. “Sometimes seeing every small thing makes it feel as if every small thing is wrong.”

Daniel’s brow tightened. “The small things add up.”

“So do the big things,” Maya said. “The rent increase didn’t happen because I bought a sandwich.”

He looked back at the spreadsheet. “I didn’t say it did.”

“No. You only count the sandwich.”

The silence that followed was not a dramatic one. The fridge hummed, a bus passed outside, and someone upstairs moved a chair. Daniel closed the laptop halfway.

Maya went to her room and took off her coat. She thought about asking Alex to meet for a walk instead, but she did not want to sound as if she were making excuses. Then she thought of her mother, who had said on the phone that the reduced hours at work were still difficult. The next transfer was due in a few days.

Maya opened her banking app, checked the date, and closed it again. The balance was enough for the planned transfer and the bills, if nothing unexpected happened. It was not enough to make her feel safe.

In the kitchen, Daniel reopened the spreadsheet. For him, the neat rows seemed to offer a way through the pressure. For Maya, the invitation she had declined sat beside the growing list of things she could not explain. Neither of them said goodnight for a long time.

13 The Neat Column

On Sunday morning, Daniel made a new version of the household sheet. He had not slept well, and the figures seemed easier to manage than the thoughts that kept him awake. He added a tab called “Maya and Daniel — personal costs” and entered the amounts they had discussed in earlier budget talks.

Their shared monthly costs came to £1,693: rent of £1,320, council tax of £160, gas and electricity of £145, water of £38 and broadband of £30. Half was £846.50 each. He had included those figures before, but now he added the due dates and a box to show whether each payment had been made.

He did not mean to make it a test. He told himself it was a tool. If they could see the exact shape of their spending, they could avoid overdrafts and decide what to delay.

Maya came in wearing socks, carrying a mug. “You’re working on it again?”

“Just finishing the monthly view. It should help us both.”

She glanced at the screen. One line was labelled “treats and small extras.” Beside it, Daniel had entered a figure based on the receipts they had kept. A café lunch and a paperback were marked as examples.

“That book was for the train,” Maya said.

“It’s still a personal purchase.”

“It was a paperback that made a long journey better.”

Daniel paused. “I’m not saying you shouldn’t buy it.”

“You put it in red.”

He looked. He had coloured the line red because it was over the amount he had chosen as a target. He changed the colour to amber, but the correction seemed only to underline her point.

“The target isn’t a rule,” he said. “It’s a way to notice patterns.”

“You notice patterns. I notice that everything enjoyable turns into a warning.”

Daniel closed the laptop. “I am trying to protect us from a bad month.”

“I know. But I don’t want to need permission to buy a book.”

“You don’t need permission.”

“Then don’t build a list that makes me feel I do.”

He had a reply ready, about their rent and the price of food, but he swallowed it. He remembered how Maya had declined the birthday dinner without blaming anyone. He had called it a good decision, as if she had passed a test.

“I can change the categories,” he said. “Maybe we should set a personal amount each, after the shared bills.”

“That would be better,” Maya said. She took her mug to the sink. “But the figures still need to include the real things.”

“They do.”

She almost told him about the £120 she sent her mother every month. The words rose, then stopped. Daniel would add the amount to the sheet and ask what else could be reduced. She could already imagine the red cell. The help mattered; so did her privacy. She was tired of defending a decision that had never felt optional.

Daniel opened the laptop again, more slowly this time. He removed “treats” and typed “personal spending.” It looked less accusing, but he knew the name was not the whole problem.

Across the room, Maya read a message from her mother. Her next shift had been cancelled. Maya replied with a heart and a promise to call later. Then she put the phone away, leaving the budget open on the table and her own figures still incomplete.

14 The Dentist’s Estimate

The pain began as a small ache on Monday and became sharper by Wednesday. Maya tried to ignore it during work, but cold water made her wince. She called a dental practice near the bus route and took the first appointment they could offer.

The dentist examined a back tooth and explained that an old filling had cracked. The tooth could be repaired, but the work would cost £380. Maya asked whether it could wait. The dentist said a short delay might be possible, but the pain or damage could get worse. There was no promise that waiting would make the treatment simpler.

On the bus home, Maya held the estimate in her hand. The paper seemed heavier than it was. She had £250 in a small emergency account, saved little by little for exactly the sort of surprise she disliked having. She could use that money and pay the remaining £130 from her next wages, though it would leave little room for other costs.

At home she found Daniel in the kitchen, comparing energy tariffs on his computer. She placed the estimate beside him.

“The dentist says it needs repairing. Three hundred and eighty pounds.”

He read it carefully. “That’s a lot.”

“Yes.”

“Can it wait until next month?”

“Maybe, but the dentist said the pain could get worse.”

Daniel leaned back. He was not trying to dismiss her, but his face had the serious look he used when he saw a number he could not fit into the month. “We should check what’s due before deciding.”

“I don’t want to decide not to have it done.”

“I didn’t say that. I mean we can look at the timing. Your emergency savings are for things like this, aren’t they?”

“They are.” Maya took a breath. “I can use £250 and pay the other £130 from wages. I’ll delay buying a new coat. The one I have will last a bit longer.”

She had been planning to replace it because the lining was coming away. Daniel knew she had mentioned it, though he had not thought it urgent.

“That sounds sensible,” he said.

Maya frowned. “It’s not exactly a choice I’m happy about.”

“I know.”

“And I’m not asking you to pay for it.”

“I didn’t think you were.”

His answer was reasonable, but she heard the carefulness in it. She was tired of being careful about every sentence. “Could you just say you’re sorry it’s happened?”

Daniel looked surprised. “I am. I’m sorry.”

She nodded. “Thanks.”

They agreed that Maya would book the treatment for the following week. She would transfer £250 from her emergency account and cover the rest herself. Daniel offered to review the shared bills with her, but she said she could manage this one.

That evening, Daniel asked James, a friend from work, whether he knew a good way to make a budget feel less overwhelming. James listened and said, “You know, not looking at money and trying to control every pound can both come from the same fear.”

Daniel gave a short laugh. “Which one do you think I do?”

“You already know.” James did not say it unkindly. “A plan can help. But if the plan turns every decision into a judgement, it stops being useful.”

Daniel thought of the red cell beside Maya’s paperback. At home, the estimate lay on the table. He moved it away from the laptop so it would not become another row before it became a treatment.

15 The Part She Left Out

Maya booked the dental appointment for Thursday afternoon. She paid the £250 deposit from her emergency account and marked the remaining £130 in her own notes. She would delay the coat and use the bus rather than pay for a taxi after the appointment. The arrangements were ordinary, but making them left her tired.

On Tuesday evening, Daniel asked if they could check their separate monthly costs together. He had changed the sheet so that each of them had a personal section. “I want to be sure the plan includes everything that matters,” he said.

Maya looked at the open laptop. She had meant to tell him before. Each month she sent £120 to her mother, whose hours at work had been reduced. Her mother had not demanded it. Maya had offered after hearing that the cost of heating and food was becoming difficult.

There was another figure she had kept to herself: £600 on a credit card. It had grown through several months of groceries, travel and small shortfalls. She paid the minimum of £45 each month. She hated the balance and had been trying to lower it, but her mother’s situation had made that harder.

“There’s something I should have told you,” Maya said.

Daniel stopped typing.

“I send my mum £120 every month. Since her hours were cut. And I have a credit-card balance of £600. The minimum payment is £45.”

Daniel sat very still. “How long has that been going on?”

“The help started a few months ago. The card balance has been there longer.”

“You didn’t tell me when we made the budget.”

“No.”

“Why not?”

Maya had expected the question, but it still hurt. “I was embarrassed. And I thought you’d treat it as another problem to solve.”

“It is a problem we need to understand.”

“That’s exactly what I mean.”

Daniel lowered his voice. “I’m not angry that you help your mother. I’m upset that I thought we were making decisions with the same information.”

“I understand that.”

“Do you? We’ve been talking about what we can afford, and I didn’t know about £165 going out every month.”

“I know it’s a lot. But Mum’s hours were reduced through no fault of hers. I couldn’t listen to her worry about basic costs and pretend I had nothing to offer.”

“You could have told me.”

“And then what? You would put it in the spreadsheet, choose a limit, and ask what I could cut.”

“That isn’t fair.”

“Maybe not. But you did turn a paperback into a red warning.”

Daniel’s jaw tightened. “I am trying to make sure we can pay the rent.”

“And I’m trying to help my family without feeling like a child reporting what I spent.”

The argument did not explode. It grew from months of unfinished conversations: the rent increase, the receipts, the denied invitation, the careful silences. Daniel said she had left him to guess at the real budget. Maya said his need to know every detail had made honesty feel unsafe.

At last Daniel shut the laptop. “I don’t know how to respond to this without sounding controlling.”

“I don’t know how to tell you something difficult without expecting a judgement.”

Neither of them moved to take back the words. The figures remained where they were, but now they had names and histories. The £120 was not just an expense. The £600 was not a careless choice. And the silence between them had cost something too.

16 Two Doors

That night Maya slept in the small spare room, where they kept the drying rack and a box of old books. She told herself it was only because she wanted space. The narrow bed made her shoulder ache, and the radiator clicked as it cooled.

She thought about the argument and tried to separate what she had felt from what Daniel had actually said. He had not called her irresponsible. He had not told her to stop helping her mother. He had said he was upset that he had not known. That was fair. But the way he watched each cost had made her expect criticism before he spoke.

In the bedroom, Daniel lay awake with his phone on the bedside table. He opened the budget app, then closed it. He had wanted to make a safer plan, not to make Maya feel watched. Yet his system had given him a sense of control, and he had not asked whether she felt safe inside it.

In the morning, they passed one another in the hall. “The dentist is at three tomorrow,” Maya said.

“I remember. Do you want me to come with you?”

“No, thanks.”

“All right.”

It was a small exchange, but both of them noticed how carefully they spoke.

At work, Maya called her mother during a break. Her mother sounded embarrassed when Maya asked whether she still needed the monthly help.

“I don’t want you getting into trouble,” her mother said. “You have your own bills.”

“I know. I can help for now, but I need to be honest about what I can manage.”

Her mother was quiet for a moment. “I don’t like taking money from you.”

“It isn’t a loan. I offered because I love you. But I don’t want either of us pretending the situation is easy.”

They agreed to speak again at the weekend. Maya did not promise that the £120 would continue forever. She also did not withdraw it out of anger or shame. The amount was a commitment she had chosen, but it needed to be considered alongside her own needs.

Daniel spoke to James again after work. He described the argument without defending himself. James listened and said, “When you don’t know the numbers, you worry. When you know them, you try to control them. Those are different habits, but they can both keep you from having the conversation.”

Daniel rubbed his forehead. “I thought a spreadsheet would make the conversation easier.”

“It can show where the money goes. It can’t decide what matters to both of you.”

James suggested that Daniel should ask before entering Maya’s personal costs into a shared plan. He also reminded him that the rent increase and higher bills were not failures caused by either flatmate. “There’s only so much a budget can repair,” he said.

At home, Daniel made tea and left it on the kitchen table. He did not add the cost to a category or ask Maya to repay half. When she came in, she thanked him and sat at the other end of the table.

“I don’t want to go back to pretending I’m fine,” she said.

“I don’t want you to,” Daniel replied. “I also need to know what affects the household decisions.”

“That’s fair. But I need to be part of those decisions, not a set of figures you manage.”

“Yes,” he said, after a pause. “I can see that.”

They did not solve the budget that evening. Maya took her tea to the spare room, and Daniel returned to the bedroom. The doors were not slammed. Still, they remained closed, each holding a different part of the same problem.

17 A Table Between Them

Maya’s dental appointment took just over an hour. The dentist repaired the tooth and confirmed the cost: £380 in total. Maya had already paid £250, so she settled the remaining £130 by card. The treatment was uncomfortable, but the pain had been making it hard to concentrate. She was relieved it was done and worried about the money at the same time.

At home she entered the payment in her own notes, not Daniel’s spreadsheet. The emergency account now had almost nothing left. She would need to rebuild it slowly. The coat could wait, but she could not pretend that every future surprise would be manageable.

Daniel asked how it had gone. Maya told him the treatment was complete and the final payment had been £130.

“I’m glad you got it done,” he said. “And I’m sorry I made the estimate feel like a problem you had to defend.”

Maya touched her cheek. “I was worried about the cost. I was also worried you’d think I should have planned for it.”

“I did wonder whether it could wait. That wasn’t helpful after you’d already spoken to the dentist.”

“No. But asking once isn’t the same as telling me what to do.”

They sat at the kitchen table with the laptop closed. Daniel had asked if they could talk, and Maya had agreed on the condition that they did not try to finish everything in one evening.

“I want to understand the full picture,” he said. “Not so I can control your money. So we can make decisions about the rent and shared bills with real information.”

“I can tell you the amounts that affect my monthly position,” Maya said. “The £120 to Mum, and the £45 minimum payment. I’ll also tell you if I’m thinking of changing either one. But I need my own spending money to stay mine.”

Daniel nodded. “That’s reasonable. I’ll stop putting your personal purchases into the sheet. We can agree on the shared costs and each take responsibility for our own part.”

“And the card balance?”

“We should include it in the discussion, but you choose how to pay it down.”

Maya appreciated that he had said choose. She also knew the balance could not simply be ignored. “I want to pay more than the minimum when I can. But with the dental cost, this month I may only manage the £45.”

“That makes sense.”

Daniel opened the laptop, then turned it so they could both see. He showed the shared total they had already discussed: £1,693 a month, divided equally at £846.50 each. The figures had not changed. The new information did change what the two of them needed to consider when planning their own months.

“We can’t make the rent smaller,” Maya said. “And we shouldn’t pretend a coffee is the answer to that.”

“Agreed,” Daniel said. “We can check the household bills, ask the landlord whether the increase has any flexibility, and look at whether a different place would actually cost less after moving expenses.”

“Not move in a panic,” Maya said.

“No. Compare properly.”

They wrote down two tasks: Daniel would check the council tax dates and household energy use; Maya would review her own transport costs and talk with her mother about what support was realistic. Neither task promised a large saving. Both were more honest than blaming one café visit.

Before they stopped, Maya said, “I should have told you earlier.”

Daniel answered, “I should have made it easier to tell me.”

It was not a complete apology from either of them, but it was true. They did not finish the budget or decide the future of the £120. They agreed only that the next conversation would include both people, not just the figures.

Later, Maya returned to the spare room. Daniel went to his own bedroom. The doors closed again, but this time the silence felt less like punishment than a pause. In the morning, they would still have the same rent, the same rising costs and the same choices. At least now they had begun to name them together.

18 A Different Kind of Agreement

The table between Maya and Daniel still held the remains of their argument: two mugs, a folded rent letter and a calculator. But neither of them was speaking as if the other were the problem now.

Daniel turned the calculator so Maya could see the screen. “I checked the figures again. Our take-home pay is £2,283 for you and £2,617 for me. That makes £4,900 altogether.”

“And we said the flat costs shouldn’t be split exactly in half,” Maya replied. She kept one hand around her mug, though the tea had gone cold. “Not when our incomes are different.”

They had agreed the previous evening to divide their shared monthly costs according to their earnings. Maya’s share came to about 46.6 per cent; Daniel’s was about 53.4 per cent. They had rounded the actual payments to £1,066 and £1,224, which together made £2,290.

Daniel tapped the rent letter. “That’s the new figure. I still don’t like it.”

“Neither do I. But we can dislike it without pretending the landlord invented every cost in the building.”

They wrote out what the shared total contained: rent, £1,680; council tax, £155; estimated energy, £165; water, £40; broadband, £35; shared groceries, £160; and household supplies, £55. Daniel checked the addition twice. The total was £2,290.

The grocery figure was modest. It covered ingredients for meals they both agreed to eat, not every snack or lunch either of them bought separately. They decided to review it after a month rather than promise themselves they could live on numbers that looked neat on paper.

“My £120 for Mum stays separate,” Maya said. Her voice was steady, but she watched Daniel’s face. “It helps with her food and prescriptions. I don’t want it counted as spare money.”

“It isn’t spare,” he said. “And my work costs aren’t optional just because they don’t show up in the rent.” He explained that his transport and occasional equipment costs had been making his own budget tight. “I want to record them honestly, not pretend I can walk across Manchester in wet work boots.”

Maya gave him a small smile. “That would save on transport and increase your laundry bill.”

They agreed their own costs would stay individual. Their written budget allowed Maya £110 for transport, £65 for health costs, £24 for her phone, £100 for food and small personal top-ups, and £120 for family support. Daniel set aside £190 for transport, £26 for his phone, £35 for work equipment, and £100 for his own food and top-ups.

“We can try to put £100 each into a shared emergency fund,” Daniel said. “If an actual emergency happens, we use it. If one month makes that impossible, we talk before changing the plan.”

“And personal spending?” Maya asked.

“After bills, necessary costs, and that fund. It’s not a crime to buy something because we like it.”

“Good,” she said. “I was starting to feel that every pound needed a witness.”

They wrote the rules on a sheet of paper and put it on the fridge. It did not solve the increase in their rent or make their wages stretch further. It did, however, give each of them a way to see the other’s responsibilities without deciding which one mattered more.

19 Small Changes, Not Small Lives

On Sunday afternoon, Maya and Daniel went through their bank accounts together. They did not exchange passwords or inspect every private purchase. Each opened their own account on their own phone and read out the regular payments they wanted to review.

Daniel found a subscription to a film service he had stopped using months ago. Maya discovered that she was still paying for a language app she had not opened since winter. They checked the cancellation dates and the next payment dates before making changes.

“That saves me £11.99 a month,” Daniel said. He sounded pleased, then careful. “It doesn’t solve the rent.”

“No,” Maya replied. “It just means I’m not paying for something I don’t use.” Her app cost £8.49. She cancelled it, but kept her music subscription because she listened every day on the bus.

Together, they found £20.48 in monthly payments they could stop. It was useful, but it was not the answer to a £70 rent rise or a larger energy bill. They both understood the difference.

They also compared their transport options. Daniel’s shifts began too early for some bus journeys, and on late nights the route home was unreliable. He paid for a monthly travel pass and kept money aside for occasional taxis when there was no safe or practical alternative. He checked whether a different pass would cover his route, but the cheaper one would leave him paying for extra journeys.

Maya used a bus pass for her regular work journey and walked when the weather and her health allowed. Her recurring pain made a long walk a poor saving on some days. She decided to keep a small transport allowance for days when walking was not realistic, rather than treat every taxi as carelessness.

“I used to think you could always choose the cheapest way,” Daniel said as he compared the routes on his screen. “But a journey has to work with your shift.”

“And a cheap plan that leaves me in pain isn’t cheap,” Maya said. She was pleased he had noticed, though she did not want to turn the moment into a victory.

For food, they planned four shared dinners each week and a basic list of ingredients. The £160 shared-grocery allowance would cover the items they both used, including oats, rice, vegetables, eggs and ingredients for soup. They would pay for personal snacks and lunches separately. If the shared food ran out early, they would check what happened before simply blaming one another.

They also made a list of annual and irregular bills. Maya’s health costs were not perfectly regular, so she would try to keep a personal reserve. Daniel planned for replacement work equipment and an annual travel-related charge. They would each set aside £50 a month from their own money for bills that did not arrive monthly.

That left their shared emergency fund separate from their personal reserves. Their target was £100 each every month, or £200 in total. They agreed the target was an aim, not a test of character.

The next day, Mrs Bennett replied to Daniel’s message about the rent. She said the increase reflected higher insurance, maintenance and service costs for the building. She offered to send a clearer breakdown and explained that the same pressures had affected several flats.

“She doesn’t sound delighted about it either,” Daniel said.

“She can be decent and still be asking us for more money,” Maya replied. “Those things can both be true.”

They left the conversation with a meal plan, two cancelled subscriptions and no fantasy that these changes could erase the structural pressure. It was a smaller victory than either of them wanted, but a more honest one.

20 The First Full Month

The first month of the new arrangement began with two standing payments and a shared note on the fridge. Maya transferred £1,066 into the household account. Daniel transferred £1,224. The amounts were not identical, but each was a clear contribution towards the same shared bills.

Their first grocery shop went well. They planned meals around food that could be used in more than one dish: carrots for soup and a tray bake, lentils for curry, and a large bag of rice. On Wednesday, Daniel came home late and found a portion of lentil curry waiting in the fridge.

“I thought you might be hungry,” Maya said.

“I was. I bought a sandwich at work, though.” He opened the fridge. “I can take this for lunch tomorrow.”

“Then it hasn’t been wasted.”

The plan felt less smooth when Daniel’s work boots split at the side. His employer required a particular kind, and the old pair could not be repaired safely. He found a suitable pair for £78. He had saved £35 a month for work equipment, but the amount in that pot was not yet enough.

He showed Maya the receipt without asking her to pay. “This is what I meant about work costs. I can’t wait three months for the savings to grow.”

“Could you use your personal reserve?” she asked.

“Some of it. I’ll move £43 from my own flexible money this month. It’s inconvenient, not a household emergency.”

Maya respected that he had brought it up before it affected their shared account. Still, she found herself thinking how easily one purchase could disturb a careful plan. At home, she checked her own account and realised a prescription and a follow-up appointment had cost more than she had expected. Her health allowance was short by £18.

She did not mention it that evening. Daniel saw her looking at the budget page the next morning.

“Something else?” he asked.

“I’m £18 over on health costs. I can cover it from my personal money. I just don’t want the sheet to look as if I failed.”

“It’s a record, not a report card,” he said. He wrote the amount beside her health line, not as a penalty but as information. They agreed she would adjust her personal reserve next month if the higher cost continued.

At the end of the month, the shared emergency fund held £200. They had also saved £50 each in personal reserves for annual and irregular bills. The transfers had left them with less unplanned spending than they were used to, but neither had needed to borrow or miss a necessary payment.

Maya’s £120 support for her mother had gone out as usual. Daniel’s transport costs had been close to his estimate, though he had paid for two late journeys home. Their personal budgets were not equally comfortable, and neither pretended they were.

They sat at the table with the figures between them. The shared bills had been paid. The food had lasted. A pair of boots and a medical appointment had stretched the personal columns.

“So, is it working?” Daniel asked.

Maya considered before answering. “It’s working as a system. That’s not the same as everything being easy.”

“Fair.”

She closed the notebook. It mattered that they could say the truth without making it an accusation.

21 A Higher Bill

The energy estimate had been £165 a month, but the next statement showed that their actual use had cost more. The account had built up a shortfall, and the supplier proposed raising the monthly payment to £205.

Daniel read the message twice. “That’s another £40 every month if we accept it.”

“If we don’t, the shortfall still exists,” Maya said. “Let’s check the meter readings and the dates before we decide.”

They compared the readings with the statement. One reading had been estimated, and the actual number was higher. There was no clear billing error, though they asked the supplier to confirm the calculation. They also checked the heating controls and found that the timer had been running longer than either of them remembered.

Daniel had turned the heating up after a run of cold nights. Maya had not wanted to complain because she had been waking with pain and found the cold made it harder to move. Neither had been wasteful. They had each been responding to a real need, without discussing how the setting affected the bill.

“I should have said why I changed it,” Daniel admitted. “I just thought you’d notice.”

“I should have said I was cold instead of assuming you’d know,” Maya replied. “But we shouldn’t make the flat uncomfortable to prove we can save money.”

They agreed to use the timer more carefully, keep one warmer room for evenings, and wear extra layers when that worked for them. Maya would continue using the heating when her health required it. Daniel would check the meter monthly so they were not surprised by another adjustment.

The £40 increase could not come from the original shared total without changing something. Their shared monthly costs would now be £2,330: rent, £1,680; council tax, £155; energy, £205; water, £40; broadband, £35; shared groceries, £160; household supplies, £55.

Using the same income-based split, they revised their transfers to £1,085 for Maya and £1,245 for Daniel. Together, those amounts covered £2,330. The proportions remained close to 46.6 and 53.4 per cent.

“That leaves less room in both budgets,” Daniel said.

“Yes. It doesn’t mean our plan was foolish. The estimate was wrong for our actual use.”

For that month, they reduced their shared emergency-fund contributions from £100 each to £80 each while the energy payment settled. They kept the money already saved in the fund untouched. They did not cancel Maya’s family support or pretend Daniel’s work costs could disappear.

The change made both of them uneasy. Maya worried that lowering the savings would become permanent. Daniel worried that the new bill would keep rising. They wrote a review date on the calendar, two months away, and agreed to restore the £100 contributions if their ordinary costs allowed it.

A week later, Mrs Bennett sent the building-cost breakdown she had promised. Insurance and maintenance had risen, and the figures matched what she had described. It did not make the rent affordable; it did make the explanation clearer.

“I still wish she could charge less,” Daniel said.

“So do I,” Maya answered. “Understanding the reason isn’t the same as liking the result.”

That evening, she cooked soup and Daniel made toast. They ate in the warmer room. The higher bill remained on the table, but it no longer looked like evidence that either flatmate had been careless.

22 An Invitation Without Guilt

A few days later, Maya received an invitation to dinner from colleagues. The restaurant was new, and the message suggested sharing several dishes. She liked the people who had invited her. She also knew that a meal there could cost more than she had planned for personal spending.

Her first reaction was to decline quickly. The second was to feel resentful, as if the budget had made the choice for her.

She looked at her account before replying. The shared transfers, her £120 for her mother, transport, phone and health costs were covered. Her personal reserve had needed extra attention this month, and she had agreed to put £80 into the shared emergency fund instead of £100. There was still some money available for enjoyment, but not enough to ignore the price.

Maya asked the group what the restaurant usually cost. A colleague estimated about £38 each before drinks. Maya replied that she could not manage that this week, but asked if they would like to meet at a park café another time. Two colleagues said yes. One suggested a picnic when the weather improved.

It was not the same evening she had first imagined. It was still a choice she had made, rather than a punishment imposed by the spreadsheet.

Daniel heard about it while they were putting away groceries. “You could have gone and spent the £38,” he said.

“I could. But I would have spent the next week annoyed with myself. I’m not saying nobody should go to that restaurant. I’m saying this wasn’t the evening I wanted badly enough to trade for that money.”

He nodded. “That makes sense. I nearly paid for a sports channel last week because I was tired after work. Then I remembered I hardly watch it.”

“You’re allowed to spend on things you actually enjoy.”

“I know. I just want to notice when I’m spending because I’m worn out.”

They decided to arrange an evening together, not as a budget exercise but because they both needed a break. Daniel found a low-cost film screening at a community cinema. Tickets were £7 each. They planned to eat at home first and buy one small bag of popcorn to share.

On the night, the screening was in a small room above a bookshop. The film was funny in a quiet way, and the audience laughed at different moments. On the walk back, they disagreed about the ending and argued cheerfully over whether the main character had been brave or simply stubborn.

“We spent £17 in total,” Daniel said as they reached the flat. “That includes the tickets and popcorn.”

“And we had a good night,” Maya said. “Both facts matter.”

The following weekend, Maya’s colleagues met her for a park meal. One brought homemade sandwiches, another brought fruit, and Maya bought a hot drink and a pastry for £6.50. They sat on a bench beneath trees, talking about work and the changes happening in the city. Nobody made a performance of how cheaply they had managed it.

Maya returned home feeling included, not deprived. Daniel had spent part of the afternoon helping a friend repair a bicycle and had not paid for entertainment at all. They did not compare the value of their choices.

The budget made room for enjoyment by naming it, not by pretending it cost nothing. There were still expensive meals they would decline and evenings when worry made even a small purchase feel difficult. But they were learning to ask what they wanted, what they could afford, and what they might regret.

23 More Than a Column

By the time their review date arrived, several weeks had passed. The energy payment had been set at £205, and the supplier’s next estimate was closer to their actual use. The flat was warm enough without keeping every room heated all evening. Maya still used the heating when she needed it; Daniel still checked the meter.

They had paid the new shared total of £2,330 for two months. Maya had transferred £1,085 each month and Daniel £1,245. Their shared emergency fund had grown more slowly than planned because they had contributed £80 each during the first month of the higher bill and £100 each in the second.

They reviewed the shared expenses again. Rent was £1,680, council tax £155, energy £205, water £40, broadband £35, shared groceries £160 and household supplies £55. The total was still £2,330.

“The grocery amount was just enough when we planned meals,” Maya said. “But it didn’t cover all the things I bought when I was too tired to cook.”

“I bought lunches at work twice,” Daniel said. “I can plan better, but I don’t want to pretend every shift gives me time to eat what I brought.”

They decided not to raise the shared grocery allowance immediately. Instead, they would keep the existing £160 for food they both used and continue recording personal lunches separately. If shared ingredients repeatedly ran short, they would review the amount with real figures rather than guess.

Maya had continued sending £120 to her mother. She had also put £50 a month aside for irregular health and annual costs, though this month the prescription expense had reduced the balance. Daniel had rebuilt some of his work-equipment reserve after buying the required boots. His £190 transport allowance had covered his usual journeys, but not every late finish.

“Can we go back to £100 each for the emergency fund?” Daniel asked.

“This month, yes,” Maya said. “But let’s not treat that as a promise for every month. If Mum needs extra help or my health costs change, I’ll tell you.”

“And if work costs jump, I’ll do the same.”

They agreed to restore the regular £100 contributions while keeping their personal reserves separate. Their remaining personal money was not equal, because their incomes and responsibilities were not equal. Each would manage personal spending without having to justify every small pleasure, while giving the other notice if a major change could affect shared payments.

Daniel looked at the budget sheet on the fridge. The lines were straight, the totals correct, and the notes in the margins were beginning to outnumber the original categories.

“I used to look at the amount you sent your mum and think it was money that might help with the flat,” he said. “That wasn’t fair. It’s a real responsibility.”

Maya took a moment before answering. “I used to think you could always spend less on getting to work if you planned properly. I know that isn’t true for every shift.”

Neither apologised as if one sentence could erase the way they had judged each other. But both had made an effort to see beyond the figures.

They spent Saturday evening at home, cooking a meal from the food they had already bought. Daniel burned one side of the flatbread. Maya laughed, then helped turn the rest before it caught. They talked about the film they had seen and the people Maya had met at the park.

The rent was still high. Bills could still surprise them, and their savings were small beside the costs of a serious emergency. Their plan did not make low wages, rising prices or family care disappear. It gave them a way to respond before fear turned into blame.

The spreadsheet remained on the table after dinner. Daniel put it away, and Maya did not ask him to keep it open. For that evening, they were not a salary divided by a percentage, or a set of costs to be managed. They were two friends sharing a flat, tired but listening, with a meal to finish and a future they could face together.
