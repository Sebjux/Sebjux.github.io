0 The Invitation

Olivia Bennett read the email for the fourth time. The marketing team at her company in Bratislava had chosen her to attend a three-day conference in London. It would be her first business trip outside Slovakia.

“You’ll be fine,” her manager, James Carter, said, stopping beside her desk. “You know our new campaign better than anyone. I’d like you to present it on the second day.”

Olivia looked up from her screen. “Present it? In front of everyone?”

“Just a short presentation, then a discussion. You have the slides nearly finished, don’t you?”

She did. The words were ready on her computer. Speaking them aloud was another matter. Olivia knew English grammar well and could read reports easily, but her confidence often disappeared when people spoke quickly.

James pulled over a chair. “Let’s get the ball rolling with travel plans. Sophie Mitchell is going too, and she can help you find your way around the conference.”

Olivia nodded. She pictured a ball rolling across the office floor. “I can bring it,” she said.

James paused. Then he smiled. “No ball needed. It just means we should start.”

“Oh. I thought perhaps it was a game at the conference.”

“That would be a very unusual marketing conference,” he said. “I’ll send you the schedule. And keep me in the loop if anything changes.”

Olivia wrote the words in her notebook. Keep me in the loop. She imagined James sitting inside a large loop of rope. She did not ask what it meant. She already felt she had asked enough questions.

That evening, Olivia opened the travel folder James had sent. Her flight left early on Monday morning. The conference began the next day and ended on Thursday afternoon. She would stay in London for three nights.

She checked her passport, hotel confirmation, conference ticket and presentation notes. Then she checked them again. She put printed copies in a blue folder and saved digital copies on her phone.

Her friend Sophie Mitchell sent a message. Sophie worked in the company’s Prague office and spoke English comfortably, though it was not her first language either.

“I’m on the same flight,” Sophie wrote. “We can meet near the check-in desks. Don’t worry if London feels confusing at first. It confuses me sometimes too.”

Olivia smiled and replied, “Thank you. I will prepare everything.”

She began rehearsing her presentation. She stood in her small living room and spoke to an empty chair. She tried once, then again, then a third time. At half past ten, she was still practising the first slide.

Her brother came to the door. “Are you talking to the chair?”

“It is the conference audience,” Olivia said.

“They look quiet.”

“They are a very good audience.”

The next morning, Olivia found a small paper card beside her coffee. James had written a note for her: Bring the campaign figures. Leave time for questions. Breathe.

At work, she stopped by his desk. “James, when you said ‘keep me in the loop,’ did you mean I should tell you about changes?”

“Exactly. Just send me a message if your plans change or if something important comes up.”

“I understand now. I will keep you updated.”

“Perfect,” he said. “And remember, you don’t need to understand every phrase at once. Ask when you need to.”

Olivia put the blue folder into her bag. She still felt nervous, but the trip had become real. In three days, she would be speaking to people from different countries, in a city she had never visited. She took a slow breath and checked that her passport was there.

1 A Change at the Gate

On Monday morning, Olivia reached the airport before sunrise. She met Sophie beside the check-in desks. Sophie had a small suitcase and a bright red scarf.

“You’re early,” Sophie said.

“I wanted extra time in case I made a mistake.”

“That’s sensible,” Sophie replied. “I once went to the wrong terminal. Extra time is useful.”

They checked in and found the security area. Olivia followed each sign carefully. At the gate, she sat with her blue folder on her knees and read the flight details.

A message appeared on the screen. The gate had changed. Olivia looked at the new number, but an announcement began at the same time.

The speaker’s voice was fast and unclear. Olivia caught the words “London,” “gate” and “boarding.” She thought she heard a gate number, but the sound of the announcement made it difficult to be sure.

“Did you hear the new gate?” she asked Sophie.

“I caught the first part,” Sophie said. “Let’s check the screen again.”

They looked up. The screen now showed a different number from the one Olivia had written down. People began walking towards a corridor.

Olivia picked up her bag and followed them. Sophie was behind her, answering a message, and did not see which way Olivia went.

After several minutes, Olivia reached a set of doors marked for another part of the airport. She stopped. A flight information worker stood beside a desk.

“Excuse me,” Olivia said. “Could you repeat the gate number for the London flight, please? I’m sorry, I didn’t catch it.”

“Of course,” the worker replied, speaking more slowly. “It’s gate twenty-four. The screen over there has the latest information.”

“Gate twenty-four. Thank you. Is that in this direction?”

The worker pointed back along the corridor. “Go straight on, then turn left after the café. You’ll see the signs.”

Olivia thanked her and hurried back. She found Sophie near the café, looking worried.

“There you are! I thought you’d gone ahead.”

“I followed the people, but they were going to a different gate. I asked the staff. It is twenty-four.”

“Good idea,” Sophie said. “Let’s go together.”

They reached the gate with time to spare. Olivia felt embarrassed, but she was also pleased that she had asked for the information herself.

On the plane, Sophie sat in another row. The man beside Olivia introduced himself as Daniel Brooks. He was travelling to London for a meeting with a partner company.

“Is this your first time in London?” he asked.

“Yes. It is my first business trip outside my country.”

“How exciting.” Daniel spoke clearly, but the plane engine made some words hard to hear. Olivia caught “meeting” and “partner,” but not the question after that.

She hesitated. Then she said, “Sorry, could you say that again, please? The noise is making it difficult to hear.”

“Of course. I asked whether you’re going to a conference.”

“Yes, a marketing conference. I’m giving a short presentation.”

“I’m sure it will go well. I have to explain a new service at my meeting. I’ve been practising my opening line all week.”

“Me too,” Olivia said. “I have practised so much that my chair knows the whole presentation.”

Daniel laughed. “At least your chair will be a friendly audience.” Olivia laughed too. The conversation was not perfect, but it was easier than she had expected. When she did not understand, she asked. Daniel repeated his words without making a fuss.

2 A Room with a View

At London Heathrow, Olivia and Sophie collected their bags and followed the signs to the train. Sophie had travelled there before, but she checked the route on her phone.

“We need the train to Paddington,” she said. “Then we can take a taxi to the hotel.”

At the ticket machine, Olivia looked at the choices. The screen offered several kinds of tickets. She was not sure which one was right.

A station worker came over. “Can I help?”

“Yes, please. We are going to Paddington. Which ticket should we buy?”

He showed them the correct choice and explained how to pay. Olivia listened closely, then checked the screen. “Two adult tickets to Paddington?” she asked.

“That’s right,” he said.

On the train, London appeared beyond the window: roads, buildings and rows of houses. Olivia wanted to look at everything, but she also kept checking the hotel address.

At the hotel desk, a receptionist asked for her booking name. Olivia gave it and showed her passport.

“You’re in room 508,” the receptionist said. “Breakfast is served from seven until half past nine. The conference coach leaves at eight tomorrow.”

Olivia repeated the time. “The coach leaves at eight?”

“That’s right. Please be in the lobby by a quarter to eight.”

Sophie leaned closer. “That means 7:45.”

“Thank you,” Olivia said. She wrote the time in her notebook. The room was small but comfortable. From the window, she could see part of the street below and a line of buses.

After they unpacked, Sophie suggested they walk to a nearby café. They ordered tea and sandwiches. Olivia was tired from the early start, but the warm drink helped.

A man at the next table noticed their conference bags. “Are you here for the marketing event?” he asked.

“Yes,” Sophie replied. “We’re with a company from Central Europe.”

“I’m Daniel,” he said. “We met on the plane, Olivia.”

“I remember,” Olivia said, pleased to see a familiar face. “Did your meeting go well?”

“It starts tomorrow. I’m staying near the station.” He asked what the conference was about, and Olivia explained the campaign in simple terms.

Daniel listened and asked a question about the company’s customers. Olivia understood most of it, but one phrase was unfamiliar.

“Could you explain what you mean by ‘reach’ in this question?” she asked.

“I mean how many people see the campaign,” he said. “Sorry, I should have been clearer.”

Olivia explained that their campaign used short videos and local events. She spoke slowly at first. Then, when Daniel asked about the video, she relaxed and described how the team had made it.

“That sounds interesting,” Daniel said. “You know your subject well.”

“I know the slides well,” Olivia replied. “I am still learning how to talk about them without reading every line.”

“That takes practice,” Sophie said. “But you don’t need to sound like a newsreader. Just explain what you know.”

The next morning, the three of them met near the conference coach. Daniel was going in a different direction, so they wished him luck.

Olivia checked the clock in the lobby. It was 7:42. “We have time,” she said.

“Yes,” Sophie replied. “And this time we know exactly where we’re going.”

As the coach carried them through the morning traffic, Olivia looked at her notes. She was nervous again, but she had asked strangers for help, checked a ticket and held a conversation about her work. The day had not even begun.

3 Touch Base

The conference hotel was busy. People stood near signs, checked their badges and talked over cups of coffee. Olivia collected her badge and found a table with Sophie.

The first session began at nine. Speakers discussed how companies could understand customers in different countries. Olivia wrote down new ideas and marked two questions to ask later.

At the break, she met Emma, a marketing manager from another company. Emma had a warm smile and spoke with a gentle British accent.

“How are you finding the conference?” Emma asked.

“Very interesting,” Olivia said. “Some speakers speak quickly, but I can read the slides.”

“They do get excited when they talk about their work,” Emma said. “Would you like to join us for lunch?”

“Yes, thank you. That would be lovely.”

At lunch, Emma introduced Olivia to two other visitors. They talked about travel, food and the weather. One visitor asked whether Olivia had tried fish and chips.

“Not yet,” Olivia said. “I have only tried tea and a sandwich.”

“Well, you’re off to a very British start,” Emma said with a smile.

When the next session began, James sent Olivia a message. “How’s it going? Touch base with me after your presentation.”

Olivia read it twice. She knew he wanted to speak to her, but she was unsure what “touch base” meant. It sounded like a sport. She did not want to misunderstand again, so she wrote, “I’ll call you after the presentation to tell you how it went.”

James replied, “Exactly. You’ve got it.”

Olivia felt a little proud. She had asked about his earlier phrase, and now she had worked this one out from the message. She went back to the room where the speakers were meeting.

That afternoon, Olivia joined a small group discussion about marketing in different countries. Emma sat beside her. The group leader asked each person to describe one challenge they had faced.

Olivia planned to say that local jokes did not always work in translation. When her turn came, she began carefully. “We once used a short phrase that sounded funny in our office. In another country, people understood it differently.”

“What did you do?” Emma asked.

“We asked local colleagues to check the words before we published them. They found a better version.”

The group leader nodded. “That’s an important point. A translation can be correct but still feel wrong.”

Olivia wanted to add that it was not only words. Pictures and colours could also have different meanings. She paused to find the sentence.

“Take your time,” Emma said quietly.

Olivia explained her idea. She used simple words and gave an example from a recent campaign. The group asked two questions. One was difficult to hear, so she politely asked the speaker to repeat it.

When the discussion ended, Emma said, “That was clear and useful.”

“Thank you. I was worried I would forget everything.”

“You didn’t,” Emma said. “And you didn’t need to use complicated language.”

Back at the hotel, Olivia called James. She told him about the sessions and the useful discussion. She also explained that she had asked a speaker to repeat a question.

“That’s good,” James said. “How are you feeling about tomorrow?”

“Nervous. I have practised a lot, but perhaps too much. I can say the presentation by heart, but then I worry if one word is missing.”

“Use your notes as a guide, not a script,” James said. “The audience wants to understand the campaign, not hear a perfect performance.”

Olivia wrote that down. Then she closed the presentation file. For the first time in days, she decided not to practise again that night.

4 The Presentation

On the second morning, Olivia arrived at the conference room early. She checked that her slides opened correctly and placed her notes beside the computer. Sophie sat in the front row.

“You look ready,” Sophie said.

“I feel ready for the first two minutes,” Olivia replied. “After that, we will see.”

Emma came in carrying a cup of coffee. “The programme says your talk starts at ten. Is that still the time?”

Olivia checked the printed schedule. “Yes. Ten o’clock. Thank you for checking.”

People took their seats. The chair of the session introduced Olivia and her campaign. She walked to the front and looked at the room. Her hands felt cold.

For a moment, she forgot the first line. Then she looked at Sophie, who gave her a small encouraging nod.

“Good morning,” Olivia began. “I’m Olivia Bennett, from Bratislava. Today I’ll talk about a campaign that helped us introduce a new product to families.”

She explained the main idea, then showed a short video. The audience watched quietly. When the video ended, Olivia described how the team had worked with local shops and asked customers for feedback.

She noticed that she was no longer reading every sentence. She looked up and spoke to the people in the room.

At the end, the chair invited questions. A man near the back asked something about the campaign’s results. Olivia heard the beginning but missed the final part.

“I’m sorry, could you repeat the last part of the question, please?” she asked.

He repeated it more slowly. Olivia understood and answered with the figures from her notes.

Another person asked whether the team would use the same plan in every country.

“Not exactly,” Olivia said. “We would keep the main idea, but we would check the words and examples with local colleagues.”

There was a short discussion. Olivia answered two more questions. When the chair thanked her, the audience applauded. She sat down, surprised by how quickly the presentation had passed.

During the break, James called. “I watched the online stream. You were clear, and the questions were good.”

“Thank you. I forgot one sentence, but I explained the same idea another way.”

“That’s what a good speaker does,” James said. “Thanks for keeping me in the loop.”

This time Olivia understood the phrase immediately. “I’ll send you the final notes this afternoon,” she said.

Emma joined them by the coffee table. “Your example about local shops was especially helpful,” she said. “Would you mind sending me the name of the report you mentioned?”

“Of course. I’ll email it when we get back to the office.”

Sophie handed Olivia a cup. “Tea. I asked for milk on the side, just in case.”

Olivia took a sip and pulled a face. “I think this is coffee.”

Sophie looked at the cup. “Oh, you’re right. I must have picked up the wrong one.”

They both laughed. “At least it isn’t another gate change,” Olivia said.

That afternoon, the conference included a meeting about future partnerships. Olivia shared her team’s idea and asked how the other companies preferred to plan joint work. She listened carefully, made notes and checked one date before agreeing to it.

When the meeting ended, Emma offered to show them a small café near the conference hotel. On the way, a visitor asked Olivia for directions to the main hall.

“Go straight ahead, then turn right at the desk,” Olivia said. “The sign is just above the doors.”

The visitor thanked her and walked away. Olivia looked at Sophie. “I think I gave the right directions.”

“You did,” Sophie said. “And you sounded very sure.”

On Wednesday, the final conference day, Olivia took part in a workshop and met people from several companies. She did not understand every word, but she asked questions, confirmed details and shared her ideas.

That evening, she called James again. She told him what she had learned and promised to send a short report after returning home.

“You’ve done excellent work,” he said. “How was your first business trip?”

Olivia looked out at the London street, where buses moved through the rain. “Busy, confusing and very useful,” she said. “I’m ready for the next one.”

5 The Wrong Way Out

Olivia Bennett stepped off the plane at Heathrow with a stiff neck, a heavy suitcase, and a folder full of conference notes. It was her first business trip to London, and the city seemed both exciting and much larger than it had looked on her laptop. She checked her phone. James had sent a message: “Take the Tube to King’s Cross, then get the train to the hotel. Ask at the desk if you’re not sure.”

At the station, Olivia stood beneath a board of coloured lines. She found the Piccadilly line and followed a sign that said TICKETS. A man in a blue uniform was helping a family. When he looked free, Olivia asked, “Excuse me, is this the right line for King’s Cross?”

“That’s right,” he said. “You’ll want to change at Green Park.”

Olivia heard the words but not the meaning. “Change the line there?”

“Yes, love. Victoria line northbound. You can’t miss it.”

She nodded, though she was not sure what northbound meant. She thanked him and joined the queue at the ticket machine. A woman behind her noticed Olivia studying the map.

“First time in London?” she asked.

“Is it that clear?” Olivia said.

The woman smiled. “Only because you’ve been looking at the map as if it might move.” She showed Olivia how to tap her bank card at the gate. “You don’t need to buy a paper ticket.”

Olivia followed the signs to the platform. At Green Park, she got off and found the Victoria line after asking a station worker to repeat the instruction. The worker spoke more slowly this time and pointed towards the stairs. Olivia reached King’s Cross without another problem, but there the signs seemed to point in every direction at once.

She found the exit marked TAXIS and walked outside. A driver in a black cab was waiting near the kerb. Olivia showed him the hotel address on her phone. He looked at it and said, “You’re after the other side of the station, really. Best to hop back in and go round.”

“Sorry, could you say that again?” Olivia asked.

The driver repeated himself, pointing to a road that curved away from the station. He explained that the hotel was close, but traffic made walking around the station easier than driving through it.

Olivia decided to walk. She rolled her suitcase along the pavement, stopping twice to check the map. At one crossing, a cyclist rang his bell and she stepped back quickly. At another corner, she went the wrong way and had to turn around. By the time she reached the hotel, her arm was tired and her hair had escaped from its neat travel bun.

The receptionist greeted her with a bright smile. “Good afternoon. You must be Olivia Bennett. We’ve been expecting you. Your room’s all ready, and we can get you sorted in a jiffy.”

Olivia blinked. “Sorted in a… what?”

“A jiffy,” the receptionist said, seeing her confusion. “It means very soon. I’ll just need your passport and the card you used to book.”

The phrase had sounded like a warning at first, but the receptionist’s warm voice made its meaning clearer. Olivia handed over her passport and asked, “Could you please tell me what time breakfast starts?”

“From half six. That’s six thirty.”

“And does the conference shuttle leave from here?”

“It does, at eight fifteen. If you’d like, I can write that down for you.”

Olivia thanked her. Upstairs, she found a small room with a view of a brick wall and one stubborn green plant. She laughed, then sent James a message: “I’m here. I learned that ‘in a jiffy’ means soon, and I have not yet lost my suitcase.”

6 A Table for Six

Olivia changed her shirt and checked the conference schedule before going downstairs. James had sent a message saying the team would meet at a restaurant near the hotel at seven. She arrived five minutes early and found Sophie waiting outside, holding an umbrella under one arm.

“You made it!” Sophie said. “How was the journey?”

“Long, but fine. I got lost at King’s Cross for a while.”

“That’s practically a London welcome,” Sophie said. “James got lost there on his first visit too. He insists he was following a sign, but I think he was following a sandwich.”

A few minutes later, James arrived with Daniel and Emma. Olivia recognized Emma from emails about the conference. Emma Wilson was the event coordinator, and she carried a notebook with coloured tabs.

“I’m glad you’re here in person,” Emma said. “Email makes it hard to tell who’s making the jokes.”

“I’m glad to meet you too,” Olivia replied. “I’ve already had a helpful lesson in London transport.”

At the restaurant, a waiter showed them to a long table near the window. He asked whether they wanted still or sparkling water. Olivia heard “still” and wondered if he meant the water was not ready. Sophie quietly explained, and Olivia ordered still water.

The conversation began with travel. Daniel had arrived that afternoon from Manchester. Emma had been in London all week, arranging signs and checking rooms. James asked Olivia about her flight and her hotel.

“The room is comfortable,” she said. “The view is of a wall, but I’m sure it has a strong character.”

“A historic wall?” Daniel asked.

“Possibly. I haven’t asked it yet.”

They laughed, and Olivia felt her shoulders relax. Sophie asked about the work Olivia had been doing at the company. Olivia described a campaign for a small outdoor clothing brand. She explained how the team had used customer interviews to find out why people chose one jacket over another.

“So you listened first,” Emma said. “That’s useful. Tomorrow’s sessions are about the same thing, but with bigger budgets and more complicated charts.”

“I can handle charts,” Olivia said. Then she paused. “Most of the time.”

James told the group that the first conference day would be busy. He asked everyone to arrive before registration opened and to meet by the main entrance at eight.

“Eight o’clock sharp?” Olivia asked.

“Eight at the latest,” James replied. “We’ll walk over together.”

Olivia repeated the time back. “Main entrance, eight o’clock. I’ll be there.”

The waiter came to take their orders. When he asked if they were ready, Olivia was still deciding between two dishes. “Could I have another minute, please?” she asked. He nodded and moved away without any impatience.

Later, James asked if Olivia had tried the local cider. She had not, so Sophie recommended a small glass. Olivia took a careful sip. It was sharp and sweet at once.

“What do you think?” Daniel asked.

“I think it tastes like an apple has made a very serious decision,” Olivia said.

Sophie laughed so loudly that the waiter looked over, smiling. The dinner lasted longer than Olivia expected, but the talk never felt like a test. They discussed work, family, and the best way to stay awake during morning talks. Emma advised coffee, Daniel advised an open window, and James advised not staying up to read the whole conference programme.

Back at the hotel, Olivia placed her alarm for six thirty. She wrote the meeting time on a card and put it beside her phone. The day had been tiring, yet the unfamiliar phrases now seemed less like secret messages and more like small doors she could learn to open.

7 The Badge Mix-Up

The next morning, Olivia was ready before her alarm rang. She had breakfast, checked the time twice, and reached the hotel entrance at seven fifty-five. James, Sophie, Daniel, and Emma were already there.

“Good morning,” Emma said. “Everyone’s up and about.”

Olivia understood that this meant they were awake and busy. She smiled. “I’m definitely up. I’m not sure about the busy part yet.”

They walked to the conference centre together. At the registration desk, a volunteer asked Olivia for her name. After finding it on the list, he handed her a badge that read OLIVIA BENNETT — GUEST.

“I’m with the marketing team,” Olivia said. “I should be a speaker’s assistant, I think.”

The volunteer glanced at his screen. “You’re down for the afternoon workshop, and the morning session’s full.”

Olivia felt a quick rush of worry. She had been told that she would attend the morning session, and her name was on James’s team list. “Sorry, could you repeat that? Are you saying I can’t go into the morning session?”

“I’m afraid so,” he said. “Your badge is for the workshop only.”

Olivia asked him to check the spelling of her name and the company name. He did, then looked at the badge again. “It may be a booking error.”

James had been speaking to someone at the next desk. He came over when he saw Olivia waiting. “What’s happened?”

Olivia explained what the volunteer had said. James opened the registration email on his phone, but the list did not show the full details. Emma came over and asked to see Olivia’s confirmation.

“Let’s check the schedule together,” Emma said. She spoke slowly and clearly, but did not make Olivia feel that she had done anything wrong. “Your ticket includes all three days. The morning session is included too. The problem may be the type of badge they printed.”

Emma spoke to the desk supervisor. Olivia stayed beside James and listened. The supervisor said that the system had placed Olivia in a group for afternoon workshops, but that did not mean she was barred from the main talks. The word “only” had been added by mistake when the volunteer read the note.

“So I can attend the first session?” Olivia asked.

“Yes,” the supervisor replied. “Your badge is fine for the main hall. We’ll print a corrected one so the workshop team can find you later.”

“Thank you for checking,” Olivia said. “I wanted to be sure before I went in.”

Emma gave her a new badge and pointed to the hall entrance. “The first talk begins at nine. We’ll have time for coffee, but not a long queue.”

Olivia checked the clock. “It’s eight forty-five. Is the session at nine or quarter past nine?”

“Nine,” Emma said. “The doors open at quarter to.”

Olivia repeated the details to make sure. “Doors at eight forty-five, talk at nine.”

“Exactly,” said Emma. “You’ve got it.”

They found seats together near the middle of the hall. Olivia opened her notebook and wrote the title of the first talk: “Listening to Customers Across Markets.” She put a box around the time and underlined the word “customers.”

The speaker began with a quick welcome and moved straight into examples. His voice was clear, but he spoke quickly. He used phrases Olivia had heard before, such as “get the ball rolling” and “a bit of a learning curve.” She caught the general direction, but not every point.

At one moment the speaker said that the team had “gone back to the drawing board.” Olivia wrote the phrase down, then asked Sophie quietly, “Does that mean they started again?”

“Yes,” Sophie whispered. “Their first plan didn’t work, so they made a new one.”

Olivia nodded and added that explanation to her notes. When the speaker showed a chart, she focused on the main message: customers in different countries wanted the same basic result, but they used different words to describe it. She wrote, “Ask local customers what the problem means to them.”

She still missed several examples, but she had found the central idea. When the talk ended, she looked at her notes. They were not complete, yet they were useful. That felt like a good start.

8 The Main Message

During the short break, Olivia went to the coffee table with Sophie. The line moved slowly, and people talked over one another. Olivia heard someone say, “I’ll grab a flat white,” and wondered if there was a special cup she had not seen.

“It’s a coffee with milk,” Sophie explained. “You can ask for one if you want.”

“I think I’ll grab tea,” Olivia said. “I understand that one.”

They found a quiet place near a window. Olivia opened her notebook. “I followed the main point, but I lost some details when the speaker talked about the survey results.”

“That happened to me the first time I heard him,” Sophie said. “He speaks quickly, especially when he gets excited. You could ask him after the next session.”

Olivia was unsure about approaching a speaker, but she decided it was better than guessing. When the speaker came out of the hall, she waited until he had finished talking to another person.

“Excuse me,” Olivia said. “Could I ask you to repeat one point from your chart? I understood that customers wanted the same result, but I missed how many people took part in the survey.”

The speaker smiled. “Of course. We spoke to two hundred people in four countries. I should have made that number clearer.” He explained that the survey had included fifty people in each country.

Olivia thanked him and wrote the number down. The speaker asked what work she did, and she told him about the outdoor clothing campaign. He said that interviews could explain things a short survey might miss.

The next session was a small meeting about planning campaigns for different markets. Olivia, Sophie, and Daniel sat around a table with five other people. The session leader asked everyone to share one problem they had faced.

Olivia waited, then spoke. “In our last campaign, we translated the words, but customers still didn’t understand why the jacket was useful. We had to ask better questions.”

“That’s a good example,” the session leader said. “Sometimes a message is correct in a dictionary but wrong for the people who hear it.”

Olivia felt pleased that her idea had made sense. One of the other guests asked how the team had changed the campaign. Olivia explained that they had spoken to customers and used their descriptions in the new adverts. She kept her sentences short and gave an example.

The group discussed how to decide when to use local words and when to keep a shared company message. Olivia did not understand every comment, but she asked two people to repeat their points. Each time, the discussion continued without fuss.

At the end, Emma came by with a schedule card for the next day. “Just a heads-up,” she said. “The first talk starts at half nine tomorrow, not nine. Registration is already done, so you can come straight in.”

Olivia checked the card. “Half nine means nine thirty, correct?”

“That’s right,” Emma said. “We say it that way here.”

Olivia wrote “9:30” beside the words and asked, “And should we meet at the main entrance at nine?”

“Yes, nine o’clock. James will send a message tonight too.”

“Thanks. I’ll check both.”

That evening, Olivia joined the team for a quick meal at a small restaurant around the corner. She told James about the badge problem and how it had been fixed.

“Good thing you asked,” he said.

“I nearly accepted the first answer because the volunteer sounded certain,” Olivia admitted. “But the details didn’t match my email.”

“That’s exactly the right reason to check,” Emma said. “Polite questions save time for everyone.”

Sophie raised her glass of water. “To clear questions.”

“And useful answers,” Daniel added.

Olivia raised her glass too. She was still learning how London sounded, and she knew there would be more confusing phrases ahead. But she had found her way from the airport, corrected a booking mistake, and asked a speaker to repeat a number. Tomorrow would bring another full day of the conference. This time, she felt ready to listen, check, and join in.

9 A Friendly Welcome

After the morning talk, Olivia stood near the conference coffee table with a cup in both hands. She had just finished speaking about her company’s new campaign. The room had been quiet while she talked, and several people had asked questions. No one had laughed at her grammar. Still, she could remember every small mistake she thought she had made.

Emma came over with a woman wearing a green jacket. “Olivia, this is Priya Shah. She works with a travel company in Manchester.”

“Hello, Priya. It’s lovely to meet you,” Olivia said. She worried that lovely sounded too personal, but Priya smiled.

“And you. I enjoyed your talk,” Priya said. “The example about the family choosing a local shop was very clear.”

“Thank you. I’m glad it made sense.” Olivia took a breath. “What kind of customers do you work with?”

They spoke about travel advertising and the difficulty of reaching people who did not use social media every day. Olivia did not know one word Priya used, so she asked what it meant. Priya explained it without making Olivia feel foolish. Soon they were laughing about a campaign that had used a picture of a suitcase with a broken wheel.

More people joined them. Olivia introduced herself several times, trying to remember names and companies. She was afraid of making mistakes, but she found that a short introduction was enough: her name, her company, and one clear sentence about what she did.

A man called Peter asked, “So, are you doing anything nice while you’re here?”

Olivia almost answered with a long story about the conference. Then she remembered that it was a simple question. “I’d like to see a museum, if I have time. I’ve never been to London before.”

“You must go to the National Gallery,” he said. “It’s right by Trafalgar Square.”

“I’ll put it on my list,” Olivia replied. “Thank you.”

When Peter left, Emma said, “You’re doing well. You don’t need to use difficult words. People want to know what you mean, not how many words you know.”

Olivia smiled. “I keep thinking about my grammar.”

“I know,” Emma said. “But your message was clear. That matters most.”

Across the room, Sophie was speaking to a man from a London design firm. She looked serious, and the man was speaking quickly. Olivia wondered if Sophie was annoyed with him. When the conversation ended, Sophie turned to Olivia.

“I’m sorry I was a bit short,” Sophie said. “We’re running late, and I need to get the room ready for the next session.”

Olivia had thought Sophie’s brief answer earlier meant she did not want to talk. “I thought you were upset with me,” Olivia admitted.

“Oh, no,” Sophie said. “I was trying to finish three jobs at once. In this office, ‘I’ll get to it in a minute’ can mean ‘I haven’t had a chance yet,’ not ‘I don’t care.’”

“Thank you for explaining,” Olivia said. “I wasn’t sure how to understand it.”

Sophie nodded. “If you’re not sure, just ask. We don’t all mean the same thing, even when we speak the same language.”

A bell sounded from the speakers. A voice announced that the afternoon session would begin in five minutes in Room Two. Olivia listened closely, then checked the sign on the wall.

“Room Two,” she said. “Five minutes. I’ve got it.”

“You have,” Sophie said, and gave her a quick thumbs-up. Olivia felt more relaxed as they walked into the hall together.

10 Two Thirty

At lunch, Emma showed Olivia the schedule for the rest of the day. “There’s a small meeting about the campaign at half past two,” she said. “You, me, Sophie, and Daniel. We’ll meet by the East Room.”

Olivia looked at the printed programme. The conference map showed two rooms with similar names: East Room and East Meeting Room. “Half past two in the East Room?” she asked.

“That’s right,” Emma said. She was already checking a message on her phone. “See you there.”

Olivia read the schedule again. A note beside the campaign session said, “2.30—Room 4.” She thought perhaps this was another arrangement, or perhaps the small meeting was in Room 4. The programme had been changed that morning, and one of the pages was folded over.

She decided to check with Sophie, but Sophie was helping a speaker with a microphone. Olivia did not want to interrupt. She went to Room 4, thinking she could wait there until someone arrived.

At two thirty, Room 4 was empty. Olivia sat near the door and opened her notes. Five minutes passed. Then ten. She sent Emma a message: I’m in Room 4. Are you coming here?

Emma called at once. “Olivia, where are you? We’re in the East Room.”

“I thought the meeting was in Room 4,” Olivia said. “I’m sorry.”

“We’re just starting,” Emma replied. “Could you come over now? It’s beside the main hall.”

Olivia hurried along the corridor. A group was coming the other way, so she stepped aside. “Excuse me. Is the East Room this way?” she asked a staff member.

“Yes, just past the blue doors,” he said. “You can’t miss it.”

Olivia found the blue doors, but there were two rooms beyond them. She stopped at the first one. A woman inside was giving a talk about online shopping. Olivia turned around, cheeks warm, and found a sign pointing to the second door.

When she entered, Emma, Sophie, and Daniel were waiting beside a table covered with papers. Olivia closed the door quietly.

“I’m very sorry,” she said. “I read the programme and went to Room 4. I should have checked with you.”

Emma looked at the clock. “We’ve lost ten minutes, but we can still cover the important points.” She did not sound angry. She moved a chair out for Olivia.

Olivia sat down. “I want to take responsibility. I misunderstood the room, and I should have asked instead of guessing.”

“Thanks for saying that,” Emma replied. “The programme isn’t very clear, either. I should have put the room in our message.”

Sophie opened her notebook. “Let’s make a list of what we need to decide. We can be quick.”

They discussed the campaign launch, the customer survey, and a possible change to the poster. Olivia wrote down each decision and repeated the most important point to make sure she had understood.

“So the new poster goes to the printer tomorrow morning,” she said. “And I’ll send the final wording tonight.”

“Exactly,” said Daniel. “That’s the main thing.”

The meeting ended on time. Olivia had been late, but she had not hidden the mistake. When they left the room, she felt embarrassed still, but also relieved. Asking questions, she decided, was better than spending ten minutes in the wrong place.

11 The Joke Daniel Missed

After the meeting, Daniel walked with Olivia towards the coffee table. “That was a good recovery,” he said. “I once went to the wrong hotel for a client meeting. I only noticed when I saw a wedding cake in the lobby.”

Olivia laughed. “What did you do?”

“I called the client and said I was at the wrong party.” Daniel grinned. “They were kind about it.”

A man at the coffee table turned around. “You’re not going to the wrong party tonight, are you?” he asked.

His accent was unfamiliar, and he spoke quickly. Olivia understood the word party but not the full question. She smiled, unsure.

Daniel answered, “No, we’ll try to keep her on the right side of the door.” The man laughed. Olivia did not know why.

When he had gone, Daniel said, “Sorry. It was a joke about the wrong hotel.”

“I didn’t catch it,” Olivia said. “Could you say the question again, please?”

Daniel repeated it more slowly. Olivia understood this time, and they both laughed. “Thank you,” she said. “I thought you were talking about a real party.”

They found a quiet place near a window. Below them, buses moved along the street, and people hurried beneath umbrellas. The sky was grey, but Olivia liked the view.

“Do you ever miss things people say?” she asked Daniel. “You speak English all day.”

“All the time,” he said. “I missed a joke this morning because I thought someone said ‘a new plan’ when they said ‘a new plant.’ I spent a whole minute wondering why we needed a plant for the campaign.”

Olivia laughed. “That makes me feel better.”

“Good. Accents can be difficult, and people use short expressions that aren’t in books. I sometimes ask them to repeat something.”

“I worry that people will think I’m not clever.”

“They may think you want to understand them,” Daniel said. “That’s not a bad thing.”

A staff member made an announcement about the next talk. Olivia heard “market” and “Room Three,” but the rest was unclear. She checked the screen by the door. It showed the same details.

“Room Three, in five minutes,” she said.

“You heard enough,” Daniel replied.

As they walked to the talk, Olivia asked Daniel about his family. He told her that his sister lived in Bristol and worked as a nurse. Olivia spoke about her parents and how they had helped her prepare for the trip.

“Did they give you advice?” Daniel asked.

“My mother said, ‘Don’t be afraid to ask for help.’ My father told me to keep my travel papers in one place.”

“Good advice,” Daniel said. “My dad once put his train ticket in the fridge. He was making a sandwich at the same time.”

“Did he find it?”

“Yes. The cheese was cold, but the ticket was fine.”

Olivia laughed. She was beginning to enjoy the small conversations that had seemed so difficult on her first day.

At the door, Daniel held it open for her. “After you.”

“Thank you,” Olivia said, walking inside. “And if I miss another joke, I’ll ask you to explain it.”

“Please do,” he said. “Then I can enjoy it twice.”

12 A Clear Plan

That evening, Olivia returned to her hotel room and spread her notes across the desk. The next morning she was meeting Mr Anderson, a possible partner for her company. Emma had told her that he was interested in helping small businesses reach new customers.

Olivia wrote three points on a clean page: who her company was, what the campaign could do, and what she needed to ask him. She kept the sentences short. When she tried to make them sound more formal, she became confused and lost the main idea.

She practised aloud. “Our company helps local shops connect with customers. This campaign tells people about offers near them. We would like to learn what your customers need.”

The words were simple, but they were true. She wrote down questions too: How do you choose a new partner? What results are most important to you? What would be a useful first step?

Then she checked the meeting time in Emma’s message. “Nine thirty. Meeting Room Six,” she said aloud. She opened the conference map and found the room. This time, she planned to ask if she was not sure.

The next morning, Olivia arrived early. She checked the room number twice and waited near the door. Mr Anderson came at nine twenty-five. He was a tall man with silver hair and a dark blue tie.

“Ms Bennett?” he asked.

“Yes. Good morning, Mr Anderson. Thank you for meeting with me.”

“Of course. Please call me Richard.” He shook her hand. “Would you like a coffee?”

“Yes, please. A small one, if that’s possible.”

He brought two cups from a table at the back. “It’s a bit strong,” he said. “It’s conference coffee.”

Olivia took a sip. “I think it will help me stay awake.”

Richard laughed. They spoke for a few minutes about the weather and the busy roads near the hotel. Then he asked about Olivia’s company. She followed her notes, but she did not read every line. She explained the campaign in her own words and gave an example of a shop that wanted more people to visit its website.

Richard listened without interrupting. When she finished, he asked how the company would measure success.

Olivia paused. She had prepared for that question, but she wanted to make sure she understood it. “Do you mean how we will know whether the campaign is working?” she asked.

“Exactly,” Richard said.

She explained that they could count how many people opened the shop’s page and how many used a special offer. She also said they should speak with the shop owner before choosing the final measures.

“That sounds sensible,” Richard said. “We need to be careful not to promise more than we can deliver.”

“I agree,” Olivia replied. “What results matter most to your team?”

Richard told her that his company wanted clear reports and a simple way to test the idea with a small group. Olivia wrote this down. When he used a phrase she did not know, she asked him to explain it. He did so calmly and gave an example.

At the end, Richard said, “We could arrange a short online meeting next week. Would Tuesday morning work for you?”

Olivia checked her calendar. “Tuesday at ten is good. Could you send me the invitation with the time and the link?”

“Certainly,” he said. “And I’ll send you a short note about the test group.”

They stood and shook hands. Olivia left the room with a clear next step, not a promise of a deal. That felt better. She had asked what she needed to know, and Richard had listened.

13 No Need to Guess

Olivia found Emma and Sophie outside the meeting rooms. Emma saw the notes in Olivia’s hand. “How did it go?” she asked.

“Well, I think. Mr Anderson wants to test the idea with a small group. We have an online meeting next Tuesday.”

“Brilliant,” said Sophie. “Did he give you a time?”

“Ten in the morning. I asked him to send the invitation and the link.”

Emma nodded. “Good. Clear and practical.”

Olivia told them what Mr Anderson wanted to measure. Sophie suggested adding a question about how easy the offer was to use. Emma wrote it down, and they agreed to discuss it with the team after the conference.

“I was nervous about my grammar,” Olivia said. “But once we started talking, I stopped trying to make every sentence perfect.”

“That’s a good way to do it,” Emma said. “If your meaning is clear, the conversation can move forward.”

A message came over the conference speakers. The final session would begin at eleven in the Main Hall. Olivia heard the time, but the room name sounded like “small hall.” She looked at Emma.

“Did the announcement say Main Hall?” she asked.

“Yes,” Emma said. “Near the front desk.”

“Thanks. I heard something different.”

On their way, Sophie stopped to talk with a visitor. Olivia waited, then heard Sophie say, “I’ll give you a ring tomorrow.” The visitor looked at the phone in her hand.

After they moved on, Olivia asked, “Does that mean you will call her?”

“Yes,” Sophie said. “It’s just a common way to say it.”

“I thought perhaps you were going to give her an actual ring.” Olivia smiled.

Sophie laughed. “That would be a more expensive way to keep in touch.”

The final session was about building useful business relationships. Olivia listened, taking notes when an idea could help her company. She also noticed how people spoke to one another. Some were direct, and some used softer words. A request that began with “Would you mind” was still a request, not a question about someone’s feelings.

After the session, the conference host thanked everyone and announced that the next day’s programme would start at nine. Olivia checked the printed page and saved the updated schedule on her phone.

Emma walked with her towards the hotel entrance. “You’ve had a busy first trip,” she said.

“I have. I went to the wrong room, missed a joke, and nearly misunderstood an announcement.”

“And you fixed each problem,” Emma said.

“Not alone,” Olivia replied. “People helped when I asked.”

Outside, the evening air was cool. Olivia could see the lights of buses and shops along the street. She was still tired, and London still felt large, but she no longer felt that she had to understand every word the first time.

“Tomorrow is the last conference day,” Emma said. “We’ll meet at eight forty-five for breakfast.”

Olivia repeated the time. “Eight forty-five, at breakfast. I’ll be there.”

“Lovely,” Emma said. “See you then.”

Olivia walked back to her room with her notes tucked safely in her bag. She had come to London to present an idea. She was leaving the day with something else too: the confidence to ask, listen, and try again.

14 A Question from Mr Anderson

Olivia checked the room number on her conference schedule for the third time. Room 4B was on the fourth floor, not the fourth room on the second floor, as she had first thought. She arrived with two minutes to spare, carrying her notebook and a paper cup of tea.

Mr Anderson was already there. He stood beside a screen and spoke quietly with Emma, the conference organiser. When he saw Olivia, he smiled and offered his hand.

“Olivia Bennett. Good to meet you at last,” he said.

“Thank you for making time for me,” Olivia replied. She had planned that sentence on the way upstairs. “I’m pleased to meet you too.”

Emma showed Olivia to a chair. “We’ve got about twenty minutes before the next session, so this is a good chance to talk through your idea.”

Olivia opened her notebook. She had practised her explanation the night before, but Mr Anderson’s calm attention made her feel as if every word had become important.

“Our company sells outdoor equipment,” she began. “We want more young people to see our products as useful for everyday life, not only for camping trips.” She explained her idea: invite a small group of customers to share short stories and photographs of how they used the equipment in their daily routines. The company could then post the best stories online, with permission.

Mr Anderson nodded. “So you’d be asking customers to create the advertising for you?”

Olivia hesitated. “Not exactly. I mean, they would share their own experiences. We would choose a few examples, but we shouldn’t tell them what to say.”

“And how would you find the people?” he asked.

“We could start with customers who already follow us online. Perhaps we send a simple invitation to a few hundred people and offer a small prize for taking part.”

Mr Anderson leaned back. “What if only ten people reply? And what if the stories are all about hiking, which is the audience you’re trying to move beyond?”

Olivia felt her face grow warm. “Those are good questions. I don’t have the numbers yet. We could test it with a smaller group first, then change the invitation if we only get hiking stories.”

She wanted to add more, but the next sentence had disappeared from her mind.

“When you say ‘a smaller group,’ how many people do you mean?” Mr Anderson asked.

Olivia looked down at her notes. “I’m sorry, could you repeat the question, please? I want to make sure I understand.”

He asked again, more slowly. Olivia heard the key point this time. “You’re asking for a number. I’d begin with about fifty customers. It would be large enough to give us different answers, but small enough to manage.”

“That’s clearer,” he said. “And what would success look like after the first month?”

“We could check how many people take part, how many others look at the stories, and whether they visit our product pages. I wouldn’t say that a lot of views means more sales straight away. We’d need to compare the results over a longer time.”

Mr Anderson smiled. “Good. I’m glad you’re not promising magic.”

Olivia laughed, relieved. “Marketing sometimes sounds like magic when we explain it too quickly.”

Emma glanced at the clock. “We need to finish in five minutes. Olivia, could you send Mr Anderson a one-page outline by Friday?”

“Yes, I can do that.”

Mr Anderson stood. “You’ve got a sensible starting point. Keep the first test simple, and make sure the customers know how their stories might be used.”

Olivia wrote that down. She had not answered every question perfectly, and she had needed one question repeated. Still, the idea had survived the meeting—and become more useful.

15 The Schedule Changes

At lunchtime, Olivia returned to the conference hall. A new notice had appeared beside the entrance: the afternoon workshop would begin at two forty-five, not two thirty. The room had also changed from 3A to 2C.

Olivia looked at her watch. It was one fifty-five. She had promised James she would meet him near the coffee stand at two fifteen, and Sophie was expecting her in the old workshop room at two thirty.

Her phone buzzed. It was Emma: “Urgent change: the panel starts at 3.15 in Room 2C. Please let your group know.”

Olivia read the message twice. “Starts at three fifteen” sounded clear, but she wanted to check whether the workshop was still before the panel.

She called Emma. The call went to voicemail. Olivia took a breath and went to find Sophie.

Sophie was speaking to two visitors beside the stairs. Olivia waited until there was a pause.

“Sorry to interrupt. Have you seen the new schedule?” Olivia asked.

“Not yet. What’s changed?”

“The workshop is at two forty-five in Room 2C, and the panel is at three fifteen. At least, that’s what I understand from the notice and Emma’s message.”

Sophie checked her phone. “I’ve got the same message. But the workshop was meant to finish at three.”

“That leaves no time to get upstairs—or is Room 2C on this floor?” Olivia asked.

“It’s on the second floor,” Sophie said. “We should check with Emma. I don’t want to tell everyone the wrong thing.”

They found Emma near the registration desk, speaking with Daniel. Olivia waited again, then said, “Could I check something with you? Does the workshop still finish at three, or has the panel time changed too?”

Emma looked at the message on her phone. “The panel is now at three thirty. I’m sorry—I sent the first time by mistake. The workshop is two forty-five to three fifteen, then the panel begins at three thirty in the same room.”

“So the correct panel time is three thirty,” Olivia repeated.

“That’s right. Thank you for checking.”

Daniel offered to update the notice, while Sophie sent a message to the workshop group. Olivia offered to tell the people waiting by the old room.

She walked quickly to Room 3A. Several people were already sitting there with their notebooks open.

“Hello. I’m Olivia Bennett. I’m afraid the room and times have changed,” she said. “The workshop begins at two forty-five in Room 2C, on the second floor. It finishes at three fifteen. The panel starts at three thirty in that same room.”

A man near the door looked confused. “Sorry, did you say the panel starts at three fifteen?”

“No, three thirty,” Olivia said. “The workshop finishes at three fifteen. I’m sorry, that is easy to mix up.” She pointed to the updated message on her phone. “Here, you can check it too.”

The man nodded. “Thanks. I’ll let my colleague know.”

Olivia stayed long enough to answer another question about the lift, then hurried upstairs. James was waiting near the coffee stand.

“You look as if you’ve been managing a small airport,” he said.

“Something like that. The times changed twice, so we checked the final version and told people in different places.”

“Good job. Did you get to eat?”

Olivia looked at the sandwich in her hand. “I did. It may be slightly flatter than before.”

James laughed. They walked towards Room 2C together. Olivia was still worried about keeping up with the conference, but she had stopped trying to solve the whole problem by herself. She had checked the details, shared the information, and made sure people understood. It was not a dramatic skill. It was simply useful.

16 A Short Presentation

At three thirty, the panel began. Mr Anderson sat at the front beside two other speakers. Olivia and Sophie sat near the aisle, with Daniel a few chairs behind them. The room was busy, and people were still settling down when Emma introduced the first speaker.

During the discussion, Mr Anderson described the problem of reaching people who did not already know a brand. Olivia listened carefully and wrote down two points. When the chair asked for questions, a woman near the window raised her hand.

“How do you keep customer stories honest if you offer a prize?” she asked.

Mr Anderson answered that clear rules and open explanations were important. Olivia added a note: explain the prize, ask permission, do not edit a story to change its meaning.

At the end, Emma came over. “We’re short of one speaker for the final five-minute section. Would you be comfortable giving a short version of your idea?”

Olivia’s stomach tightened. “I can try. Could you tell me exactly when I need to speak?”

“After Daniel’s question, before the final comments. I’ll introduce you.”

Olivia turned to Sophie. “Can you listen for the time? I don’t want to miss my turn.”

“Of course,” Sophie said. “You’ll be fine.”

“I’ll be understandable,” Olivia replied. “Fine is a bigger promise.”

Daniel asked a question about how companies could use customer feedback without making every message sound the same. Emma then introduced Olivia.

Olivia stood. “I’ll keep this short. I work with a team that sells outdoor equipment. We want to show how the products can be useful in ordinary life, not only on long trips.”

She explained the small customer test and the need to ask permission. She spoke slowly, but not so slowly that each sentence felt like a separate exercise. Once, she said “the people is” before correcting herself: “Sorry—the people are the most important part.” No one laughed. A woman in the front row smiled in encouragement.

“We could invite about fifty customers to share a short story or photograph. We would explain where we may use it, and they could say no. After one month, we would check how many take part and how many people look at the stories.”

A man near the back raised his hand. “Would you use a public website, or just your company’s own channels?”

Olivia heard “public website,” but she was unsure whether he meant the company website or a wider online platform. She paused.

“Could you clarify what you mean by a public website?” she asked. “Do you mean somewhere outside our own channels?”

“Yes, exactly,” he said. “Would you invite people to post anywhere, or ask them to send the stories directly to you?”

“For the first test, I would ask them to send the stories directly to us. That gives us a chance to check permissions and make sure people are comfortable. Later, we could consider a wider campaign.”

The man nodded. “That makes sense.”

Olivia finished by saying that a small test would not prove everything, but it could show what people wanted to share. When she sat down, she let out a quiet breath.

“You made the point clearly,” Sophie whispered.

“I made one grammar mistake,” Olivia whispered back.

“You corrected it, and everyone understood you.”

After the session, Mr Anderson came to speak with her. “I liked your answer about keeping the stories private at first. It shows you’re thinking about the people involved, not only the campaign.”

“Thank you. I’m still not sure how much information we should ask for in the first invitation.”

“Start with what you need to make a decision. You can ask more later, if there’s a good reason.”

Olivia wrote the advice down. Then, remembering the question about wider platforms, she checked what he meant by “later.” He explained that he meant after a first test had shown that customers wanted to take part.

As the room emptied, Daniel joined them. “You kept your answer short,” he said. “That was smart. I often add three extra points when one will do.”

“I nearly added four,” Olivia said.

They laughed. Olivia knew she still missed words when people spoke quickly, and she sometimes needed a moment to find the right phrase. But she had asked a question instead of pretending, and her answer had helped the discussion. That felt better than getting every sentence perfect.

17 The Journey Home

On the final morning of the conference, Olivia packed her notebook, charger, and the small paper programme she had carried all week. She checked out of her hotel and left her bag at reception while she went to the last session.

Afterwards, she returned for her bag and found a woman looking worried beside the front desk. The woman held a map and a train ticket.

“Excuse me,” she said. “Do you know which bus goes to King’s Cross? I’m not sure I understood the driver.”

Olivia recognised the look on her face. “I’m going there too. What did the driver say?”

“I think he said to take the number forty-nine, but my ticket says I need to be at the station by one.”

Olivia checked the time. It was twelve twenty. “Let’s check the bus information together. The desk may know if there are delays.”

The receptionist explained that a nearby road was closed and the bus would take longer than usual. The underground was faster. Olivia repeated the route to make sure she had it right, then showed the woman the map.

“So we take the underground to King’s Cross, changing at the next station?” the woman asked.

“Yes. One change, then we follow the signs to the main station. Let’s ask which platform before we go down.”

The woman smiled. “Thank you. I was afraid to ask again.”

“I know that feeling,” Olivia said. “It’s better to check than to guess.”

They walked together to the station entrance and checked the signs. The woman’s name was Marta. She was visiting London for the first time to see her sister, who lived in the north of England.

On the train, Marta asked Olivia about her trip. Olivia told her about the three-day marketing conference and the meeting with Mr Anderson.

“Was it difficult?” Marta asked.

“Sometimes. People spoke with different accents, and they used expressions I didn’t know. I thought ‘touch base’ was about a place at first.”

Marta laughed. “It sounds like a place.”

“I had to learn to ask what people meant. I also found that the context helped. If someone said ‘Are you all right?’ in a shop, they were usually just being friendly, not asking me to explain my whole life.”

“That is useful,” Marta said. “I was afraid to ask the bus driver to repeat himself.”

“I still get nervous. I just try to ask one clear question.”

At the change, they followed the signs together. Olivia pointed out the station information desk and helped Marta confirm that her train time had not changed. When they reached King’s Cross, a large board showed a platform number for Marta’s train.

“You saved me a lot of worry,” Marta said.

“You did the hard part,” Olivia replied. “You asked for help, and then we checked the details.”

They said goodbye near the ticket barriers. Olivia felt pleased that a conversation with a stranger had become easy. A week earlier, she might have avoided starting it.

On the train back home, Olivia looked through her notes. The pages were full of crossed-out words, questions, and small reminders: ask them to repeat; confirm the time; check what “they” refers to; give one example.

Her phone rang. James was calling from the office.

“How was the journey?” he asked.

“Fine. I helped someone find the right train. We checked everything rather than guessing.”

“That sounds familiar.”

Olivia told him about the short presentation and the questions from the audience. She also described the mistake with “people is.”

“Did anyone have trouble understanding you?” James asked.

“No. But I needed a moment, and I asked what one question meant. I didn’t answer every point, either.”

“That’s realistic,” James said. “You’re not going to understand every person every time. The useful thing is that you can make sure you’re talking about the same thing.”

Olivia watched the fields pass the window. “I think I’m more confident, but I’m not suddenly an expert.”

“You don’t need to be. Let’s turn the idea into a one-page plan when you’re back. We can test it with a small customer group and decide what to measure.”

“Yes. And we should explain clearly how we might use the stories.”

“Agreed. Send me a draft next week, and we’ll look at it together.”

When the call ended, Olivia rested her notebook on her lap. She had not understood every accent or found every word quickly. She had made mistakes, corrected some, and asked people to explain others. Confidence, she thought, was not a perfect performance. It was practice, attention, and the willingness to try again.

The train carried her towards home. On Monday, she would have new questions to ask. This time, she was ready to begin.
